### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Both sides argued competently and the debate was genuinely competitive, but CON built the cleaner path to victory across the full exchange. The decisive advantage came from CON maintaining a structurally meaningful distinction between friend-shoring (allied concentration preserving scale economies) and the proposition's literal mandate of domestic localization (every country building duplicative factories). PRO's strongest move—collapsing friend-shoring and domestic localization as mechanically identical—was answered effectively: subsidies to a single efficient allied producer are structurally different from subsidies that multiply redundant national facilities. More damaging, CON's sharpest rebuttal (B4.5) turned PRO's own IRA evidence against the proposition: PRO celebrated investment flowing to India, Southeast Asia, and Latin America, which is exactly the allied-concentration model CON defends, not domestic localization as the proposition specifies. PRO never recovered from this inversion. CON also had the better of the China solar lesson throughout—the cost revolution came from concentrated scale, and the proposition's mandate, applied globally, would fragment that concentration. PRO answered with "prices haven't spiked yet," but CON correctly noted that localization hasn't yet achieved meaningful scale, making the non-effect premature vindication. PRO's strategic risk argument (rupture risk = infinite price) was genuine and effective, but CON's response (multiple suppliers, not domestic factories, prevent catastrophic rupture) was adequate. PRO's closing retreated further from the literal proposition toward defending industrial policy broadly, which validated CON's framing rather than rebutting it. Both sides had clipping costs that were roughly symmetrical. CON wins on sharper rebuttal work, better crystallization in later rounds, and successfully defending the key distinction the debate turned on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.5 move was the most decisive exchange: PRO's own empirical evidence—that the IRA is generating manufacturing investment in India, Southeast Asia, and Latin America—is actually consistent with friend-shoring (concentrated efficient allied production) and inconsistent with domestic localization as the proposition demands. PRO celebrated investment dispersing to efficient developing-nation producers, which is exactly the model CON defends and the opposite of every country building its own supply chain. This inversion directly undermined PRO's empirical grounding without PRO having an answer in the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): B won by making the decisive distinction between industrial policy for diversification and the proposition’s domestic localization mandate. A’s strongest point was strategic vulnerability to Chinese chokepoints, but B persuasively answered that resilience can come from multiple allied/competitive suppliers, stockpiles, and targeted bottleneck investment without every country duplicating supply chains. B also won the climate-weighing clash: higher prices slow deployment, especially for poorer countries, while A never convincingly explained how fragmented domestic factories preserve the scale economies that made clean tech cheap. A’s attempt to label friend-shoring as “industrial policy with a different label” was partially effective, but B’s response that mechanism is not the same as national localization was cleaner and more tied to the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive exchange was turning A’s China example: China’s industrial policy lowered costs through concentrated scale, but the proposition would fragment that scale across national supply chains. That undercut A’s learning-curve argument and reinforced B’s price-and-speed framing.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully isolating the motion's specific burden—domestic localization—and demonstrating how it structurally fragments the scale economies required for affordable, rapid clean-tech deployment. While Side B (PRO) effectively framed the security risks of concentrated supply chains and cleverly argued that friend-shoring is just industrial policy by another name, Side A consistently turned PRO's own examples (China's scale, IRA-driven investment in Southeast Asia) to show that allied concentration preserves learning curves without the redundant costs of national duplication. Side A's pressure on Global South affordability created a lasting comparative advantage that PRO never fully neutralized. Both debaters handled format requirements well and delivered sharp, substantive rebuttals, but Side A's structural distinction and cleaner weighing of speed/scale versus sovereignty provided the more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 turn on PRO's IRA example [A4.5], where CON points out that PRO celebrating manufacturing investment in India and Southeast Asia actually proves the friend-shoring/concentration model rather than domestic localization, effectively collapsing PRO's attempt to conflate the two and reinforcing the scale vs. fragmentation clash.
