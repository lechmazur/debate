### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won for three interlocking reasons. First, PRO correctly and consistently locked in the operative comparison: for the disengaged worker, the realistic alternative to 2-3% auto-enrollment savings is zero, not an ideal 10% voluntary contribution. CON's entire case rested on measuring auto-enrollment against a savings adequacy standard it never claimed to fully satisfy, while refusing to grapple with the population that saves nothing under voluntary systems. Second, PRO's pressure Q2 exposed a genuine contradiction CON never resolved: if a low-income worker cannot afford a 2-3% default deduction, a future tax benefit does not make voluntary contributions affordable now. CON's rebuttal introduced "refundable deduction designs" as a late speculative mechanism—a new concept with no prior development that landed as an evasion rather than an answer. Third, CON's legitimate catch on auto-escalation (not in the written proposition) was honestly conceded by PRO, but PRO correctly noted it didn't change the core comparison: even a fixed 2-3% compounding over a career still beats zero, which is what the disengaged worker saves under voluntary-deduction systems. CON repeatedly demanded that auto-enrollment achieve adequacy, but the motion's standard is "more effectively"—a relative, aggregate test—not perfection. PRO's "floor not ceiling" framing and the "you can't incentivize your way to universal security if people aren't in the game" line were rhetorically effective and argumentatively grounded. CON had valid points on inertia and default inadequacy, but never built a convincing path showing how deductions outperform on aggregate, especially for the zero-saver population, which is where the policy debate most clearly turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure question Q2—"If they can't afford 2-3% when automatically enrolled, how does a future tax benefit make voluntary contributions affordable now?"—was the most decisive exchange. It exposed the core contradiction in CON's affordability argument and CON's answer (introducing refundable deduction designs late) was under-developed and evasive, leaving the contradiction live through the closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A established that the decisive metric is moving the large population of disengaged, non-saving workers into accumulation, and that auto-enrollment does this by harnessing inertia while tax deductions fail to overcome it. Side B never squarely answered why disengaged workers would suddenly participate under a voluntary deduction scheme, and undermined its own low-income advocacy by relying on "refundable deduction" mechanisms that stray from the proposition and by conceding deductions do nothing for workers who cannot afford to save. While Side B effectively raised concerns about low default rates, Side A's floor-over-ceiling framing and direct answers to pressure questions provided the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal that the relevant comparison for disengaged workers is auto-enrollment's 2-3% savings versus the zero savings produced by voluntary/deduction systems, combined with the observation that tax deductions are worthless to low-income households with minimal tax liability. This exchange exposed that Side B's model depended on workers overcoming the very behavioral barriers the proposition assumes they will not.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by establishing a cleaner comparative baseline and successfully defending the motion’s mechanism against CON’s adequacy critique. PRO consistently framed the choice as 2–3% default savings versus zero for disengaged workers, a weighing advantage CON never fully overturned. PRO also landed the decisive structural blow on tax deductions: they require voluntary action and meaningful tax liability, leaving the poorest and most disengaged untouched. CON’s focus on low default rates and potential debt risk was valid, but PRO’s opt-out safeguard and intelligent concession on auto-escalation neutralized the inertia-trap argument. CON’s late pivot to refundable deductions felt reactive, and their closing largely recapitulated earlier points without resolving the core participation gap. PRO’s rhetorical clarity and direct pressure handling made their path to victory cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 directly answering CON’s pressure on escalation and low-income debt. By conceding escalation was outside the motion but doubling down on 2–3% beating zero, and framing the opt-out as a sufficient hardship safeguard, PRO turned CON’s strongest attacks into proof of inertia’s power while exposing the structural limits of deductions for low-tax-liability households.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built a cleaner, more disciplined case and maintained it across all five rounds. The central comparison was always: what does each policy deliver to the 40% of households currently saving nothing? PRO answered that clearly — a 3% auto-enrollment default produces roughly $3,500/year in retirement income that those households would never see under increased standard tax deductions. CON never genuinely escaped this comparison. Instead, CON repeatedly pivoted to defending refundable tax credits, a policy not named in the motion, which PRO correctly identified as a structural evasion and hammered with B3.1. The fatal moment came in A4.2, where CON explicitly conceded: "for the 40% of households saving $0, increased tax deductions would likely generate little to no additional retirement income." That is a self-inflicted concession on the very population the motion is most about. CON's remaining arguments — low default rates, gig worker exclusion, psychological anchoring — had real merit, but PRO gave reasonable responses to each (auto-escalation, state portable plans, $0 baseline comparison). PRO's numbers were specific and plausible without being brittle, and PRO answered pressure questions with the required A1:/A2: structure and substantive directness. CON made the A3.2 pressure question confusingly self-defeating, effectively asking how standard tax deductions help gig workers — a point that undermines CON's own position. The closing confirmed the asymmetry: CON's best case was that auto-enrollment is imperfect and that deductions could theoretically be reformed, while PRO held the concrete claim that for the households most at risk, auto-enrollment moves the needle from zero to meaningful, while standard deductions move it from zero to nothing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B3.1 pressure question — asking why CON was defending refundable tax credits instead of the standard tax deductions actually named in the motion — was the most decisive exchange. It forced CON to answer in A4.1–A4.2, where CON both acknowledged the off-topic drift and conceded that increased standard deductions would "likely generate little to no additional retirement income" for the 40% saving nothing. PRO then crystallized this in B4.3 and the closing, converting CON's own words into the clearest reason to vote PRO.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON)): Side A won by maintaining a clear comparative frame: for the 40% of working households with zero retirement savings, automatic enrollment delivers roughly $3,500 annually while increased standard tax deductions deliver nothing, since low-income households pay little to no federal income tax. Side B never overcame this comparative disadvantage, instead conceding that deductions would generate "little to no additional retirement income" for those non-savers. B also spent much of the debate defending refundable tax credits—a policy not in the motion—rather than justifying increased standard deductions on their own merits. While B landed valid concerns about low default rates and employer-plan coverage gaps, A neutralized the coverage issue by noting deductions also fail gig workers (who lack taxable income) and that state portable auto-enrollment plans already address the gap. On the decisive question of which policy more effectively increases long-run financial security, A showed that moving households from $0 to meaningful savings beats a tax incentive that bypasses the most vulnerable entirely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure questions and second rebuttal exposing that Side B was defending refundable tax credits outside the motion, crystallized with the "$0 versus $3,500" frame that B could not answer without conceding standard deductions fail the most at-risk households.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B wins decisively by forcing Side A to concede that increased tax deductions generate "little to no additional retirement income" for the 40% of households currently saving nothing. Side B successfully frames the core comparison as $3,500/year versus $0, making Side A's attacks on the "adequacy" of auto-enrollment backfire. Side B also effectively polices the motion's boundaries, correctly noting that Side A's repeated pivot to "refundable credits" abandons the assigned policy of tax deductions. Side A's coverage gap argument is mitigated by Side B's point about state portable plans, and Side A's own policy fails that same demographic due to low tax liability. Side B's rebuttals are sharper, directly answer pressure, and crystallize a clear, winning comparative path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which weaponize Side A's concession that deductions yield ~$0 for non-savers, cleanly resolving the central clash in PRO's favor ($3.5k > $0) while shutting down the off-topic refundable credit pivot.
