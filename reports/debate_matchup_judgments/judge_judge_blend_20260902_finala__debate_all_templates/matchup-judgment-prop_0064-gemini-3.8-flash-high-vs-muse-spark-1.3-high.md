### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): A (CON) built the more resilient case by consistently blurring B's central distinction between "acute safety systems" and "always-on coaching," showing that fatigue monitors and dosage checks only function because they watch continuously — a point B's Rebuttal 2 answer to the pressure question asserted but never actually defended, and B's closing simply reasserted the distinction without engaging A's rejoinder. A also introduced a genuinely damaging and largely unanswered point: that a "voluntary" regime stigmatizes the workers who most need coaching (the junior/least-skilled), undermining B's repeated claim that voluntary adoption solves everything. A's firewall proposal (bar coaching logs from discipline, judge only independent output) gave a concrete, policy-relevant answer to B's coercion pressure question, while B's rebuttal that mandated nudges "inevitably" carry disciplinary weight was asserted rather than demonstrated. B's case remained rhetorically strong and consistent (the "mind vs. labor" framing, panopticon imagery) but left A's coordination-failure and stigma arguments unrebutted, and its own safety/coaching line was pressured hard without a fully convincing defense.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 point that "a dosage check only fires because it parsed charting all shift, and a fatigue monitor only fires because it watched eyes all night" directly undercut B's Pressure Question answer that safety systems are categorically different from coaching companions, and B never recovered this distinction in the closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully defended the boundary between an employer’s right to evaluate output and its claim to control a worker’s moment-to-moment cognition. CON’s central case relied on conflating narrow safety alerts with broad productivity coaching; PRO exposed this as a category error in both rebuttals and showed that a mandate turns a “tutor” into an unaccountable warden. CON’s regulatory firewall collapsed under PRO’s pressure: if an employer requires the tool, ignoring its real-time nudges inevitably signals insubordination, and no data-use rule can neutralize the lived coercion of continuous cognitive monitoring. PRO’s closing crystallized the clash cleanly—employers buy labor, not minds—while CON never squared the contradiction of calling the companion empowering yet insisting workers be coerced into using it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s rebuttal of the safety/productivity conflation and the demonstration that CON’s “empowering tutor” and “necessary mandate” cannot coexist, which together dismantled CON’s constructive support and left the cognitive-autonomy frame dominant.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a clear, defensible line between discrete safety guardrails and continuous cognitive coaching, effectively neutralizing Side B's strongest analogies. Side A also successfully exposes the contradiction in Side B's case: if the AI is a helpful tutor, mandates are unnecessary; if mandates are necessary, the AI acts as a coercive warden, rendering Side B's proposed "firewall" against discipline practically unworkable. Side B's pragmatic argument about existing surveillance was strong, but Side A's defense of cognitive autonomy and sharp rebuttals secured the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they cleanly answer the pressure questions by distinguishing acute safety hazards from continuous cognitive parsing, and dismantle Side B's "firewall" defense by pointing out that a mandated instruction inherently carries disciplinary weight if defied.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): B built a tighter case around the motion's actual scope ("always-on" monitoring/coaching), repeatedly distinguishing this from the narrow safety checks, checklists, and alarms that A leaned on as examples. This exposed a real bait-and-switch in A's case: A's strongest analogies (surgical checklists, autopilot protocols) are episodic, not "always-on," and B named this directly and consistently. B's pressure question ("if it's truly private help, why must it be forced?") struck at the core tension in A's position, and B's subsequent answers (A1/A2) were direct and format-compliant, adding the sharp point that a firewalled-data promise is not credible when the employer owns the infrastructure. A did answer the "why mandate" question with a voluntary-safety-is-an-illusion argument (fatigue causes self-blind spots, so opt-outs collapse the safety net), which is a legitimate rebuttal, but A never fully closed the gap between mandating a discrete safety tool and mandating continuous behavioral coaching/scoring of tone and pace — the specific thing the motion bans. A's closing mostly repeated the liability-contradiction framing without sharpening this distinction, while B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly. B's rhetorical framing (tool vs. leash, coach-as-informant, "trust-me surveillance") was also more memorable and did real argumentative work rather than just decorating the case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 ([B2.1]-[B2.2]), where it separated narrow task-bound safeguards (checklists, dose alarms) from the always-on companion the motion actually targets, undercut A's central analogy set and was never fully resolved by A's later "ambient risk" replies.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by cleanly distinguishing narrow, task-specific safety mandates from always-on productivity surveillance, and showing the latter was not required for the safety cases CON raised. CON's liability and underground-surveillance arguments were blunted by PRO's polygraph analogy and the persistent tension that a truly private copilot would not need to be forced. PRO crystallized this clash more effectively across the later speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's exposure of CON's bait-and-switch between narrow safety alerts and comprehensive workplace monitoring, capped by the polygraph analogy and the pressure question showing that a private benefit would not need a mandate.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing and maintaining a crucial distinction between narrow, task-bound safety checks (which A concedes are permissible) and the always-on monitoring and coaching targeted by the proposition. Side B repeatedly conflates the two, defending checklists and dose alarms, which A easily dismisses as outside the ban's scope. Side A's pressure questions effectively expose that if the tool is merely a helpful copilot, a mandate is unnecessary, and if it is mandated, it functions as a tool for compliance and control. Side A's rhetorical framing is sharp and memorable, making the comparative stakes clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantles Side B's ambient risk argument by pointing out that fatigue is cured by rest and staffing rather than buzzing the tired, and exposes Side B's bait-and-switch between narrow life-rafts and always-on minders.
