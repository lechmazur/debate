### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON's historical grounding proved more resilient under pressure. PRO's strongest weapon—tariff escalation blocking African processed exports—was met with a real, if imperfect, counter (preference schemes) and then decisively undercut by CON's Vietnam/Bangladesh examples showing poor countries did industrialize by selling manufactures to rich markets, a point PRO never directly rebutted in its closing. CON also landed a sharp analytical distinction (population vs. purchasing power) that exposed a soft spot in PRO's "1.4 billion consumers" and "import bills prove demand" claims, and PRO's closing merely reasserted the point rather than answering the rebuttal. CON's use of historical import-substitution failures (Latin America, India, Egypt) directly targeted PRO's own "continental import substitution" framing from R2, and again went unanswered in PRO's closing. PRO's pressure questions were sharp and CON's answers, while contestable, were direct and format-compliant; CON's own questions likewise forced a real concession dynamic that PRO answered but did not fully neutralize. The clipped B opening is a genuine execution cost, but the lost content was minor restatement, not a load-bearing argument, so it only marginally tempers CON's otherwise stronger trajectory through the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 introduction of Vietnam and Bangladesh as counter-examples to PRO's "rich markets block African manufacturing" thesis was the most decisive exchange—it directly falsified PRO's central causal claim with concrete comparators, and PRO's closing failed to respond to it, leaving that point live and unanswered in CON's favor.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B won by separating manufacturing from regional-market prioritization. Its export-led alternative answered the extraction problem while showing that value-added production can still target richer global customers. Side A never adequately reconciled its claim that rich markets keep Africa at the bottom with the acknowledged experience of Asian manufacturers selling into those same markets. A also leaned heavily on unsupported tariff and trade-composition generalizations. B incurred a clipping cost and sometimes overstated history, but still provided the clearer comparative path to capital, scale, and industrial capability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response that Japan, Korea, and China industrialized by changing what they exported rather than abandoning rich customers decisively weakened A’s central claim that distant markets inherently prevent industrialization. B then reinforced this by distinguishing existing demand from the competitiveness needed to supply it.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by making the live comparison clearer: trade composition and market structure, not just destination size. A showed that six decades of raw/intermediate export-for-FX failed to industrialize Africa, that distant markets systematically buy unrefined inputs while blocking finished competition, and that intra-African commerce already skews manufactured while latent demand is visible in large finished-goods import bills. B’s export-led ladder and purchasing-power critiques were strong and landed real blows on thin regional volume and failed ISI, but B’s clipped opening and partial steelman of A as isolation weakened execution; A better answered the proposition as written—priority of continental manufacturing over raw-material dependence—without needing fragile new facts late.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal flipping B’s 15% arithmetic via composition + tariff escalation (raw exports to rich markets vs already-manufactured intra-African trade), forcing B onto capacity gaps and preferences rather than pure market logic.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides ran a coherent, well-structured case, and CON's forex/capital-goods mechanism was a genuinely strong recurring pressure point that PRO never fully dissolved (PAPSS doesn't buy turbines). But PRO did more productive work across rounds: it correctly distinguished East Asia's manufactured-goods export strategy from Africa's raw-material export strategy, undercutting CON's central historical analogy, and it built a two-track model (upgrade exports for hard currency + regional manufacturing for regional demand) that answered the "why not just keep exporting raw materials" challenge without conceding the motion. The most consequential moment was PRO's turn of CON's own answer to the Q1 pressure question: CON blamed decades of failed industrialization on "governance and capital misallocation" rather than the export-dependency model, and PRO's Rebuttal 2/Closing correctly noted this concession undercuts CON's own prescribed path, since the same governance failures would equally derail CON's hard-currency sequencing strategy. CON never directly re-engaged this turn in its closing, instead restating the forex mechanism without resolving the governance rebuttal, making its closing more recap than resolution. That asymmetry in adapting to the live clash is what tips a close, competent debate toward PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2/Closing turn on CON's "governance failure, not the model" answer to Q1 — showing that CON's own explanation for decades of stagnation undermines the feasibility of CON's own hard-currency-first sequencing, and CON never squarely answered this in the final speech.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by distinguishing prioritization from abruptly ending commodity exports. That allowed continued foreign-exchange earnings while shifting policy toward processing, capabilities, and regional demand. Side B identified a serious financing and sequencing problem, but repeatedly treated any reprioritization as strangling the existing revenue stream and never established why both could not coexist during transition. A also exposed that B's proposed commodity-funded industrialization depends on the same governance and allocation capacity that has repeatedly failed to deliver it. The clipped opening is a modest execution cost, but A still made the clearer comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest response was that commodity exports could continue during the transition while value-added global exports and regional manufacturing expand. This undercut Side B's repeated claim that prioritization necessarily causes an immediate foreign-exchange collapse, although B sharply answered that local-currency payment systems alone cannot finance imported machinery.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by defending the proposition as written—priority shift and value capture, not overnight severance—and by making the status-quo-failure clash decisive. B’s hard-currency/sequencing case was the strongest CON route and stayed live throughout, but A repeatedly forced the burden B never fully met: decades of raw-export priority still left Africa currency-starved and under-industrialized. B’s governance/misallocation reply explained past failure without restoring confidence in continued prioritization of the same model. A’s dual-track replies (process then sell globally and regionally; start with basic manufactures; soft-currency systems for regional retail) were imperfect but sufficient against a CON case that often argued against abandonment rather than against prioritization. Closing crystallizations favored A: “dependency with better excuses” vs. repeated FX restatement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Q1/R2/closing chain: if commodity exports were the industrialization engine, why after decades are the factories still missing—and why is governance failure an excuse that only convicts the model CON wants to keep prioritizing.
