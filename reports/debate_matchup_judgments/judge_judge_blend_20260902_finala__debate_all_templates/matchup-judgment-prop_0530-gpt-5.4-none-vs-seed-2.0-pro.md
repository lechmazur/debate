### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won a clear but competitive debate by sustaining two arguments CON never fully neutralized: that institutional control is not neutral but is a specific form of bias favoring the same gatekeepers who often failed victims, and that "substantial control" is meaningfully distinct from absolute veto. CON's strongest move was the allocation-mechanism problem—who certifies which descendant groups?—and the suppression examples were concrete and initially damaging. But PRO's Rebuttal 2 provided workable answers: institutions can certify descendant standing without displacing it, and the distinction between falsifying the evidentiary record and governing how verified facts are presented and contextualized is real and already practiced in museum ethics. CON's closing rightly crystallized the "hollow label" dilemma (institutions must certify → they retain power), but this goes too far; certifying a legal body's existence is not the same as controlling what that body decides. CON's transparency-rules defense also never answered the pointed institutional-bias argument: enforceable disclosure rules do not give a museum or state superior moral authority to decide how the dead of communities they failed are treated. PRO's closing framing—"our side may require messy shared governance, but theirs normalizes a cleaner injustice"—was the most memorable line in the debate and captured a genuine comparative weight. CON's opening and rebuttals were efficient and precise, but several of CON's key objections (suppression, multi-community conflict) were answered adequately by PRO's second rebuttal, while CON never fully addressed PRO's core institutional-bias argument across any round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 [B2.3–B2.6]: PRO reframed CON's multi-community conflict examples (Serb/Bosniak, multi-ethnic Nazi labor camps) as arguments for structuring shared governance among all affected descendant groups rather than arguments against descendant authority at all. This turned CON's flagship opening examples into support for PRO's position, and CON never recovered a clean version of those examples for the rest of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully establishing a moral baseline for descendant authority and defending it against Side A's practical objections. While Side A sharply pressed the administrative difficulty of multi-ethnic sites and the risk of bias, Side B's analogy to contested estates cleanly separated procedural certification from substantive moral control, neutralizing Side A's strongest offense.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 explanation that courts can recognize estates without owning the dead brilliantly dismantled Side A's argument that institutional certification negates descendant governance.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won because its moral and epistemic case for descendant authority survived B's administrative objections, while B never overcame the fundamental problem that institutional control normalizes bias by parties with weaker claims and mixed incentives. A's distinction between factual record and interpretive framing answered B's suppression concerns, and the family-burial analogy exposed the implausibility of defaulting to strangers when descendant allocation becomes contested. Execution costs from A's clipped speeches narrowed the margin but did not obscure A's clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to B's multi-community objection in A2.1/A2.6, showing that overlapping claims multiply rather than erase descendant standing and that the answer is shared descendant governance rather than reverting power to institutions.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the stronger path to victory by maintaining a consistent and consequential distinction the motion demands: "substantial control" means final authority, not consultation or co-curation. CON repeatedly caught PRO silently redefining "substantial control" as merely "guardrails against dehumanization"—a reframe that amounts to accepting most of what CON already endorses. That catch (A2.6, echoed in A4.4–A4.6) was never squarely answered by PRO. CON also pressed the hard-case governance problem—who holds final authority when survivors, local families, diaspora, and multiple groups disagree—and PRO's answer (self-determined existing governance structures, inter-nation treaty processes) is specific enough to be plausible in one context but plainly does not generalize to every atrocity site where descent lines are fractured or contested. CON's "institutional design for hard cases" framing held throughout: even if institutions have often failed, that proves only that oversight and co-governance are needed, not that substantial final authority should transfer to any single stakeholder class. PRO's strongest contribution was the empirical record of state sanitization (residential schools, plantation narratives) and the claim that community-led excavations in practice invite forensic teams and publish evidence. CON never fully neutralized that empirical pattern, which kept PRO's case live. But PRO's closing was short and largely repetitive rather than crystallizing a new comparative edge, and PRO's core affirmative case never escaped the tension that the motion asks for "substantial control" while PRO's examples only show communities wanting guardrails and expert inclusion. CON's clipped speeches are a real execution cost but the visible portions were substantively strong enough to carry the comparison. Overall, CON gave the cleaner reason to vote: honor descendants with deep involvement and binding dignity protections, but do not hand any single constituency final authority over evidence and public memory, because the hardest moments are precisely when sacred grief and civic truth collide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.6 (Rebuttal 1): catching PRO consistently redefining "substantial control" as merely guardrails against dehumanization, while the motion explicitly grants final power over excavation, display, and explanation—including veto-like authority at the exact moments when community preferences and transparent public truth collide. This was never adequately answered by PRO and remained the decisive live clash through the closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully distinguishing 'substantial control' from consultation, proving that granting final authority to one group risks the broader public's access to historical evidence. While Side B effectively weaponized the history of state failures, Side A's point that institutional design must account for hard cases dismantled Side B's reliance on assumptions of good community behavior without a limiting principle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, where they point out that Side B's defense against evidence-suppression relies entirely on assurances of good behavior rather than providing a limiting principle for when goals conflict.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): Side A won by establishing an empirical asymmetry that Side B could not overcome. A demonstrated with concrete examples that state-controlled institutions have repeatedly sanitized atrocity narratives, while showing that descendant communities have both effective self-governance mechanisms and a proven track record of facilitating forensic truth without dehumanizing display. B raised legitimate theoretical concerns about hard cases and veto power, but offered no comparable real-world evidence of community control suppressing evidence, relied heavily on hypothetical risks, and suffered severe execution costs from three clipped speeches that truncated key rebuttals and the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.3), which directly answered B's pressure questions by citing inter-nation treaty negotiation processes as a concrete rule for resolving internal disagreement, and by noting the absence of any systemic record of descendant communities suppressing forensic evidence while highlighting the extensive documented record of state institutions doing exactly that. This exchange defanged B's core institutional-design objections and sharpened the comparative framing of proven harm versus hypothetical risk.
