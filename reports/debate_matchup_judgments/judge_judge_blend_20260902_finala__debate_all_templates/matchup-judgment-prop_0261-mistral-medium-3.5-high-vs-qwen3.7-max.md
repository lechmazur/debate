### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): A wins narrowly. B made the strongest practical challenge with pricing, subscriptions, copyright, and account privacy, but leaned too hard on the claim that inheritance becomes a transferable or infinitely destructive secondary market. A's later narrowing to one-time, non-commercial, non-resale inheritance directly answered that central impact and made the property inconsistency feel more decisive. A had some execution costs from the clipped rebuttal and shakier support on courts and GDPR, but B's economic doom scenario remained overstated and repetitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal was most decisive: by distinguishing inheritance from resale and answering the legal-mechanism question with a statutory override, it blunted B's main route that the mandate would destroy digital markets.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully distinguishing the economics of digital licenses from physical property. Side A proved that because digital goods do not degrade, legally mandating multi-generational inheritance would force publishers to inflate prices or shift entirely to subscription models. Side B failed to neutralize this economic impact, merely insisting that inheritance prevents a commercial "secondary market," which did not address Side A's point about perpetual, multi-generational primary market loss.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal, which capitalized on Side B's admission that publishers could "price for it upfront," effectively turning Side B's own mitigation into proof of Side A's core impact.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): CON built the stronger case by consistently showing that zero-marginal-cost digital licenses differ fundamentally from physical goods, that statutory override would force price hikes or subscriptions, and that PRO’s technical fixes (family sharing, new legislation) fail to solve distribution economics. PRO’s property-rights framing was undermined in every exchange and never neutralized the core pricing and market-destruction objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1–B2.5 and B4.3–B4.6 directly dismantled PRO’s “price for perpetuity” claim and exposed the secondary-market and distribution-copy problems that PRO’s inheritance-is-not-resale distinction could not answer.
