### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won by establishing that the default screen is where discovery actually happens, and filling it with paid placement makes the app a steering tool rather than a search tool. CON's "functional access" defense—that visible price/distance sorts solve the problem—never overcame the live point that ordinary users rely on defaults and that sponsors pay precisely because those defaults capture attention. PRO successfully narrowed the clash, extracted CON's concession that hidden controls obstruct, and showed CON's model makes truthful discovery opt-in. CON landed strong hits on the vagueness of PRO's "meaningful portion" and the subjectivity of "struggle," but could not square the circle on why a heavily sponsored default does not constitute obstruction for users who never tap away. PRO's clipped speeches were an execution cost, yet the visible argumentative core remained more persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal 1 argument that "you cannot search for a restaurant you do not know exists," dismantling CON's claim that accessible sorts eliminate struggle by showing the homepage is the discovery mechanism, not merely a billboard.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): PRO kept the motion modest — not ban ads, but stop heavy privileging that buries cheap nearby independents. Its live route — defaults are the product, you can't search a place you don't know exists, and auctions scale with budget — survived. CON's functional-access test (labels + one-tap sort) was exposed as covering only users who already express a filter, leaving the core default-user struggling. CON conceded buried sorts obstruct yet denied density itself matters, and its helpful-independent claim never overcame the entry-fee dynamic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal that B1.1's "search its name" escape hatch fails for discovery — you cannot search what you don't know — plus A3's pressure forcing CON to admit a buried sort obstructs, collapsing CON's "struggle is too subjective" line.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively controlling the definition of "struggle" and the functional reality of user interfaces. While Side B argued that visible filter buttons meant users weren't struggling, Side A brilliantly exposed the contradiction in treating the default screen as valuable enough to sell to advertisers but trivial enough to dismiss when users don't actively change the filters. Side A's rhetorical trap—that Side B cannot treat the default as valuable enough to sell and trivial enough to ignore—perfectly crystallized the clash. Side A also effectively neutralized Side B's argument about independents using ads by pointing out that auction mechanics inherently favor deep-pocketed chains. Side A's minor word-limit clippings only affected concluding flourishes and did not impact the substantive strength of their case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they trapped Side B's concession that "buried" menus obstruct by applying the exact same logic to the default screen, proving that a visible but universally ignored filter is functionally identical to a buried one.

### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing that PRO could not settle on an administrable standard without collapsing the motion into either a toothless nullity or unworkable pixel regulation. CON showed that if the test is functional—users can reach cheap independents via filters—every major app already complies, making the rule empty; if the test is quantitative—screen-space dominance—it requires regulators to design feeds. CON compounded this by demonstrating that PRO’s alternative discovery mechanism (rotation among “suitable” matches) effectively reduces newcomers without reviews to a random lottery, while paid placement is the one non-incumbent signal a new kitchen can actually use. PRO’s closing rhetoric remained eloquent, but never squaredly resolved the tension in their standard or showed that capping sponsorship would help, rather than freeze, the independents they claimed to defend.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s trap over the functional versus quantity standard—showing that PRO’s functional test (two taps to filters) renders the motion vacuous while PRO’s quantity intuitions (screen-space dominance) require unadministrable pixel counting, and crystallizing that contradiction by forcing PRO to concede an 80-percent-sponsored feed could comply.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): PRO kept a consistent modest line: sponsorship may buy attention but not practical invisibility, tested functionally by whether cheap nearby matches are quickly reachable via obvious controls. That absorbs CON's best points – limited paid launch remains allowed, relevance can be price/distance/cuisine/availability not just ratings, and if apps already comply burden is low. CON's core routes – newcomer ladder and revenue-must-rise – depend on treating any cap as a ban and on dismissing non-incumbency signals as randomness, which PRO directly answered in A4.1/A4.2.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.2 / A4.1 – PRO answers Q2 with concrete non-incumbency relevance signals (price, distance, cuisine, hours, dietary) and clarifies the 80% sponsored feed complies iff the slider genuinely surfaces independents without another wall, collapsing CON's quantity-vs-function dilemma.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively exposing the contradictions in Side A's standard. Side A shifted from a "screen space" quantity test to a "two taps" functional test, allowing Side B to prove that under the functional test, the status quo already complies (making the proposition a nullity that still carries compliance costs), while the quantity test requires burdensome pixel-counting. Furthermore, Side B's argument that organic rankings inherently favor incumbents, making paid placement the only viable discovery tool for newcomers, was highly persuasive and effectively dismantled Side A's "rotation" solution as a mere lottery.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side A's "rotation" answer to the pressure question, showing that rotating among "equally suitable" matches without history is just a lottery that buries newcomers, while also cementing the trap that Side A's functional test makes the proposition a nullity.
