### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but A built a slightly stronger and more durable path through the clash. A's core structural tension—that bands wide enough to preserve matching flexibility are too wide to constrain discrimination, while bands narrow enough to bind employers destroy matching—was introduced early, sharpened under pressure, and never fully neutralized. The most decisive moment came when B's Q2 trapped A into naming a workable ratio: A's answer ("no ratio exists due to structural contradiction") was bold, honest, and effectively turned the question into a structural indictment of the mandate itself. B's closing did not answer this. B's best counterpunch was reframing internal equity adjustments as gap reduction rather than efficiency cost—a genuinely clever move that A only partially answered with "destructive compression." But A correctly pointed out that raising the floor does not address within-bracket clustering or the ceiling dynamics where most of the gap accumulates, and B never closed this hole. B's Colorado evidence remained contested throughout; A's selection-bias argument was never refuted, and B's response (remote-worker applicability, newer NY/CA timelines) was only partial. B's floor mechanism—"eliminates the $42k offer"—is compelling at the bottom of the distribution but leaves the structural argument about where the gap actually lives (occupational segregation, promotion tracks, seniority) largely unanswered. A consistently returned to the proposition's comparative burden and made a cleaner weighing argument: efficiency losses are immediate and structural, gap reduction is marginal and easily gamed. Two of B's turns were clipped, which is a real execution cost, but the visible portions of those turns did not substantially change the decisive clash. Overall A made the decision easier by maintaining a coherent comparative frame across all rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal exchange was B's Q2 ("what minimum-to-maximum ratio constrains discrimination without harming efficiency?") and A's direct answer ("no ratio exists—the tension is structural, not arithmetic"). A's refusal to name a workable number was honest and clinically effective: it exposed the mandate's architectural flaw rather than retreating into evasion, and B's closing never adequately responded to it.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): B won because it made the floor mechanism and incumbent-pay adjustment turn do comparative work: even broad ranges prevent worst lowballs, improve anchors, and expose underpaid workers, while A's efficiency harms remained more speculative and often described the pay-gap correction as a cost. A's wide/narrow dilemma was the best CON argument, but B persuasively showed partial compression still counts and A did not prove matching losses outweigh it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive exchange was turning A's internal equity crisis: if posted ranges force firms to raise underpaid incumbents, that is direct pay-gap compression rather than merely an efficiency loss.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): PRO wins by successfully turning CON’s internal equity “crisis” into a direct gap-reduction mechanism and by demonstrating that even wide posted ranges establish a hard floor that prevents the extreme lowball offers that disproportionately anchor women and minorities. While CON effectively highlighted how employers can game wide bands and argued that the real gap lives in promotions and occupational segregation, PRO’s compounding-salary mechanism and early-screening efficiency defense provided a cleaner, more direct path to the motion’s comparative weighing. PRO also handled pressure questions more directly, neutralizing CON’s structural dilemma by showing the floor works regardless of band width.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s turn of CON’s internal equity argument across Rebuttals 1 and 2, reframing forced incumbent pay adjustments not as a market distortion but as a secondary channel of gap reduction that directly advances the proposition’s core metric.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): Side B wins on balance across three reasons. First, execution: Side A had two clipped speeches (A1 and A4) for exceeding the word limit, meaning the judge never sees the tail of A's opening or second rebuttal. That is a real cost, not a cosmetic one, since we simply don't know what arguments A chose to put last. Side B finished all speeches within limits. Second, on the most contested clash—whether a posted range actually reduces the ask-gap or merely shifts bias to within-band placement—B's "bounded variance" response (B4.1–4.2) is the clearest single rebuttal in the debate. It concedes that within-band placement bias can persist but correctly notes that the magnitude of discrimination collapses from an unconstrained spectrum to a bounded interval, that the remaining variance is observable and subject to comparative scrutiny, and that a $5,000 gap is categorically different from a $25,000 opacity gap. A never directly neutralizes this distinction; A's "double-edged sword" (A4.3) that visible floors themselves anchor disadvantaged applicants is interesting but speculative and underdeveloped. Third, B successfully turns A's own structural-driver concession. A argues that occupational segregation and promotion bias are the real engines—but B responds that when workers are already locked in lower-paid segregated tracks, the starting anchor is precisely inescapable; the structural barriers make market-mobility recovery harder, not easier. A's closing effectively recites this concession without closing the loop. A's strongest live point—that firms preemptively narrow bands downward, compressing wages for everyone—was not squarely answered by B and remains a real cost on the efficiency ledger. But B's temporal asymmetry framing (transient administrative friction vs. compounding equity gains) and the bounded-variance argument together create a cleaner path to winning the proposition as written. The margin is meaningful but not dominant because A's structural-driver critique and within-band bias arguments were genuinely damaging, and the preemptive compression point remains live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1–4.2 (bounded variance): When A pressed that bias simply shifts to where within the band an offer lands, B responded that the relevant question is magnitude—eliminating the sub-floor exploitation scenario ($45k when the band is $60k–$90k) is a categorical gain, not mere cosmetic compliance, and the remaining within-band variance is bounded, observable, and remediable through mobility and internal scrutiny in a way that hidden-budget lowballing was not. This directly addressed A's most important pressure point and meaningfully reduced its force without overclaiming total victory.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): B won because it made the cleaner comparative case: transparency directly reduces information asymmetry and at least eliminates below-range lowballing, while A’s efficiency harms from rigid bands, delays, and wage compression remained more speculative and overclaimed. A was right to stress structural pay-gap drivers and within-band bias, but B answered that the proposition need not solve every driver and that bounded bias is materially better than opaque bargaining. B’s exact lifetime-gap numbers deserved some discount, but A also relied on unsupported predictions, and A had clipped turns as an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest rebuttal was that even a broad $60k-$90k range prevents a qualified applicant from being pushed to $45k, so A’s “broad bands are cosmetic” attack did not neutralize the core equity mechanism.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a cleaner comparative framework: temporal asymmetry (one-time administrative friction vs. compounding lifetime earnings gaps) and magnitude reduction (caging bias within a bounded range rather than demanding total elimination). Side A effectively turned Side B's structural drivers argument, noting that if occupational mobility and promotion ladders are already constrained, the initial salary anchor becomes even more critical to prevent irreversible lowballing. Side B raised valid points about within-band placement and matching delays, but two clipped turns disrupted its development, and its efficiency harms remained more speculative against Side A's grounded adaptation examples. Side A's direct A1/A2 responses and consistent weighing made the path to victory clearer and directly satisfied the proposition's comparative burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 directly answered the pressure questions by reframing the "bias shifts within the band" critique as a magnitude win ($5k bounded variance vs. $25k unconstrained lowball) and turning the structural mobility point to show why initial anchors matter more when job-hopping is constrained. This crystallized the comparative stakes and neutralized CON's core efficiency trade-off.
