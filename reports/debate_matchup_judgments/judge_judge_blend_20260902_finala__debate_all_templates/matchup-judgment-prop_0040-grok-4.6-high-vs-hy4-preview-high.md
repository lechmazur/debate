### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): The pivotal exchange was B's overbreadth attack: the motion as written bans any company from training a mimicking chatbot absent pre-death opt-in, with no commercial carve-out, so it necessarily sweeps in family memorial bots and classroom tools. A tried to save the case by narrowing "the motion" to "commercial mimics" [A2.8], which B correctly flagged in Q1 as a concession that the literal text is overbroad. A's answer to that pressure (A4.1) simply asserted companies are always the banned actor regardless of client, but never squared this with the earlier narrowing move, leaving a live inconsistency. B also pressed the sharper dilemma in Q2: if opt-in doesn't make invented answers true, why does it cure the harm at all—only who authorizes it? A's response (A4.3) tried to say only the person's own yes can accept that risk, but this is asserted rather than defended against B's parallel point that an heir's license is likewise "the living rightsholder's authorization," not a fictional stand-in for consent. B's distinction between "claiming the dead said yes" (fiction) versus "the living owner exercising present authority" (not fiction) is a genuine conceptual move A never fully dismantled — A kept restating that heirs "cannot volunteer someone else's interactive self" without rebutting why that differs from other descendible identity rights (voice licensing, publicity rights) that A itself invoked in the opening. B also lands a real practical point unanswered by A: banning domestic training does not stop offshore manufacture and forfeits the accountability (heir veto, deception liability, labeling) that regulation could provide — A's closing simply restates "keep the letters, don't hire a firm" without addressing the black-market leakage problem it conceded exists. Both sides argued with discipline and no fake sourcing, but B's case held together better under the specific pressure exchange and better resolved the who-authorizes-it question with a coherent alternative regime, while A's case showed a real crack (the commercial-carve-out concession) that was pointed out and not fully repaired.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q1 (B3.1) catching A's [A2.8] narrowing to "commercial mimics" as a concession that the motion's plain text is overbroad, which A's A4.1 answer did not fully resolve.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently distinguishing preservation of genuine archives from manufacturing an interactive identity that invents new judgments. Side A offered a serious estate-licensing alternative, but never fully justified why heirs should acquire authority to volunteer another person’s simulated self. Its strongest examples also overstated disappearance: journals and recordings remain available even if an unauthorized mimic is prohibited. B directly answered both pressure questions and made prior opt-in a coherent acceptance of distortion risk rather than an arbitrary checkbox.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it embraced the family-memorial implication instead of evading it, explained that opt-in authorizes the risk without making generated statements true, and exposed why estate ownership is not equivalent to the deceased’s consent.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A wins by building a sharper, more cohesive case around the unique harm of generative AI: it invents new, unendorsed speech. Side A successfully distinguished AI mimics from static archives or bounded voice licenses, arguing that only the deceased can accept the risk of their interactive identity being distorted. Side B's strongest point was the overbreadth of the motion regarding family memorial bots, but Side A effectively bit the bullet, arguing that companies should not manufacture unauthorized talking doubles even for grieving families. Side A's rhetoric was more memorable and its rebuttals more directly dismantled Side B's reliance on estate control for generative speech.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it cleanly answered the pressure questions by accepting the motion's strictness on family-hired companies and sharply distinguishing between an heir administering property versus volunteering someone else's interactive self to generate unendorsed speech.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B's pressure question forced A into a revealing bite-the-bullet moment: A conceded the rule would ban even a labeled, scholarly chatbot trained on a dead author's already-published novels, since no AI-specific opt-in could have existed. B then exploited this consistently in Rebuttal 2 and Closing ("freezes the public record at a use no author contemplated"), turning A's "narrow rule" framing against itself. B also landed a sharp tu quoque against A's signature DadBot example: even with opt-in, the bot could still generate false first-person praise, so the checkbox does not uniquely solve the dignitary harm A relies on as its central emotional hook. A's answers (autonomy-as-risk-transfer, licensing-vs-training distinction) were principled and consistent, but largely absorbed rather than defused B's reductio, leaving B's practical-cost narrative as the more decisive, memorable throughline by the closing exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question forcing A to answer "No" on the labeled literary chatbot trained on published novels, followed by B's Rebuttal 2/Closing turning that concession into a "freezes the public record" reductio against A's claim that the rule was narrow.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the relevant harm the unauthorized creation of a synthetic persona, not merely deceptive or commercial outputs. Side A effectively exposed the rule’s breadth through the literary-chatbot example and offered estate control, labeling, and endorsement law, but never fully answered why heirs should be able to authorize an intimate new identity use that the deceased did not choose. Side B also showed that archives, biographies, and recordings remain available, limiting Side A’s claimed cultural cost. Its erroneous reference labels were an execution flaw, but the underlying responses were clear and accurate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest response was that opt-in need not prevent every inaccurate output: it prevents the prior unauthorized transformation into a mimic. That directly answered Side A’s claim that labels and estate control solve the same harm and preserved B’s autonomy-based case.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A built a highly compelling philosophical framework, distinguishing "resurrection" from "quotation" and "manufacture" from "memory." A's rhetorical crystallization ("Licensing transfers what was made; training a mimic commissions what was never made") effectively neutralized B's analogies to biographies and estate licensing. While B made strong practical points about the de facto ban and estate control, B struggled to counter A's core claim that the intrinsic dignitary harm is the unauthorized transformation itself, which output-focused laws (fraud, endorsement) fail to prevent. A's direct, principled answers in the pressure round and cohesive closing secured a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction in Rebuttal 1 and Closing that AI mimicry is "resurrection, not quotation" and that "licensing transfers what was made, while training a mimic commissions what was never made," which decisively dismantled Side B's analogies to biographers, archives, and estate licensing.
