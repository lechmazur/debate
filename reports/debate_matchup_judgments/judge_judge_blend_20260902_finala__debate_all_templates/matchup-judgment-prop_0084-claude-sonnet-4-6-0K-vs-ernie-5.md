### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (PRO)): Side A won by consistently narrowing the debate to the gap between Side B's narrow success stories (like bottle deposits) and the sweeping scope of the proposition.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument in Rebuttal 2 that Side B's reliance on caps concedes the policy is inherently a tax that requires trusting uncaptured regulators, which history does not support.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (no reasoning) (CON)): CON won by exposing a fatal scope gap in PRO's case: every real-world success PRO cited—Germany's bottle deposits, deposit-return schemes, the EU Directive—was a narrow, product-specific program, while the motion demands a broad "much more" cost shift across all packaging. CON showed that PRO never bridged that gap, and instead slid between the two policies every round. CON also landed the incidence argument (households pay twice during transition) and the regulatory-capture point (caps are needed precisely because unconstrained levies become taxes, yet those caps require capture-prone regulators). PRO's opening was clipped, relied on brittle fake precision ("98% recovery," "$200 billion subsidy"), and never offered a concrete mechanism for short-term household savings. CON's skepticism was better grounded and more consistently applied.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal crystallizing that PRO had spent the entire debate conflating narrow deposit-return schemes with the sweeping "much more" proposition, thereby collapsing PRO's empirical support under the actual motion.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.0 (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (no reasoning) (CON)): Side B (CON) wins by consistently exposing the gap between PRO’s narrow, context-dependent examples (deposit schemes, Germany) and the motion’s broad demand for producers to pay “much more.” CON’s economic analysis of cost incidence—showing that fees pass to consumers and create a regressive double-burden during the transition—was never fully neutralized by PRO’s long-term innovation optimism. CON also effectively turned PRO’s concession about fee caps into a liability by highlighting regulatory capture, undermining PRO’s trust in calibrated implementation. While PRO followed format well and presented a coherent normative frame, it relied on brittle, unsourced statistics and repetitive assertions that failed to resolve the core mechanistic burdens CON pressed. CON’s case was tighter, more epistemically disciplined, and offered a cleaner comparative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s response to PRO’s “caps prevent overreach” argument (B2.5-B2.6, B4.3-B4.4) was decisive. By pointing out that caps require regulators, and regulators are historically susceptible to industry capture, CON turned PRO’s proposed safeguard into a concession that actually reinforced the risk of policy distortion and tax drift. This exchange crystallized the debate’s core clash over institutional trust, cost incidence, and mechanistic realism.
