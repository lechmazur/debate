### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-organized cases and answered pressure questions in the required A1/A2 format, keeping this close. A's strongest thread — that tips don't track service, aren't transparently delivered, and outsource wage risk onto the least powerful workers — was pressed consistently and never fully died. But B adapted more sharply across rounds: it reframed volatility as "compensated risk" with real upside, introduced the correctability distinction (a customer can override a tip in the moment, but cannot override hiring, scheduling, or pool-splitting bias under fixed pay), and then converted A's own concession that a service charge "still breathes with sales" into the debate's most decisive rhetorical pivot — "a pay cut with the same weather." A's closing reasserted its opening themes (who owns Tuesday, tips aren't direct) but never squarely answered B's sharper claims about uncorrectable structural bias or the shift-cutting mechanism from fixed labor costs. That left B's later-round reframing as the most live, least-neutralized argument standing at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3's move turning A's "breathes with sales" admission into "risk was just socialized into a smaller, flatter pool," crystallized in the closing line "a pay cut with the same weather," was the pivotal exchange A never fully defused.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully turning Side B's core argument about wage stability. When Side B conceded that a service charge "still breathes with sales" to defend against the impact on businesses, Side A sharply framed Side B's world as "a pay cut with the same weather"—lower overall pay with the same volatility, plus added dependence on management for shifts. Side A also effectively neutralized the bias argument by pointing out that relying on a manager for a pooled split or a Friday night shift privatizes bias with zero customer override.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, which seized on Side B's statement that a service charge "breathes with sales" to prove that wage risk never disappears under Side B's model, but is simply socialized into a smaller pool that removes server upside.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by making the proposition's core decision rule easier to apply: hidden demand-risk subsidy plus weak service signal versus honest pricing and a wage floor. Across openings through closing, A kept returning to who should own a slow Tuesday and steadily dismantled B's two load-bearing fictions—that tips reliably track service quality and that they go directly to the individual server. B mounted a strong economics-and-agency defense (variable labor costs, upside for skilled servers, risk of thinner schedules) and landed a sharp late punch on A's "still breathes with sales" concession, but never fully rehabilitated "control" once A showed the residual choice is mostly mood/bias leverage rather than quality grading. A's pressure questions were cleaner and more damaging; both sides answered in format, yet A's A1/A2 reframes (menu already embeds ~20%, earmarked charge + floor) weighed more cleanly against the motion than B's commission analogy and pool-dependence counter. Final crystallization favored A: once accuracy and directness fall, B's remaining system looks like the guilt/hidden-cost model the proposition rejects.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's sustained rebuttal of B's control claim (tips track bill size/bias more than service; pools/tip-outs already break "direct" earmarking), sharpened by Pressure Q1 on why only servers absorb demand risk other staff/businesses do not, and closed by the "who owns Tuesday" crystallization.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B established and sustained a decisive structural point that A never fully neutralized: under current law, tips are worker property while a service charge is house property, meaning the very "reform" A proposes strips workers of a legal protection they currently have and hands discretion to management instead. B reinforced this with the concrete (if approximate) Danny Meyer "Hospitality Included" reversal example and the sharp floor-vs-ceiling reframe of A's minimum-wage/seatbelt analogy, showing that forcing the switch cuts high earners' pay rather than lifting a floor for everyone. A's rebuttals (calling the ownership issue a "design choice," claiming service charges are more auditable) were plausible but never actually closed the legal gap B kept exploiting, and A's closing still rested on abstract framing ("guilt vs accountability") rather than resolving that concrete asymmetry. A scored well on bias/kitchen-inequity arguments and vivid framing, but B's answers to the pressure questions were tighter and more responsive, and B's case progressively narrowed the debate to the property-rights crux more effectively. B's rebuttal 1 being clipped for length is a real execution cost, but it did not cost B the central thread of the argument, which survived intact through closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's repeated hammering of the tips-as-worker-property vs. service-charge-as-house-property distinction (introduced in B1.4, sharpened in B2.6, and never squarely defeated by A's "design choice, not destiny" response in A2.4/A4.4) was the single most decisive exchange, since it undercut A's core promise that a service charge model would be more accountable and fair to workers.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by turning the resolution's mechanism (service charge) against Side B, successfully arguing that converting tips (worker property) into service charges (house property) legalizes managerial skimming. Side B never fully resolved this legal distinction, allowing Side A to offer tip pools as a superior, property-retaining alternative for kitchen equity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 provided a decisive answer to B's second pressure question, contrasting the illegality of managers stealing tips with the complete legality of owners reallocating a service charge.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the proposition’s core live—employer vs. stranger payroll responsibility, honesty of price, and BOH equity—and by converting B’s strongest facts (server exits, tip ownership) into a collective-action/race-to-the-bottom problem rather than pure preference. B’s ownership and thin-margin case stayed potent and consistent, but the clipped R1 and weaker weighing of who should bear business risk left A with the cleaner decisive frame after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A4.1/A2.3 collective-action answer to the quitting/pay-cut charge: lonely first-movers get punished; floors (min wage/health codes) require common rules, so exits do not prove workers prefer tipping insecurity.
