### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B built a more durable path to victory across the debate. Its core arguments—information asymmetry, consumer-protection parallels, and the point that only landlords hiding known problems benefit from silence—remained live through every round. Crucially, Side B's Rebuttal 2 landed the debate's decisive exchange: it exposed a contradiction in Side A's position. Side A had simultaneously argued that (a) tenants can discover this information through crime maps and reviews, and (b) formal lease disclosure creates catastrophic new liability. Side B correctly showed these positions are irreconcilable: if the information is already publicly available, formal disclosure adds no novel liability; if it genuinely isn't discoverable, tenants need the protection. Side A's best counter—that a lease transforms public data into a contractual warranty triggering tort premises liability—was a real and substantive point, and Side B never fully demolished it. But Side B had partly pre-empted it by arguing the landlord already possessed actual knowledge, meaning the duty arguably exists regardless. Side A's category-error argument (dynamic behavior vs. static physical defects) was maintained consistently but was never developed into a full normative rebuttal; it explained why the analogy to lead paint is imperfect without explaining why the informational gap that justifies disclosure obligations should not apply here. Side A's "two-year cutoff" attack on regulatory line-drawing was effective pressure, but Side B answered it adequately by pointing to how analogous housing disclosures already work with thresholds. Side A's definitional objections (hypersensitive tenants, frivolous complaints) were met with the reasonable response that landlords already make credibility assessments in nuisance and eviction proceedings. The clipped closing is a minor execution cost for Side B but did not materially affect the outcome since the contradiction argument was already fully on the table. On balance, Side B answered the most important pressure more directly, framed the stakes more memorably, and left fewer live burdens unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.3–B4.4] exposing the irreconcilable contradiction in Side A's position: Side A had argued both that tenants can find this information through public crime maps and reviews, and that putting the same information in a lease creates catastrophic new legal liability. Side B correctly identified these cannot both be true—if the information is publicly discoverable, formal disclosure doesn't manufacture novel liability the landlord didn't already face; if it isn't discoverable, tenants genuinely need the protection. Side A's Rebuttal 2 response [A4.1] attempted to distinguish informational public data from contractual warranties, which was a real legal point, but it arrived late and was less persuasive as a normative defense of concealment than as a technical tort observation.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A effectively neutralized Side B's core argument about an 'irreconcilable contradiction' by clearly explaining the legal distinction between a tenant discovering public information and a landlord formally adopting a behavioral nuisance as a known defect in a binding contract. Side A's underlying premise—that dynamic, third-party behavior cannot be regulated or disclosed in the same way as static physical defects—remained robust and highlighted the practical litigation risks of the mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively answered Side B's pressure question by explaining how a formal lease disclosure legally transforms informal public data into a contractual warranty and premises liability.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A wins by exposing an unresolved contradiction in B's position on public discoverability versus lease-created liability, directly answering pressure questions, and sustaining clear comparative pressure across rounds without evasion or widening.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON built the stronger technical case and maintained it under pressure. Its two decisive live arguments — (1) the perverse incentive structure (documenting formally creates disclosure liability, so rational landlords resolve problems informally, off-paper, leaving tenants with no paper trail at all) and (2) the sharp distinction between specific tenant-violation records used in eviction proceedings and the categorical "building-wide chronic condition" label this mandate demands — were introduced cleanly and never fully neutralized. PRO's best counter on the perverse incentive was that landlords "must document to evict," but CON correctly identified this as conflating two distinct record types, and PRO's "same underlying reality" response in Rebuttal 2 did not close the gap. PRO also never provided a threshold, safe harbor, or compliance mechanism to resolve the ambiguity in "recurring," "severe," or "chronic," leaving CON's "litigation bait" critique fully live through the closing. PRO's strongest contributions — the information-asymmetry opening, the "constructive eviction as trap" point, and the Q2 pressure question about proving fraud without internal logs — were genuine pressure points, and CON's answer on fraud discovery (subpoena once a claim is filed) was only partially satisfying given the burden it imposes. PRO's moral framing ("financial suicide notes," "sanitized illusion") was rhetorically vivid but did not resolve the mechanism question CON kept pressing. CON's closing was clipped — a real execution cost — but the key arguments were already crystallized in Rebuttal 2. On balance, CON's identification of how the mandate inverts incentives and punishes the exact transparency it purports to demand was the decisive analytical contribution neither side could fully resolve in PRO's favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [A2.5] "accidentally confirms" turn in Rebuttal 1, where it identified that PRO's own concession — that disclosure "naturally lapses" once a problem is resolved — structurally rewards the willfully ignorant landlord (never discloses because never documents) over the honest landlord (faces liability during the entire resolution window). PRO returned to this in A2 of Rebuttal 2 but its answer ("disclose as actively being resolved") assumed a safe harbor that no proposed threshold or mechanism established, leaving CON's version of the incentive story intact through the close.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A wins by demonstrating that the mandate's subjective thresholds ("severe", "chronic") create perverse incentives for landlords to resolve issues informally and off-paper, ultimately hurting tenants. Side B relies heavily on the moral necessity of disclosure but fails to adequately defend how landlords navigate the lack of objective thresholds without facing liability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction in Rebuttal 2 between documenting specific tenant violations for eviction versus classifying an entire building with a "chronic condition" effectively dismantled Side B's main defense against the perverse incentive argument.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): B wins by more sharply exposing the proposal's unworkable subjectivity, missing thresholds, and perverse incentives against record-keeping, which A never fully neutralized across rebuttals and closings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 on distinct documentation categories and litigation bait, directly answering Pressure Qs while crystallizing the incentive flaw.
