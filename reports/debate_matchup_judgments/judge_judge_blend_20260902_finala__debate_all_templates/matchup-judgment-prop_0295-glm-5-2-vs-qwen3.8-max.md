### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides argued the motion as written and executed the pressure-question format correctly, producing a tight, well-structured clash. PRO's core move—that CON's "voluntary, separate" model is functionally "declineable" and thus offers the receiving country no real recourse—landed hardest, especially after CON's answer to the pressure question (recourse via "reputational pressure" and "legal channels where they exist") proved vague and unenforced. PRO also successfully flipped CON's central leverage argument, arguing that withholding archives/funding is itself a continuation of colonial control, and turned CON's delay argument back on itself by noting the status quo (unconditional-or-nothing) is already glacially slow, with capacity concerns already used as a pretext for delay. CON's strongest surviving point—that funding requires audits/benchmarks so "the payer remains inside the process" even with third-party assessment—was sharp but arrived late (end of Rebuttal 2) and was only partially addressed in PRO's closing. On balance, PRO's rebuttals did more consistent damage to CON's live route (lack of enforceable recourse) than CON's rebuttals did to PRO's (residual donor involvement), giving PRO a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure question ("what recourse does the receiving country have... how is that not the thief choosing his own apology?") and CON's comparatively weak answer (separated claim, reputational pressure, legal channels "where they exist") was the most decisive exchange, exposing the enforcement gap in CON's voluntary-cooperation model.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B won by separating immediate physical return from the continuing obligation to fund repair. Side A compellingly warned that donor funding can create supervision and delay, but repeatedly treated the proposition as requiring the artifact to remain hostage until donor-defined benchmarks are met. Side B answered that the object can move immediately while recipient-defined support remains owed, fitting the motion more closely. Side A also conceded that archives, training, and assistance may be owed, leaving its voluntary, separately negotiated model vulnerable to refusal without a comparably strong remedy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response to the self-determination challenge was decisive: a claimant may demand immediate return without thereby cancelling separate debts for archives and capacity. This neutralized Side A’s hostage framing while preserving B’s core claim that shipping alone cannot let the former power close the file.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A won a competitive debate by keeping the live clash on the full harm of colonial taking—capacity, archives, and meaning—not mere possession, and by converting B's concession that archives/training matter into a demand that they be obligations, not declineable add-ons. B's leverage and delay attacks were sharp and memorable, but never fully escaped the "no recourse / thief chooses the apology" pressure; voluntary-plus-reputation was a weaker completion story than A's recipient-need funding framed as repair rather than management. Both sides stayed disciplined and progressive; A made the motion's completeness rule easier to affirm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 answer that object transfer and colonizer-debt settlement are distinct debts, so claimant urgency for the crate does not cancel archives/capacity claims, while exposing B's model as leave-it-to-goodwill.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON identified and sustained the debate's decisive structural weakness in PRO's model: the "who decides when the funding duty ends" dilemma. CON pressed this directly in Pressure Questions (B3.1) and, after PRO's answer that the recipient defines completion via "pre-agreed professional criteria," CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4) showed this dodges rather than resolves the dilemma—any dispute-resolution mechanism still implicates donor discretion, and PRO's closing never re-engaged that specific point, effectively conceding it by omission. CON also landed a sharp reframe on the causation argument, noting colonial powers often preserved objects for centuries, so deterioration risk traces to broader institutional legacies of colonialism rather than the specific act of taking—this "proves too much" critique exposed real scope-creep in PRO's repair obligation that PRO's "baseline capacity" answer only partially cabined. PRO's case (repair requires more than a handover, recipient-directed funding avoids paternalism) remained plausible and well-argued, but it left the core mechanism-design objection unresolved through to the close, giving CON the cleaner, more decisive path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4), showing PRO's "recipient defines completion" answer creates an unresolved dilemma—either the obligation has no floor or the donor retains grading power—which PRO's closing never directly answered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by tying post-return support to the specific harms of dispossession and explaining how title-first, recipient-directed, object-specific funding could preserve sovereignty. Side A created serious pressure over dependency and endpoints, but its claimed dilemma between indefinite recipient control and unilateral donor control was too absolute: finite, pre-agreed obligations provide a plausible third option. Side A also never fully answered the special relevance of archives and provenance records retained by former colonial powers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the capacity objection with “causation, not incompetence” and bounded the duty around concrete repair. Side A’s reply that all remaining needs were merely ordinary museum challenges did not adequately address withheld records or object-specific damage.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): B won by converting the stopping-point problem into a structural trap PRO never escaped: if the recipient alone defines completion the duty is open-ended; any donor input reintroduces colonial grading. B kept the clean line (title transfer finishes restitution; further support is separable aid) and used concrete counter-examples of existing capacity to blunt the causation/repair frame. A’s recipient-directed finite-repair model was coherent and anti-paternal on paper, but remained vulnerable once B showed that every grant structure still needs an approver. The live clash therefore favored B’s simpler metric of justice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4–B4.5 exposing structural incoherence of “recipient defines completion”
