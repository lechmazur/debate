### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's proposed alternative of a calibrated audit regime. A demonstrated that without consumer visibility to trigger disputes, B's "purpose limitation" relies entirely on self-certification, and that banning the domestic market effectively cuts off institutional buyers, neutralizing B's "offshore black market" threat.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Closing provided the decisive blow by pointing out that a dispute system for deleted traces is structurally impossible because the data is defined by its absence, turning B's calibrated audit into a self-certifying honor system.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by forcing and then crystallizing a structural contradiction in B's preferred alternative: purpose-limited, FCRA-style audits cannot fund a commercial deleted-trace archive through the buyers B treats as indispensable while still meaningfully constraining those same buyers, and deleted data lacks the subject visibility that makes such audits workable. B raised real costs of prohibition (investigation lag, survivors/journalists, offshore migration) and correctly refused binary purpose limits, but never neutralized A's category line (public records vs. affirmatively discarded private traces) or the invisibility/self-certification problem. A's pressure questions and later speeches narrowed the clash; B's closings recapped risks without resolving the audit paradox.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure-to-R2-to-closing sequence on the purpose-limit contradiction and the "no complainant / invisible ground truth" flaw in auditing deleted traces—the point that collapsed B's middle path as either theater or defunding the archive.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing that deleted and non-public digital traces create a structural information asymmetry that makes audit-based alternatives unworkable: citizens cannot dispute or trigger oversight of material they do not know exists. Side A offered a plausible regulatory middle ground, but Side B exposed that purpose-limitation collapses without a feedback mechanism—any regime strict enough to protect citizens would defund the archive, while one generous enough to fund it would fail to protect. Side A’s offshore-migration and accountability arguments were sharp, but Side B’s closing crystallization of the audit paradox provided the cleaner, unanswered route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s closing demonstration that audit regimes depend on data-subject visibility, which is structurally impossible with deleted traces, thereby collapsing Side A’s FCRA analogy and showing that CON’s middle ground is self-undermining.
