### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): PRO won primarily because its "no alternative mechanism" argument remained live and unanswered throughout the entire debate. CON repeatedly argued that CBAM triggers a destructive trade war, but never offered a credible alternative path to global decarbonization once CON conceded that thirty years of cooperative appeals had failed and that the EU's internal carbon price alone touches only 8% of global emissions. That gap is fatal in a debate where the motion already accepts retaliation as a given—PRO only needed to show the policy is worth the cost, and CON's case collapsed without a competing mechanism. PRO's A1 answer to CON's sharpest pressure question was genuinely effective: the lever does not require instant compliance to work; the incentive for individual firms and states to price carbon domestically persists as long as EU market access matters. CON's strongest contributions were the supply-chain timeline argument (a decade to diversify vs. immediate renewable deployment needs) and the immediate/concentrated vs. diffuse/gradual pain distinction in Rebuttal 2, which was a sharp and responsive move. CON's pressure questions were also better targeted than PRO's, exposing a real internal tension in PRO's framing of retaliation as both "manageable" and "restructuring." Those contributions kept the debate competitive. However, PRO's word-limit clipping in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing was a real execution cost that slightly dulled what would otherwise have been a cleaner closing sequence, and CON's debate showed better discipline in staying under limits. On balance, PRO's core comparative frame—trade disruption is survivable, climate collapse is not, and CON proposes nothing that changes that—was never neutralized, giving PRO the cleaner path to a verdict under the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 distinction between "immediate, concentrated economic pain" (trade war job losses) versus "diffuse and gradual" climate impacts was the sharpest exchange of the debate. It directly answered PRO's Q2 by explaining why populist backlash responds asymmetrically to visible near-term harm, threatening to unravel the domestic consensus PRO claimed the CBAM protects. PRO never fully neutralized this point—its response (climate damage is also concrete and visceral) was asserted but not developed before the closing was clipped. The exchange represents the closest CON came to turning PRO's own political-consensus argument against it.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side B won by successfully exposing the short-term physical and political constraints of Side A's policy. While Side A effectively argued that trade leverage can coexist with retaliation, Side B decisively won the supply chain clash by pointing out the 10-year gap required for diversification, during which Europe's own green transition would stall. Side B also provided a more grounded analysis of how immediate job losses would shatter domestic political consensus faster than gradual climate impacts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first answer in Rebuttal 2 (B4.1) sharply dismantled Side A's diversification argument by highlighting the 10-year gap where green deployment would stall due to retaliatory export bans.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): A won by a razor-thin margin because its core mechanism—EU market leverage creates persistent incentives for global carbon pricing even if major partners retaliate—survived B's attacks more cleanly than B's contingent causal chains withstood scrutiny. B raised real concerns about supply-chain bottlenecks and political backlash, but relied on speculative claims about rival carbon-intensive blocs and failed to offer a concrete alternative to A's "no other lever" challenge. A successfully turned B's supply-chain dependency argument into a case for urgent diversification rather than permanent vulnerability, and reframed retaliation as a survivable, bounded cost against existential climate inaction. A's clipped Rebuttal 2 and Closing were an execution cost that kept the margin narrow, but the arguments present resolved B's key pressure more decisively than B resolved A's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answers to B's Pressure Questions, particularly the lever analogy (A4.1-A4.3) and the reframing of "manageable" as survivable rather than costless (A4.4-A4.6), which cleanly resolved B's tension between manageable disruption and forced supply-chain restructuring.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): Side B edges out a clear but competitive win on the strength of two decisive moments. First, the pressure questions round: Side B's Q1 directly turned Side A's anti-dumping analogy back on itself by specifying the data infrastructure gap that makes carbon intensity verification structurally different from price comparison, exposing a real burden Side A never cleanly closed. Second, and most decisively, Side B's Rebuttal 2 introduced the efficient-vs-worst-polluter problem: default country-level rates penalize a modern low-carbon Turkish mill identically to the dirtiest facility, which means the price signal punishes accuracy rather than high emissions. This is a structural design flaw in the mechanism itself, not merely a political or diplomatic objection, and Side A never answered it beyond restating the "catalyst" framing without addressing the distributional perversity. Side A's strongest moment was the "diplomacy without leverage is just begging" framing and the coherent claim that default rates shift the auditing burden to exporters—but the counter that those defaults punish the wrong producers neutralized much of that logic. Side A also incurred a minor execution cost by misdescribing opponent reference IDs as C1.4/C1.5 rather than B1.4/B1.5. Side B's clipped closing is a modest execution cost but did not forfeit any live argument. On balance, Side B made the debate's most damaging unrebutted point and asked the sharper questions, while Side A's core leverage argument remained coherent but was left with a structural hole it could not fill.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (A1 answer): the argument that default country-average emission rates punish efficient, low-carbon producers identically to the worst polluters, meaning the mechanism rewards inaccuracy and penalizes the most climate-responsible exporters. This directly undercut Side A's "catalyst for auditing" framing and was never squarely answered in the closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): Side A won by effectively framing the debate around the necessity of economic leverage versus the failure of voluntary diplomacy. While Side B landed a strong blow against A's revenue-recycling claim and heavily pressured the technical feasibility of verifying emissions, A successfully neutralized the data objection by explaining how punitive default rates shift the burden of proof and catalyze the creation of auditing infrastructure. B struggled to present a viable alternative to A's leverage model, leaving A's core mechanism standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing response to B's pressure on data verification, explaining that default baseline rates force foreign producers to opt-in to independent auditing, turning a lack of existing data infrastructure into a catalyst for creating it.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): Side A won by maintaining a coherent case about leverage and decarbonization while executing the debate format cleanly. Side B landed strong substantive hits on retaliation asymmetry and verification complexity, but never offered a specific binding alternative to CBAM and, crucially, sidestepped Side A’s pressure questions in Rebuttal 2 rather than answering them directly with the required A1:/A2: structure. Side A’s direct answers to B’s questions, its anti-dumping analogy for verification, and its clear comparative framing (temporary friction versus permanent deindustrialization) left the cleaner path to victory. Side B’s closing being clipped for exceeding the word limit added a further execution cost that made its final crystallization incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s critique that country-level default rates punish efficient foreign producers while rewarding those who withhold data (B4.1–B4.2) was the sharpest substantive rebuttal, materially damaging A’s verification defense; however, Side A’s direct handling of pressure questions and cleaner overall execution ultimately outweighed it.
