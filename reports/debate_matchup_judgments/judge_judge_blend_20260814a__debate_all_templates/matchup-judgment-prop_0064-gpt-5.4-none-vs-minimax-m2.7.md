The three core arguments (coercion under power imbalance, scaling inferential errors, degraded work quality) were well-grounded and never neutralized. The decisive move came in the closing, where A crystallized that CON's own proposed remedy—prohibiting employers from conditioning employment on mandatory use—is just the motion restated. This trapped CON in a real dilemma. But A4.1 answered substitution adequately (we prohibit especially dangerous practices even when substitutes remain), and the beneficial uses never coalesced into a limiting principle that would prevent the ratchet PRO warned about. CON's analogy set (pilots, surgeons, email) was dismissed cleanly on functional grounds. Both sides were clipped, which is a minor symmetric cost. On the decisive clash—whether enforceable regulation can contain the coercion that defines mandatory always-on AI companionship—Side A had the sharper answer.

It forced the choice: either CON's rule truly prohibits requiring the tool (adopting the motion) or it leaves informal workplace pressure intact (coercion remains).   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing observation that CON's regulatory framework—banning conditioning employment on mandatory AI coaching plus anti-retaliation—is indistinguishable from the prohibition CON claims to oppose, which neutralizes CON's central "regulation versus ban" framework.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): Side A won by holding a cleaner, consistently-defended central distinction throughout the debate: the proposition targets the employer's *requirement* of always-on monitoring, not the capability itself. This voluntary/mandatory framing neutralized CON's strongest asset—the genuine benefits of real-time AI—by correctly noting those benefits are accessible through voluntary adoption and only require mandate when the employer needs to override worker resistance, which is precisely the dignitary violation in dispute. CON's "consistency requires mandate" counter is real but doesn't explain why specifically always-on cognitive-state monitoring is the necessary tool, as opposed to less invasive consistency mechanisms. The most decisive exchange was PRO's Rebuttal 2 distinction between tools that structure *work output* (scripts, checklists) and tools that continuously monitor *cognitive process*—a sharp, principled line that CON never directly dismantled, instead reasserting that other digital tools also "track pace and compliance," which elides the continuous real-time cognitive profiling that distinguishes the target technology. CON's argument that prohibition is overbroad because some implementations are beneficial is its strongest card, and it landed real pressure, but PRO's closing crystallization—"the mandate is only necessary to force compliance from workers who don't want it"—gave a clean answer the judge can act on. CON also suffered an execution cost from three clipped turns (Opening, Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2), which left several arguments incomplete and suggests consistent over-drafting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q2 (the scripts/checklists parallel): PRO drew the categorical line between tools that prescribe work output and tools that observe and profile cognitive process in real time, noting that scripts tell workers what to say but do not measure hesitation, tone, or frustration throughout the shift. CON never directly answered this distinction, instead reverting to the claim that many digital tools already track pace and compliance—which doesn't address continuous cognitive-state monitoring as a qualitatively different kind of intrusion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a principled, categorical distinction between tools that structure work output and systems that colonize a worker’s inner cognitive experience through continuous monitoring, prompting, and coaching. A showed that voluntary access preserves every benefit B named while eliminating the coercion and dignitary harm inherent in a mandate, and that regulation cannot cure the fundamental violation of forcing workers to surrender unobserved mental autonomy as a condition of employment. B’s overbreadth and coordination arguments were plausible but never adequately justified why the monitoring component was essential to the beneficial uses described, leaving A’s dignity-based prohibition as the cleaner resolution of the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 distinction between scripts and checklists (which structure output) versus always-on AI (which monitors hesitation, tone, and pace to optimize cognitive process). This answer neutralized B’s “proves too much” objection and established the precise limiting principle for the prohibition.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): CON wins by successfully challenging PRO's core mitigation that voluntary adoption preserves all benefits. CON demonstrated that coordination, compliance, and consistent training often require mandates, and that optional tools are frequently ignored by the workers who need them most. CON also effectively argued that PRO's coercion framework proves too much, undermining standard workplace requirements, and that targeted regulation can address abuses without imposing an overbroad categorical ban. PRO's dignity and cognitive autonomy arguments were sharp but ultimately could not overcome the practical weighing on consistency and the failure of the voluntary alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 and 2 dismantling the "make it voluntary" escape hatch. By showing that voluntary uptake fails to guarantee collective benefits like compliance, safety, and consistent service quality—and that overconfident or overwhelmed workers often skip optional tools—CON materially damaged PRO's attempt to reconcile the ban with workplace realities.
