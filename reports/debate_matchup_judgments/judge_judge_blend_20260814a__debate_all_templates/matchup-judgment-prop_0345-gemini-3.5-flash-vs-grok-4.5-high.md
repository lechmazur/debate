### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): The decisive clash was whether collaboration/culture benefits can be meaningfully sliced into role-specific justifications. B built a consistent structural argument that ambient learning, mentoring, and cross-functional alignment derive their value precisely from cutting across roles, so forcing atomized justifications doesn't refine management—it severs the mechanism that makes co-location useful. A never fully answered this; its rebuttal 2 regrouping ("junior analysts need mentoring," "product teams need alignment") still smuggled cross-cutting benefits under new labels, which B explicitly called out. More damaging, when pressed on what enforcement standard would apply, A retreated to a vague "internal accountability" answer that B correctly exposed as either toothless or indistinguishable from the "perpetual audit" A had dismissed. A's case had strong opening appeal (cost externalities, autonomy, trust) and decent rhetorical color, but it didn't resolve the core structural objection or clarify what "should have to justify" actually obligates employers to do. B's closing crystallized this gap cleanly, while A's closing largely restated its opening thesis without engaging the interdependence point. Both sides stayed within plausible, non-fabricated reasoning, but B's argument was tighter, more original, and better defended under pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.3), which answered A's pressure question by showing that ambient/cross-role benefits lose their force when atomized, then turned A's own "internal accountability" answer into an admission that the standard collapses into the very audit-style scrutiny A had earlier dismissed.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won by demonstrating that legitimate business benefits can be articulated for specific roles rather than hiding behind broad cultural assertions, and that CON's warnings of bureaucratic paralysis were overstated since employers already categorize workers by operational need. CON's interdependence argument was conceptually sharp but never proved that articulating role-specific needs would sever that web, while PRO's concrete harms of arbitrary commuting, burnout, and attrition outweighed CON's speculative compliance fears.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's reframing in Rebuttal 1 that requiring role-specific justification is standard data-driven management rather than bureaucratic paralysis, and that genuine cultural benefits like mentoring or cross-functional alignment can be mapped to specific roles and teams without losing their force.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully demonstrating that the proposition's core mechanism—role-specific justification—is fundamentally incompatible with the actual nature of workplace collaboration. Side A's argument that "ambient learning" and "cross-functional alignment" rely on interdependence and cannot be neatly atomized into individual job descriptions without losing their force was a decisive blow. Side B (PRO) relied heavily on the rhetorical trope that "if a benefit is real, it can be articulated," but failed to adequately answer Side A's proof that articulating ecosystem-wide benefits on a strict role-by-role basis creates an artificial, paralyzing compliance regime. Side A's framing of culture as the "operating system" of interdependence, combined with a solid defense of market discipline via attrition, provided a cleaner and more logically robust path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's pressure question regarding "ambient learning." Side A effectively explained that these benefits inherently cut across job titles, and forcing them into role-specific boxes severs the interdependence that makes them valuable, turning real firm-wide drivers into artificial failures under the proposition's test.
