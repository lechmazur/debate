### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins by a clear but competitive margin. Three live routes favored PRO after the full exchange. First, the chronological argument: PRO correctly pushed that if the Great Recession were the primary driver, the teen mental-health cliff would have peaked in 2009 when families were losing homes, not in 2012–2014 when smartphone adoption became near-universal. CON offered competing explanations (austerity, Common Core) but never directly refuted the 2009 vs. 2012 timing discrepancy, leaving PRO's timeline argument standing. Second, the gendered demographic point: PRO raised in Q2 that the crisis uniquely and disproportionately struck teen girls, a pattern that generic economic or academic pressure cannot neatly explain. CON never addressed this gap, leaving it as a live route for PRO. Third, the non-user challenge: CON's strongest recurring argument was that teens who avoid social media still suffer, which would defeat primacy. PRO answered credibly in Rebuttal 2 [B4.1] by arguing social media has monopolized youth socialization such that even non-users face social exclusion in a world where peer life has moved entirely online. CON's closing then repeated the non-user point as a knockout without engaging PRO's environment-monopolization response—an execution cost. CON's best contributions were the aspirin analogy for RCTs and the bidirectionality point, both effective but insufficient to neutralize PRO's cumulative case. PRO's mechanisms (developing prefrontal cortex, algorithmic exploitation, sleep deprivation, quantified social comparison) were coherent and only partially contested throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 [B4.1] transforming the non-user problem: by arguing that social media has monopolized youth socialization to the point where opting out still means social exclusion from digitized peer groups, PRO converted CON's single most powerful running argument into something that actually supports PRO's primacy claim—that social media restructured the adolescent environment itself. CON never countered this reframe in the closing, instead repeating the non-user point as though PRO had left it unanswered.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): A built the stronger case by anchoring on the early-2010s adolescent-specific spike, unique peer-comparison mechanisms, and direct RCT responses that CON never fully neutralized; CON's structural claims and non-user emphasis were addressed without being displaced.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 directly answered CON's pressure questions while sharpening the ecosystem-migration and demographic-disparity comparison.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side B established a clearer causal pathway: the early-2010s spike in teen distress tracks the rise of algorithmic, image-based platforms, and the crisis’s unique adolescent/gendered profile is better explained by developmental vulnerability to 24/7 quantified comparison than by diffuse macroeconomic stress. Side B also turned Side A’s strongest pressure—non-user suffering—into evidence that social media has monopolized youth socialization, while Side A never squarely answered why the crisis spared older demographics facing the same structural pressures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 answers (A1/A2): the ecosystem-monopolization explanation for non-users and the structural-insulation distinction for adults turned apparent falsifications into evidence for environmental transformation.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): This was a genuinely competitive exchange. PRO built a coherent, parsimonious causal story: a novel factor (social media saturation after 2012) with specific, plausible mechanisms (algorithmic amplification, sleep disruption, social comparison gamification) explains a sudden, synchronized global surge. CON's strongest contributions were the prodromal-phase argument (vulnerable teens use screens more before diagnosis, not after), the destigmatization point (increased reporting may explain apparent rate increases, not actual increases), and the Great Recession lag as an alternative inflection-point explanation. The prodromal argument was the best single move CON made, but PRO's counter in Rebuttal 2—that if reverse causality fully explained the longitudinal findings, depression should predict later social media use at the same rate, and it doesn't—was sharp and analytically sound, and CON never squarely answered it. CON's destigmatization argument was the one strong undefeated point, and PRO's failure to address it was a real cost. But CON's alternative explanation for the post-2012 inflection point (a tipping point of multiple slow-moving structural factors converging at the same moment) is less parsimonious and somewhat circular, while PRO's novel-factor account is simpler and more specific. CON's effect-size argument ("multiplying near-zero by millions is still near-zero") overstates the logic—small per-person effects across near-universal exposure are how public health crises work—and PRO correctly pointed to dose-response curves and population scale. PRO's notably shorter speeches (228, 233, 151 words vs. CON's 300, 290, 283) left the destigmatization point unanswered and created execution cost, but the core causal story and the asymmetry rebuttal survived the full exchange with more live force than CON's structural alternative, giving PRO a narrow but real edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 [B4.2] answering the self-report accuracy question: "If reporting bias were the driver, depression would predict later [social media] use equally; it doesn't." This was the sharpest methodologically focused counter in the debate—it directly attacked CON's reverse-causality story by pointing to asymmetry in prediction directionality, and CON never answered it.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A built the stronger case by anchoring on the sudden post-2012 timing and specific mechanisms (algorithmic amplification, dose-response), which CON never neutralized despite strong offline rebuttals; PRO's direct answers to pressure questions further clarified directionality over reverse causality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 and answers in Rebuttal 2 effectively isolated the novel timing and platform-specific harms against B's gradualist and prodromal explanations.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by establishing that structural offline factors provide a more coherent primary explanation for the teen mental-health crisis, while successfully undermining PRO's statistical evidence. CON's prodromal-phase critique exposed a key weakness in PRO's reliance on longitudinal studies, and CON's pressure questions on cross-national divergence and self-report data revealed genuine evidential gaps that PRO answered weakly. PRO never recovered from CON's alternative explanations—particularly destigmatization-driven reporting increases and Great Recession fallout—for the post-2012 surge, leaving CON's mirror metaphor intact: social media remains a visible stage for deeper offline wounds rather than the primary driver.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's prodromal-phase argument (A2.4/A4.2), which showed that longitudinal studies controlling for baseline mental health still miss the sub-clinical onset of distress that drives teens online before formal diagnosis, thereby severing PRO's claimed causal arrow.
