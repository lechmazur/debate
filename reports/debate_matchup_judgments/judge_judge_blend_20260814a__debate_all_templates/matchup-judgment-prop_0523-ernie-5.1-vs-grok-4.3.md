- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger cumulative case. The decisive comparison was the "policy design" argument: PRO consistently held that employment-tied, capped permits are a legislative choice, not an inevitable slide into sponsorship creep, and this directly neutralized CON's core "politically fragile caps" objection, which remained speculative throughout. CON's strongest move—the sovereignty/precedent signal argument—was genuinely live, but PRO blunted it with real-world examples (DACA dampening irregular arrivals) that CON could only answer with an unsupported "one-time amnesty" distinction and a theoretical long-run model, neither of which was grounded beyond assertion. CON's rebuttal 2 and closing were also notably shorter than the cap, leaving the fiscal-household and precedent clashes incompletely resolved. PRO answered both pressure questions directly with A1:/A2: labels and provided structural rather than evasive responses. CON's answers to PRO's Q1 and Q2 were adequate but relied on future-scenario speculation. The closing differential—PRO crystallized the shadow-economy/sovereignty inversion while CON merely restated the rotation argument—further widened the gap. Overall PRO made the easier-to-decide comparative case: conditioned status delivers measurable gains whereas enforcement delivers costs and cedes control to the black market, a frame CON never cleanly overturned.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answering A's pressure questions was the most decisive exchange. CON had asked why sponsorship caps wouldn't erode politically once status is granted. PRO responded that caps are a design choice, not an inevitability, that the fiscal incentive for legislators is precisely to preserve them, and pointed to Canada and DACA as cases where caps held. CON's counter—that residency converts workers into voters and lobbyists who expand rights over election cycles—was plausible but unsupported and speculative, leaving PRO's structural answer standing as the stronger position on that clash.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Both sides ran credible, grounded cases that genuinely engaged each other, but CON (A) emerged with a slightly stronger path to victory across the full exchange. CON's deterrence argument—that regularising existing workers signals "cross, stay, and be legalised" to future entrants—was consistently developed and never fully neutralised. PRO's best answer was the "one-time adjustment plus border enforcement for new arrivals" framing in B4.1, but this relied on a distinction between existing and future workers that PRO never operationalised convincingly; CON correctly pressed that every regularisation round advertises the same route to future cohorts. PRO's loop argument (removal recreates identical vacancies) is empirically plausible and represents the debate's most original contribution, but CON successfully weaponised it in the closing: PRO's own admission that labour demand persists regardless confirms that without credible deterrence the loop never closes. On wages, PRO's counterintuitive point (status enables open negotiation, lifting the underground floor) was genuinely interesting, but CON's pressure question—what stops firms from replacing regularised workers with fresh undocumented hires?—exposed a circularity: PRO's answer relied on border enforcement being effective at deterring new arrivals, the very mechanism PRO elsewhere characterised as futile and self-defeating. CON's rebuttal 2 and closing were more adaptive and sharper than PRO's, which in rebuttal 2 was noticeably shorter than the cap and in the closing largely recapped without meaningfully crystallising the comparative weighing. The rule-of-law and sovereignty arguments were never squarely answered by PRO, only reframed as "administrability." These gaps are enough to give CON a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question Q2 (what stops firms from replacing regularised workers with fresh undocumented hires?) and PRO's answer (fresh hires face border enforcement) was the most decisive exchange: PRO's response required treating border enforcement as effective precisely where PRO's broader case treated enforcement as costly and self-defeating, revealing an internal tension CON exploited effectively in rebuttal 2 and the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): B narrowly won by making the motion-specific case that workers already integrated into labor markets represent sunk training, community stability, tax flows, and sector capacity that stricter return enforcement would disrupt without eliminating underlying labor demand. A’s deterrence and rule-of-law framing was forceful and rhetorically sharper, especially in closing, but it relied heavily on asserting that enforcement would eventually redirect employers toward legal labor or automation. B better answered the wage-suppression point by arguing that status removes deportation leverage and improves mobility, while A’s claim that legal status would still entrench an underclass was less developed. The debate remained close because B’s answer to the “wait-and-regularize” incentive was not fully satisfying, but B’s comparative account of immediate disruption versus uncertain deterrence was slightly stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was the response that removing integrated workers recreates the same labor demand and preserves an exploitable underground workforce, whereas regularization lets workers move, report abuse, and contribute openly. A’s counter that adjustment pain is the policy working was effective, but it did not fully prove that stricter enforcement would actually solve the vacancies or wage dynamics.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by showing that labor demand in sectors like agriculture and elder care is structural, so removing integrated workers generates costly turnover and draws new irregular entrants without actually raising domestic wages or restoring bargaining power. Side A (CON) relied on abstract deterrence and rule-of-law concerns but never squarely rebutted PRO's stock-versus-flow distinction or proved that deporting settled workers would shrink future migration rather than simply replace one wave with another. PRO's wage mechanism—regularized workers gaining mobility and bargaining power—also survived CON's pressure more cleanly than CON's "pain is the policy working" response survived PRO's challenge about persistent vacancies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over the "loop" of removal and replacement, where B demonstrated that enforcing return on integrated workers perpetuates underground labor conditions and recurrent enforcement costs without restoring domestic worker bargaining power, while A's counter that enforcement raises long-run hiring costs remained more theoretical and conceded transitional pain.
