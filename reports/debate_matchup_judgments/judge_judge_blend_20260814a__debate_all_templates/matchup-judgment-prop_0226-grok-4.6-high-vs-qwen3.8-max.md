### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides argued with sophistication and stayed grounded, but CON's administrability critique proved more durable. CON consistently forced PRO to specify how a "limited right" would actually function, and PRO's answers to the pressure questions (shared queues, residual intake, lawful stay when caps fill) ended up converging with CON's own discretionary framework. CON exploited this convergence directly in Rebuttal 2 ("precisely the outcome PRO attacks in our system... except now with litigation"), turning PRO's own concessions into the debate's most damaging moment. PRO's closing reasserted an "accountable right vs. unaccountable mercy" framing but never explained what concrete difference the "right" label makes once caps, queues, and receiving-state discretion are conceded on both sides. CON also never let go of the heat-threshold administrability problem (officials judging "ordinary adaptation" as a proxy for foreign infrastructure adequacy), which PRO narrowed but did not fully resolve. CON's closing cleanly reframed the vote as "not whether protection is deserved but whether PRO's right can work as promised," which tracked the actual unresolved clash better than PRO's closing did.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that PRO's own answer to the "quota full" pressure question (shared queue, lawful stay) is functionally identical to CON's discretionary system, reducing PRO's "right" to "coordinated discretion with a courtroom attached."
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A narrowly won by showing that CON's alternatives still require inhabitability findings, queues, and durable protection, but leave protection discretionary and revocable. A also gave a coherent answer to the caps objection: the enforceable right concerns recognized status, lawful protection, and non-return, not unlimited admission to a claimant's chosen country. B exposed real administrative difficulty, especially for heat claims, but did not establish why those difficulties justify withholding a legal duty when its own model makes many of the same judgments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest turn was converting B's concession that people should not be returned and may receive permanent protection into the central comparison: both models draw difficult lines and manage quotas, but only A's supplies an accountable legal claim. B's reply that a protected queue is merely discretion did not fully answer the difference between enforceable lawful status and revocable humanitarian favor.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON successfully exposed a fatal tension in PRO's case: a genuine legal right to asylum cannot coexist with the caps and shared regional queues PRO needed to prevent uncontrolled migration. PRO's "right" dissolved into what CON termed "coordinated discretion with a courtroom attached"—an aspirational label rather than an enforceable entitlement. While PRO's moral framing was powerful and the closing crystallization sharp, CON's first rebuttal established that climate causation requires impossible counterfactual modeling, and their second rebuttal showed that PRO's adaptation standards and burden-sharing mechanism re-created the very discretion PRO criticized. The live route to victory—PRO's inability to make the right simultaneously limited, administrable, and meaningful—was never neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal (A2) introduced the counterfactual causation problem and the rights-versus-caps contradiction, generating live pressure that PRO's shared-queue and survivability-line answers never fully resolved.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and handled the Pressure Questions format cleanly, but CON's central attack—that any workable threshold ("unsafe to remain," "unreasonable relocation") collapses into an unadjudicable gradient—survived the exchange better than PRO's defenses. When pressed for a cutoff (B3.2), PRO's answer ("ordinary life unsustainable even with reasonable adaptation") reintroduced the same vague language CON had already targeted, and CON's R2 explicitly caught this ("the same gradient they denied"), a point PRO's closing did not really neutralize. CON also offered a concrete alternative for PRO's strongest example (the sinking atoll) via nationality/succession law and planned relocation, directly undercutting PRO's signature case rather than just gesturing at "bargains." PRO's quota/rights tension (A4.1) was also left exposed—CON's closing observation that a right which "shifts" when quotas are full is "political assignment, not a justiciable shield" went essentially unanswered. PRO's torture-prediction analogy was a strong move, but CON's reply—that persecution asks about a discrete prohibited actor's conduct while climate harm is a continuum—was a sharper distinction that reframed the comparison in CON's favor. Overall CON did more to sharpen and resolve the decisive clash over adjudicability, giving it a clear, if not dominant, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's R2 response to PRO's tightened cutoff (A4.3), showing that "unsustainable even with reasonable adaptation" reproduces the exact vagueness problem PRO claimed to have solved, combined with the closing point that quota-based rotation of "successful claimants" reveals the right is really political allocation rather than a justiciable shield.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): A won by showing that indisputable cases such as permanent submersion create a protection gap that discretionary bargains cannot reliably fill. B sharply exposed ambiguity in habitability thresholds and allocation, but its claim that gradients are inherently unadjudicable was too absolute: A supplied objective indicators, internal-relocation limits, and a plausible non-refoulement model. B never established that imperfect borderline cases outweigh legal protection for the clear core cases.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest response compared climate adjudication with asylum's existing mixed motives and uncertain forecasts. B's actor-versus-gradient distinction was memorable, but it did not defeat A's point that courts routinely assess reasonableness and evidence, especially for irreversible inundation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by showing that a limited right could be bounded to objective, verifiable collapses of basic habitability—permanent submersion, chronic loss of fresh water, heat incompatible with ordinary residence—and that this offered surer protection for the hardest cases than Side A's reliance on pre-collapse bargains and state succession. Side A's slippery-slope argument remained forceful but never fully overcame PRO's bright-line physical thresholds or responsibility-sharing mechanism, while PRO effectively exposed that CON's alternative left the central atoll case unprotected when politics failed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's argument that documented physical inundation is more legally manageable than forecasting future persecution, coupled with the point that a separate climate channel protects the Refugee Convention by preventing doctrine-stretching, decisively shifted the adjudicability clash in B's favor.
