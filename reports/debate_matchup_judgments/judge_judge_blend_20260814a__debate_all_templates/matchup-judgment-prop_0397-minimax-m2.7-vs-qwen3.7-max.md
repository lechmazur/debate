### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won by constructing and consistently defending a logical trap that Side B never escaped: B cannot simultaneously claim that targeted programs (TPD, IDR) adequately protect vulnerable borrowers AND that those same vulnerable borrowers will flood bankruptcy courts under easier access. Either the programs work—in which case the "able-bodied strategic defaulters" scenario carries the weight of the CON case—or they don't, which collapses the "fix the bureaucracy" solution B repeatedly offered. A pressed this contradiction through pressure questions and both rebuttals, and B's responses (primarily asserting that lowering the bar invites mathematical moral hazard) never squarely answered why the "long-term inability to repay" qualifier in the very proposition would not screen out the strategic defaulters B feared. B's core "inseparable asset" argument was challenged credibly via the failed entrepreneur analogy, and B's response—that human capital remains intact unlike liquidated business assets—was logically distinct but never resolved A's deeper point about asymmetric information and uncertain returns at signing. The "sledgehammer to fix a watch" line was rhetorically effective but used multiple times, becoming repetitive rather than progressive. A's multiple speech clippings are a real execution cost, but the arguments visible in each clipped speech were strong enough in direction that the pattern held. B's case was competent and internally consistent on the lending-system-harm thread, but that thread was speculative in ways A correctly identified, and B never grappled with the proposition's built-in limiting criterion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure questions—particularly Q2 in Round 5—exposed the central contradiction: if defaults are dominated by the disabled, chronically ill, and low-wage workers B claims are already protected by targeted programs, then B's moral hazard scenario requires a population that, by B's own account, shouldn't exist. B's A2 response in Rebuttal 2 simply reasserted that lowering the bar invites future exploitation without resolving this internal contradiction, which left A's logical trap standing through the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): A won because it kept the debate tied to the motion's limiting phrase: borrowers facing long-term inability to repay. B's strongest offense about moral hazard and future lending restrictions depended on treating the proposal as broad easy discharge for disappointed able-bodied graduates, but A repeatedly framed bankruptcy as a capacity-to-pay backstop and showed why existing targeted programs are not enough. B had a real systemic-cost argument, and A's clipped turns weakened its execution, especially on future access, but B never sufficiently answered why courts could not distinguish genuine long-term inability from strategic default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's most decisive exchange was its answer that judges need not rate majors or degree value; they can assess actual repayment capacity, which directly undercut B's claim that easier discharge becomes a subjective penalty for low-earning credentials.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A exposed a fatal tension in Side B's case: B simultaneously claimed that targeted relief already protects vulnerable borrowers while warning that easier bankruptcy would unleash mass strategic default by able-bodied borrowers. A showed that current defaults are concentrated among the very populations B said were protected, making B's moral hazard scenario speculative and unsupported. A also cleanly rebutted B's "degree as inseparable asset" analogy and demonstrated that bankruptcy courts evaluate financial capacity, not the wisdom of one's major. Side B never resolved why administrative fixes would succeed after years of documented failure, and A's closing crystallized the contradiction in a memorable, decisive frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Pressure Questions exposing that Side B cannot consistently argue both that targeted relief works for the vulnerable and that easier bankruptcy would flood courts with strategic able-bodied defaulters, since the default data shows the harmed population is exactly the one B claims is already protected.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): PRO (Side A) wins on a combination of substantive performance and execution discipline. The single most decisive moment was PRO's Q1, pointing out that the vast majority of student loans carry federally-set statutory interest rates, not market-priced rates. This directly undermined CON's central economic mechanism—that easier discharge forces lenders to raise rates—and CON never adequately answered it, pivoting instead in A1 to litigation-complexity concerns. That pivot, while not worthless, is a dodge on the original pressure. PRO also built a durable framing in the opening and first rebuttal: bankruptcy's brutal friction (decade of credit destruction, public record, asset loss) already neutralizes most moral hazard, so the population of genuine abusers is small and identifiable, while the population of genuinely ruined borrowers is real and present. CON's strongest sustained argument—"fix IDR/PSLF directly rather than destabilize credit markets"—was countered effectively by PRO's point that Congress has had decades to fix those programs and failed, making them inadequate as substitutes for a functioning legal backstop. CON never closed that gap. CON also suffered two execution penalties: Rebuttal 2 and the Closing were both clipped for exceeding the word limit, leaving CON's final arguments incomplete and reducing their impact. PRO correctly used A1:/A2: format; CON used it as well but the clipping cut off the closing argument before it could land. On balance, PRO answered more of the live pressure more directly, landed the most damaging single pressure question, and delivered complete speeches throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 in the Pressure Questions round—pointing out that federal student loan rates are set by Congress by statute, not by market risk pricing—was the most decisive exchange. This undercut CON's core causal chain (easier discharge → lenders raise rates) for the dominant share of the loan market. CON's A1 in Rebuttal 2 pivoted to litigation complexity without ever restoring the causal link, leaving PRO's attack live through the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): A won by keeping the motion focused on a narrow backstop for borrowers with long-term inability to repay and by making bankruptcy friction a credible answer to moral hazard. B had a real taxpayer and future-borrower story, but its central rate-repricing claim was badly weakened by A's federal-loan question, which B never directly answered despite the required A1/A2 structure. B's targeted-IDR alternative was plausible but too speculative against A's point that bankruptcy is the universal final safety valve for unpayable debt.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's federal-rates pressure and follow-up were the decisive exchange: it exposed that B's interest-rate impact did not fit the federal loan system, and B largely dodged the question while continuing to rely on repricing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): Side A won because it neutralized CON's moral hazard argument by distinguishing federal statutory rates from private market pricing, and dismantled the "fix IDR" alternative as a legislative fantasy given decades of bureaucratic failure. This left CON defending hypothetical future harms against PRO's concrete, grounded case about current borrower suffering. Side B also incurred execution costs by having two speeches clipped and by evading the direct pressure on federal loan pricing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal distinguishing federal statutory rates from market moral hazard, paired with the "legislative fantasy" framing of CON's IDR alternative, defanged CON's core mechanism and primary counter-solution.
