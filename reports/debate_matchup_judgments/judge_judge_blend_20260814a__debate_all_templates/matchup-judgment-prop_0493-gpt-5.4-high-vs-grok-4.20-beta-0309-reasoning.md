### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON)): Side A built a durable structural case around two linked arguments that Side B never fully neutralized. First, aquifer damage is lagged, which means a perfectly legal permit can overestimate recharge and deliver harm before adjustment is possible; the community bears the cost while the firm bears only downstream revenue loss—market discipline punishes damage after damage occurs. Second, and more decisively, A introduced the source-control-versus-delivery distinction: nothing about emergency bottled supply or consumer backup requires granting private corporations ownership, trade, or extraction rights over municipal aquifers; governments can contract delivery as a service without privatizing the source. This reframe, first introduced cleanly in A2.6 and sharpened in A4.2–A4.3, was never squarely answered by B. B's consistent counter—that public agencies fail under voter pressure too—was real but worked only as a tie at best, and A responded effectively that public error is possible while private commercial rights actively entrench error through sunk investments and contractual resistance. B's "brand collapse and multi-year lease" stewardship argument suffered from the exact timing problem A identified: the market penalty arrives after the ecological damage, not before it. B also never escaped the priority problem: commercial bottling is a convenience product, and profitability does not convert it into a high-priority claim on a survival resource. A's closings were sharper, posed a cleaner decision rule, and crystallized the key asymmetry—replaceable jobs and revenue on one side, irreplaceable aquifer resilience on the other. B's closing was competent but largely recapped existing ground without resolving the key burdens against it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answering B's pressure question about government innovation (A4.2–A4.3): "Nothing turns on government 'innovating and scaling faster'… the point is simpler: emergency bottled supply does not require giving corporations ownership, trade, or extraction rights over municipal sources… CON keeps conflating private help in delivery with private control of the source itself." This was the sharpest single exchange, exposing that B had been arguing against a strawman throughout and never forcing A to defend the claim that public agencies must outperform private ones on every metric—only that governments need not cede source rights to get delivery benefits.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully distinguishing between physical risk (borne by the community) and financial risk (borne by the company), showing that market incentives only punish damage after the community has already suffered. Side B also effectively neutralized Side A's emergency supply argument by separating the contracting of packaging services from the granting of resource extraction rights.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly neutralized the "market discipline" argument by pointing out that because aquifer damage is lagged, market discipline only punishes the company after the physical damage to the community's water supply is already done.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON)): Side A won by showing that under uncertainty with lagged, hard-to-reverse aquifer damage, adding private extraction rights for a nonessential commercial use creates entrenchment that democratic public control avoids. Side B's symmetry argument—that public agencies also misallocate—was blunted by Side A's distinction between democratic correctability and contractual lock-in, and by the asymmetry that market discipline arrives only after community harm. Side B never adequately answered why private extraction and ownership of the source (as opposed to private bottling services under public procurement) were necessary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (Turn 7), which separated private delivery services from private control of the source and argued that commercial rights harden hydrological errors into sunk-cost entrenchment, while public agencies can reprioritize residents because their mandate is water security rather than saleable volume.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON)): Side A won by cleanly isolating the core asymmetry: lagged aquifer damage means harm occurs before market or regulatory corrections trigger, and private extraction rights entrench overuse through contracts, sunk costs, and political resistance to cuts. A consistently separated source ownership from delivery services, neutralizing CON's emergency and innovation claims. CON relied heavily on faith in dynamic permits and market penalties but never squared how brand risk or bonds prevent diffuse, delayed ecological harm, nor why private pumping rights wouldn't face the same political entrenchment. A's weighing of replaceable commercial convenience against irreplaceable water security was sharper, more responsive to pressure, and provided a cleaner voting issue.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1/A4.2 directly answering B's pressure questions. A conceded public fallibility but proved private rights structurally entrench error via contractual and political resistance to cuts, while cleanly showing emergency supply needs service contracts, not source privatization. This dismantled CON's symmetry argument and locked in the priority clash.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): CON (Side B) wins on three interconnected live routes that PRO never cleanly neutralized. First, the overbreadth problem: the motion bans every possible commercial arrangement with any municipal water source, including revocable, recharge-capped, drought-triggered permits in water-abundant areas where resident supply is fully protected. PRO's only limiting principle (A4.1) was essentially "no corporate involvement, ever," which confirmed rather than refuted CON's absolutism charge. Second, the enforcement paradox: PRO's central argument is that regulatory institutions are too weak to hold firms accountable, but CON (B2.1, B4.1, B5.2) correctly noted that a ban still requires definitions, monitoring, detection of illegal pumping, and resistance to lobbying for exemptions. PRO's response (A4.2) that violations become "clear-cut" under a ban was directly challenged (B4.4), and the claim was never adequately supported—aquifers do not self-report, and prosecution still requires institutional capacity. Third, PRO mischaracterized B2.1 as a concession of institutional weakness throughout, including in the closing (A5.1), even after CON explicitly corrected this as a conditional argument (B4.1). That repeated mischaracterization is a credibility cost. PRO's public-trust framing and the profit-motive corruption argument had genuine moral weight and kept the debate competitive, but CON's targeted regulation alternative consistently offered a more precise fit to the actual harm—over-extraction and loss of public priority—without the collateral damage of prohibiting safe, reversible arrangements. CON also effectively flipped PRO's closing line: "prohibition without governance is theater" answered "regulation without prohibition is theater" directly and memorably.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's enforcement paradox (B2.1, reinforced in B4.1 and B4.4): if institutions are too weak to enforce metered caps and permit revocations, they are equally unable to enforce a blanket ban against illegal pumping, exemptions, and redefinitions. PRO's answer—that a ban makes violations "clear-cut"—was rebutted sharply (B4.4) by noting aquifers still need active detection, measurement, and prosecution. PRO never recovered a credible response, leaving this asymmetry unresolved in CON's favor.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO)): Side A wins by cleanly exposing a contradiction in Side B's core logic: if governments are too weak or corrupt to regulate corporate extraction, they are also too weak to enforce a blanket ban against lobbying, exemptions, or illegal pumping. Side A successfully defends targeted regulation as a more practical, nuanced approach that preserves democratic choice and local revenue in safe, water-abundant areas.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 decisively flipped Side B's institutional failure argument by pointing out that policing a prohibition requires the same governmental fortitude as policing a metered cap.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): CON successfully demonstrated that the blanket ban is overbroad and does not solve the institutional weakness PRO relies upon. PRO never adequately answered why prohibition is easier to enforce than permits if detection and political pressure remain problems, nor justified banning clearly safe, reversible arrangements in water-abundant areas. CON's reversal—that prohibition without governance is theater—sharpened the decisive clash and left PRO's inevitability claim about corporate capture looking like unsupported absolutism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's institutional weakness turn showing that if governments are too weak to enforce caps and permits, they are also too weak to police an outright ban, police exemptions, and detect illegal pumping—making the ban theater rather than a cure.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): CON wins by successfully exploiting the overbreadth of the blanket ban and landing a decisive enforcement symmetry argument. When PRO claimed regulation fails due to corporate lobbying and institutional weakness, CON turned this by noting that a ban requires the exact same institutional capacity to monitor, detect illegal pumping, and resist political carve-outs. PRO’s response that a ban is “easier to enforce” because violations are “clear-cut” was logically thin and never fully resolved CON’s point that aquifers don’t police themselves. CON also consistently pressed PRO on a limiting principle for water-abundant regions with strict, revocable permits, forcing PRO into an absolutist stance that CON effectively weighed as “symbolic” versus “precise public control.” CON’s comparative framing—banning the harm (over-extraction) rather than every supervised tool—provided a cleaner, more responsive path to victory on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO’s enforcement question [B4.1-B4.4], which dismantled the claim that prohibition is easier to police than metered permits. CON correctly noted that illegal pumping still requires detection, measurement, and prosecution, and that if institutions are too captured or weak to enforce caps, they will also fail to enforce a ban. This turned PRO’s core institutional critique into a liability for the prohibition side and crystallized the decisive clash.
