### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate. Three decisive advantages: (1) PRO consistently held CON to the correct scope of the motion—a guaranteed floor with private markets above it, not a state monopoly—and CON never fully escaped the trap of arguing against something the motion does not propose; (2) PRO's reframe that "price rationing is a waitlist too, just without an honest queue" was the most damaging single move in the debate, materially weakening CON's central wait-time argument because it revealed that CON's alternative imposes delay without the possibility of triage or improvement; (3) CON's substitute (HSAs, sliding-scale clinics, targeted subsidies) was never shown to constitute a guarantee, which is the core word in the motion, and PRO's Q1 directly exposed this gap while CON's Rebuttal 2 answer (A1) still relied on multiple paths rather than a guarantee. CON's "definition creep" argument was interesting but repeatedly framed with overconfident universals ("inevitably," "always," "every established system," "never successfully implemented") that PRO correctly challenged as requiring impossibility rather than contingent political risk. The 40% same-week statistic was also repeated multiple times with brittle specificity that felt unverifiable, reducing its rhetorical authority over the course of the debate. CON's closings called the proposition "a lie" and "a scam" without adding new argumentative force, while PRO's closing cleanly crystallized the front-door framing. PRO answered the A1/A2 pressure correctly and more honestly, acknowledging definition-creep risk while rebutting its determinism. Overall, PRO consistently made the debate easier to decide in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to the wait-time objection in Rebuttal 1—"price rationing is a waitlist too; it just has no honest queue"—was the most decisive exchange. It reframed CON's strongest empirical point (queue length) by showing that delaying care until payday or until symptoms worsen is itself a form of harmful rationing, one that is invisible in CON's accounting. CON never cleanly answered this comparison in either subsequent speech, leaving the queue-vs.-price clash resolved in PRO's favor.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): B wins by maintaining the motion's limited scope, directly answering pressure questions on delay metrics and institutional rules, and exposing that CON's patchwork lacks any guarantee while price barriers remain the core failure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2 rebuttal 2 (A1/A2 answers) neutralizes inevitability claim and reframes wait-time comparison around net harmful delay for high-risk patients.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing price barriers as a more dangerous form of rationing than managed waitlists and by effectively using the motion's wording ("some elective services") to neutralize CON's definition creep argument. CON's alternatives (HSAs, sliding-scale) failed to meet the "guarantee" burden, leaving a critical gap in access for those facing financial uncertainty. CON also over-relied on a repeatedly cited 40% waitlist statistic that felt brittle, while A maintained stronger epistemic discipline and clearer comparative weighing throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the wait-time pressure, distinguishing between a triaged queue for minor issues and the dangerous, open-ended delay caused by cost barriers, which decisively turned CON's central access argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO opened more strongly with concrete equity framing and plausible cost-shift logic, and the "false perfection test" counter was a real asset throughout. However, CON built a decisive analytical edge in the middle and late rounds. The sharpest move in the debate was CON's Rebuttal 2 "trapped" argument: PRO either accepts the motion as written (guaranteeing only the front door while supplementary services stay private, leaving the poor unable to afford the follow-up imaging, specialists, and medicines that make the visit meaningful) or they rewrite the motion into a much larger public package whose cost and delivery they never adequately defended. PRO's answer—that universal systems typically bundle essential follow-up or offer income subsidies—was precisely the expansion CON identified, and PRO never acknowledged the logical move they were making. CON's pressure question on imaging and specialist follow-up was more precise than PRO's K-12 analogy question, and CON's answer to the K-12 question (you can build classrooms faster than you can train doctors) was cleaner than PRO's answer to the imaging/follow-up question. CON's closing crystallized the trapped argument effectively. PRO's closing repeated familiar framing without escaping the dilemma. Both Rebuttal 1s were clipped equally, so no differential penalty there. The edge belongs to CON for analytical precision, the originality of the trapped-argument structure, and sharper progression across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 "trapped" argument: PRO must either keep the motion narrow—guaranteeing only the first appointment while wealth still buys timely follow-up—making the guarantee mostly universal diagnosis rather than universal care; or PRO must expand the policy into a much larger public package covering imaging, specialist referral, and medicines, at which point the limited-compromise framing they sold collapses. PRO's response ("most systems bundle essential follow-up") confirmed the expansion without acknowledging it, allowing CON to close by showing that PRO's case only works by quietly rewriting the motion.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by consistently pressing the exact scope of the motion and exposing that PRO's responses required expanding the guarantee beyond primary care alone, while A's targeted-capacity approach remained internally coherent and better answered live pressure on feasibility and follow-up access.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.2 and A4.3 directly neutralized B's waitlist and two-tier defenses by forcing the concession that meaningful access still depends on supplementary services, turning PRO's own answers against the narrow motion.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully framing the proposition's compromise as structurally flawed: guaranteeing only the "front door" of primary care while leaving downstream services private creates a system where wealth still dictates health outcomes. When Side A answered pressure by suggesting essential follow-up would be bundled or subsidized, Side B trapped them, arguing this quietly rewrites the motion's limited scope and breaks the cost-saving narrative. Side A's fallback that incomplete access is "better than nothing" conceded the guarantee's hollowness under the motion's exact terms. Side B's capacity-first framing and sharper weighing on the diagnosis-versus-treatment pathway provided a cleaner, more decisive reason to vote CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 dismantled Side A's K-12 analogy by contrasting predictable schooling with scarce clinical supply, then set a decisive strategic trap: keep the motion narrow and the guarantee is hollow ("universal diagnosis"), or expand it to make care usable and abandon the motion's compromise and fiscal claims. This framing controlled the late-round clash and forced Side A into a defensive concession.
