### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON sustained the decisive economic clash more effectively. PRO's core claim—that day-and-date release creates a new, additive revenue pool rather than cannibalizing existing ticket sales—was never fully secured against CON's two sharpest pressure points: (1) the revealed-preference argument that if simultaneous release were genuinely revenue-positive for tentpoles, studios would already be doing it broadly, and (2) the reductio that standard staggered release windows (books, albums, international rollouts) are not normally treated as discrimination, which undercuts PRO's "cooling equals exclusion" framing. PRO's answers relied on asserting qualitative differentiation between theatrical and home experiences, but never explained why that differentiation wouldn't also apply today under hybrid models, nor addressed why studios haven't already captured the "expanded base" PRO describes. CON also successfully turned PRO's own accessibility promise into a liability, arguing that the funding for "robust" home accessibility is itself dependent on the theatrical revenue PRO's policy would erode—a self-undermining loop PRO acknowledged but did not convincingly resolve. PRO's equity framing remained emotionally compelling throughout, but the debate's live economic crux tilted toward CON by the end, and PRO's closing recapped rather than repaired the exposed gaps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.3 "if day-and-date were genuinely more profitable, every blockbuster would already open that way" combined with the B4.3 staggered-release reductio was the most damaging unresolved thread; PRO's segmentation/differentiation answer in A4.1–A4.3 addressed the surface claim but never explained the industry's revealed behavior or fully rebutted the reductio.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): B won the central economic clash. A established genuine accessibility and participation harms, but its claim that simultaneous release adds a separate pool without meaningful cannibalization rested on overly rigid audience segmentation. B directly explained why convenient home access could draw ordinary theatergoers downward in price while many excluded viewers might still wait for cheaper tiers. A never adequately resolved that revenue tradeoff, and its nonexistent [C3.1] reference was an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal most decisively answered A’s segmentation mechanism: the newly accessible audience need not pay premium prices, while substitution is not confined to people already unable to attend theaters. That undermined A’s claim that access and total revenue necessarily rise together.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won the economic clash that decides the motion. PRO claimed simultaneous release expands the pie by converting never-theater viewers, but CON sharply showed [A2.1][A4.1] those groups are price-sensitive and won't pay peak VOD rates, and that regulars defect when convenience dominates, turning premium tickets into cheaper streams - headcount != revenue. PRO's answers asserted segmentation via immersion but never explained why mass defection wouldn't happen, and cited nonexistent [C3.1, C2.2] IDs. CON also answered both pressure questions directly with a workable 30-45 day standard and reductio, while PRO left the cannibalization mechanism unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1/A4.1 - price-sensitive locked-out groups won't pay theatrical-equivalent VOD and regular moviegoers substitute down, so PRO's expanded base is a discount transfer not net expansion, undermining both revenue and the accessibility funding PRO treats as automatic.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and answered pressure questions in the required A1/A2 format, but B built the more durable economic mechanism. When A pressed "if the theatrical experience is genuinely superior it should survive consumer choice," B did not merely concede but explained a coordination/collective-action logic (scarcity is what converts private convenience into event-scale demand), which is a more sophisticated answer than A's counter-framing of "coercion." B also landed a decisive turn that A never fully answered: a collapsed theatrical pipeline shrinks the overall slate and screen count for everyone, including the very disabled, rural, and family audiences A claims to champion — directly undermining A's core beneficiary argument. A's accessibility case (delay as exclusion from the cultural moment) remained emotionally resonant and under-rebutted in isolation, but A's supporting evidence (claiming "hybrid era" blockbuster records prove day-and-date sustainability) was shakier grounding, since most box-office record holders relied on traditional windowing rather than true simultaneous release, and B's rebuttal that these examples "free-rode on residual exclusivity" went largely unanswered. The result is a close but real edge to B on argument depth and rebuttal resolution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's public-goods/coordination-problem answer to A's Q1 ("If the big screen is truly superior it should survive on merit") plus B's turn that a collapsed theatrical pipeline harms the same disabled/rural/family audiences A invokes was the most decisive exchange, since A's closing never squarely rebutted the "thinner slate hurts everyone" claim.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by better explaining why simultaneous availability can cannibalize theatrical demand even when theaters retain distinctive value. A established serious accessibility harms and offered memorable framing, but repeatedly assumed home purchases would be net-new revenue and never adequately resolved substitution, lost event urgency, or financing risk. B directly weighed temporary delayed access against a potentially thinner long-term film market.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer that theatrical value depends partly on coordinated urgency, not merely intrinsic spectacle, effectively answered A’s central claim that theaters should survive unaided if they are genuinely valuable. This exposed the weakness in A’s repeated “earn the audience” framing and brought the debate back to aggregate financing rather than individual consumer preference.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO (B) kept the decisive clash alive after strong CON push. CON's best move was collective-good/scarcity and selective-vs-default free-riding to damage hybrid examples. But PRO's accessibility route to victory was never neutralized: patchwork theater devices vs reliable digital tracks, and segregation-by-delay where disabled viewers miss the live conversation, directly answers the proposition's accessibility language. CON's A2 on disabled viewers - better standards + later access - failed B's Q2 pressure, while conceding via A4.1/A4.3 that theatrical demand collapses when given choice, which PRO successfully framed as gatekeeping, not art-protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Exchange around [A2.4]/[A3.2]/[B4.2]: CON argued full captions arrive after the run and theaters can be improved; PRO answered that by then memes/spoilers/discourse have moved, so delay equals segregation, and equal cultural participation requires synchronous home access - leaving CON's inclusion alternative weak.
