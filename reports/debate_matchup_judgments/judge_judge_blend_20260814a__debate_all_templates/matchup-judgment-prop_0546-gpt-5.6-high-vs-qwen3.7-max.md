CON largely repeated assertions about structural dependency and distrust across rounds without adding new mechanistic force, while A's rebuttals sharpened the institutional distinctions between visible, costly, collective retaliation versus quiet private capture. Both sides executed the pressure-question format correctly with direct A1/A2 answers, but A's answers more precisely dismantled the "inevitable capture" framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing rebuttal exposing CON's "honest void" claim as incoherent—arguing citizens cannot seek out reporting they don't know is missing, and that unwatched corruption needs no conspiracy to stay hidden—decisively undercut CON's core baseline comparison, which CON never repaired.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing successfully dismantled Side A's 'honest void' argument by noting that citizens cannot seek reporting they do not know is missing, and that any flawed investigation still raises the costs of corruption far more than no investigation at all.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): PRO built a plausible causal story (news deserts → polarization/corruption) and repeatedly invoked analogies (public defenders, universities, public broadcasting) to argue firewalls make capture "highly mitigated." But CON's rebuttals systematically dismantled those analogies (public defender funding follows caseload not the investigated entity; universities have diversified revenue and broader missions), and CON introduced a decisive mechanistic point that PRO never fully neutralized: capture need not involve explicit interference—appointment power, eligibility definitions, and renewal cycles create "soft capture" and agenda-setting/omission effects that are harder to detect than commercial bias. CON also correctly reframed the burden: proving the market is broken does not prove this specific remedy produces net benefits, since funding buys labor/hours but not courage, adversarial willingness, or public trust. PRO's answers to the pressure questions (statutory locks, independent trusts) were answered but CON's second rebuttal showed those are labels rather than enforcement, a point PRO's closing did not directly rebut—PRO mostly reasserted "mitigated risk vs certain harm" without engaging the enforcement critique. CON's case therefore tracked the actual comparative wording of the motion more precisely and left PRO's central mechanism more exposed at the end of the exchange. Both sides suffered minor execution costs from clipped speeches, but the substantive clash favors CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3-B4.5), arguing that statutory firewalls are enforcement labels rather than solutions—since appointment power, eligibility redefinition, and renewal cycles remain politically controlled—decisively undercut PRO's public defender/university analogies and was never adequately answered in PRO's closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A won by successfully proving that the structural conflict of interest in public funding (the watchdog being funded by the watched) cannot be solved by statutory firewalls, which are vulnerable at renewal. Side A also cleanly delineated why Side B's analogies to public defenders and universities failed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's breakdown of the public defender and university analogies in Rebuttal 1, noting that neither institution is primarily tasked with independently setting an investigative agenda against the very coalition that funds them.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON won the comparative weighing. PRO relied on certainty of news-desert harms and asserted a mechanical link from funding to less corruption/polarization via firewall trusts modeled on public broadcasters/defenders. CON directly undercut that link: blind formulas require political choices over eligibility, metrics, appointments, and renewals; anticipatory omission is harder to detect than commercial bias; benefits need a long chain (fair allocation → adversarial use → trust → behavior change) while capture needs one pressure point. CON also answered PRO's pressure for alternatives with diversified non-state models and showed PRO conflates diagnosis with cure. Clipping hurt A's execution but not the core defeat of PRO's net-benefit claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 counter to firewall enforceability and benefit-chain asymmetry – statutes can be amended/expiration-squeezed, retaliation bans don't prove intent, and safe civic summaries don't equal courage/trust – neutralized PRO's institutional analogies and swung the net-benefit comparison.
