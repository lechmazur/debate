### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won because it kept the debate centered on the motion: producers design and profit from packaging, so making them bear end-of-life costs creates fairer and stronger incentives than leaving municipalities and taxpayers to clean up. Side B raised good concerns about pass-through, regressivity, and infrastructure bottlenecks, but leaned too heavily on the claim that any producer cost simply becomes a consumer tax, which does not fully answer A's internalization and design-incentive case. A also better exposed the tension in B's argument that fees are both too small to change producer behavior and large enough to seriously burden consumers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question on the fee contradiction was the most decisive exchange: B's answer that fees are marginal per unit but massive in aggregate only partially resolved the problem, while A used the point to preserve its core claim that cost signals can change producer incentives.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A wins because its rebuttals and answers to pressure questions more effectively exposed EPR's regressive price pass-through under monopoly conditions and the persistent infrastructure gap, neutralizing B's incentive claims while preserving the democratic-accountability contrast.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's direct answers on aggregate fees and failed demand creation damaged B's market-reshaping route most cleanly.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A wins because its core framework—aligning packaging design power with financial responsibility—remained live after the exchange. While Side B landed sharp blows on consumer pass-through and infrastructure bottlenecks, it never fully neutralized the market-failure diagnosis at the heart of the proposition. Side A effectively showed that the status quo forces taxpayers to subsidize corporate externalities with no leverage over design, and that well-structured EPR creates direct incentives for sustainable innovation. Side B’s alternative of municipal control was less developed, and its reliance on market consolidation to nullify consumer choice, while strong, did not squarely defeat the comparative fairness and efficiency case for producer responsibility.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s pressure question challenging what cheaper alternatives low-income consumers could actually buy under monopoly pricing (B3.1), and its sustained counter that producer mandates without processing capacity trigger bidding wars rather than build plants (B2.5–B4.5), did the most damage to Side A’s consumer-choice and market-creation claims.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by making the motion easier to decide: producers externalize disposal costs, and making them pay changes design incentives while relieving public budgets. Side B’s regressivity argument was the strongest challenge, but it depended heavily on the contested and under-supported claim that municipal funding is meaningfully more progressive, while A plausibly answered that consumers already pay hidden and often regressive costs. B’s innovation and cartel claims were rhetorically sharp but increasingly asserted rather than proven, especially the claim that producer responsibility necessarily locks firms into legacy recycling. A better connected fairness, incentives, and infrastructure funding to the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s fee-modulation rebuttal to B’s innovation claim was decisive: it turned producer payment from a supposed barrier to innovation into the mechanism that makes firms redesign packaging and fund better infrastructure.

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): PRO defended the core externality argument by showing the status quo already imposes hidden regressive costs, while internalizing costs creates design-stage incentives. CON's regressivity objection was mitigated, and its alternative of mandates and tax incentives was introduced too late and left underdeveloped, failing to displace PRO's mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal to CON's regressivity claim, arguing that municipal waste funding already relies on regressive sales taxes and utility fees, while transparent producer-paid costs drive lightweighting and material reduction.
