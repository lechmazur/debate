### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that clinical consent occurs too late to address the upstream normalization and persuasion created by influencer marketing. A raised meaningful ambiguity and chilling-effect concerns, especially around clinician promotion, but overstated age-gating as hiding doctors or blocking medical education generally. B's repeated claim that A conceded existing rules were failing was inaccurate, but not decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest response was that a doctor at the procedure-room doorway cannot undo months of influencer persuasion, while imperfect age verification is not therefore worthless. This directly weakened A's central reliance on the medical checkpoint.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully dismantling the practical application of Side A's framework. While Side A established a strong conceptual argument regarding the 'funnel of persuasion' that precedes a medical consultation, Side B proved that Side A's proposed mechanism would backfire. Side B effectively demonstrated that because ad law treats a doctor linking a clinic as marketing, Side A's rule would inevitably age-gate trustworthy educational counter-speech, leaving minors exposed only to unregulated actors who ignore the rules.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing decisively crush Side A's 'education vs. marketing' carve-out by pointing out that a financial interest (linking a clinic) legally constitutes marketing, proving the rule would ban the very medical professionals Side A claimed would be exempt.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): CON wins by decisively dismantling PRO's core analogies and exposing a fatal textual flaw in PRO's case. PRO relied on an unwritten carve-out between marketing and medical education to avoid chilling doctor-patient communication. CON correctly pointed out that under actual advertising law, a doctor linking her clinic is engaged in marketing, meaning PRO's policy would inadvertently age-gate the most trustworthy medical voices. CON also effectively neutralized the tobacco and alcohol analogy by emphasizing the difference between a retail clerk and a medical professional who can refuse service.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2, where they exposed that PRO's educational carve-out exists only in their own speech and not in the proposition or real ad law, effectively collapsing PRO's defense against the chilling effect on medical counter-speech.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that sponsorship disclosure and clinic-stage consent do not address misleading persuasion at the point of exposure. Its distinction between personal narratives and paid medical marketing was persuasive, as was its argument that imperfect throttling still reduces harm. Side A offered a credible enforcement alternative but treated provider sanctions as mutually exclusive with the motion and never established why additional warnings and reduced youth targeting should be rejected. Its claim that behavioral modeling necessarily misses minors who falsify their age also failed to answer B's aggregate-targeting mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was that a three-letter advertising disclosure cannot be deemed sufficient while a risk warning is dismissed as useless, coupled with the point that platforms can reduce distribution to behaviorally modeled youth audiences even without perfect individual age verification.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A won by successfully defending the efficacy of age-gating and algorithmic throttling. A convincingly explained that platforms use behavioral modeling rather than just signup age to identify minors, making throttling feasible. Side B's alternative of punishing clinics was rhetorically powerful but B failed to address A's throttling mechanism in the closing, leaving A's proactive prevention mechanism largely standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 explanation of how platforms model age via behavioral data decisively countered B's reliance on the idea that age-gates fail when teens lie about their birth year.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A wins by decisively establishing that the primary harm of influencer marketing for cosmetic procedures is psychological and occurs at the point of persuasion (the scroll), not just at the point of sale (the clinic). Side A effectively neutralizes Side B's arguments about age-gating feasibility by distinguishing between individual age-lying and aggregate behavioral modeling, which platforms already use for ad targeting. Side B relies on a false dichotomy between the motion's requirements and provider enforcement, and fails to adequately answer how clinical gates address the parasocial harm inflicted before a minor ever books a consultation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they answer the pressure questions by clarifying that health regulators would write standardized warning text (neutralizing the "corporate theater" argument) and explaining that aggregate behavioral modeling can throttle distribution to minors even if individual users lie about their age (neutralizing the "age-gating is bypassed" argument).
