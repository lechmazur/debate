### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Both sides were competent and the debate was genuinely contested, but PRO (Side B) held a narrow structural advantage that CON never fully neutralized. PRO's core argument—that fentanyl's extreme potency and microscopic supply volume makes supply-side interdiction physically incapable of creating meaningful market gaps, and that synthetic supply is so elastic that enforcement merely reshuffles routes rather than reducing availability—was grounded in concrete physical logic and was only met by CON's "markets have friction" response, which is plausible but comparatively thin. CON's best sustained argument, the voluntary-uptake structural critique (requiring hotlines, naloxone orders, clinic visits), was genuinely strong and well-executed across all rounds, culminating in the "who gets left behind" closing. However, CON overreached by framing harm reduction as requiring "perfect" voluntary engagement: community saturation of naloxone, peer distribution networks, and over-the-counter access create real network effects that partially survive the isolated-user critique. More importantly, CON's answer to PRO's Q1 pressure—asking for evidence that interdiction has ever sustainably reduced fentanyl street price or purity enough to prevent deaths—was essentially conceded with "temporary disruptions matter," which is logically coherent but fails to meet the comparative burden. Since the motion asks which approach reduces deaths MORE over five years, and CON accepted that harm reduction saves lives while offering only speculative "friction gaps" as supply-side evidence, PRO's case carried the live comparative advantage into the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's [A2.4] distinction between user penalties and trafficking penalties—directly defusing PRO's 911-fear argument by clarifying the actual policy target—was the sharpest single rebuttal exchange. It materially damaged a PRO argument and forced PRO to drop that line rather than develop it. CON's turn on the balloon effect [A2.1]—arguing it undermines demand-side logic as equally as supply-side logic—was clever but ultimately weakened CON's own case by conceding the core elasticity problem.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A built the stronger case by consistently sharpening the core clash around voluntary uptake and the isolated user, exposing how harm reduction leaves structural gaps while supply disruption operates without prerequisites, even after accounting for the balloon effect.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The A rebuttals on peer networks and isolated users in turns 8 and 10, which directly neutralized B's network-effect claim by noting peers must be present at the exact crisis moment.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A won by establishing that fentanyl's microscopic potency and elastic supply make interdiction structurally futile, while harm reduction and MAT deliver direct, scalable mortality reductions. Side B landed effective pressure on voluntary-uptake gaps, but never overcame the core logistical impossibility of intercepting a chemical whose entire national supply fits in a few containers. Side A's closing crystallization of "saturation versus starvation" provided the cleaner comparative frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over the isolated user—where Side B exposed the structural limits of voluntary-uptake systems, Side A answered with peer distribution networks, and Side B rejoined that peers must be present at the moment of crisis—was the most decisive and illuminating clash.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO built and sustained the stronger structural case across the full exchange. The core argument—that fentanyl is fully synthetic with no agricultural chokepoint, making supply interdiction structurally incapable of achieving lasting gains—was well-grounded in general knowledge and never adequately answered. CON's most important pressure question (Q1: what changes in five years?) received the weakest answer of the debate: vague claims about AI port scanners and precursor chemical agreements, which are speculative and do not address the underlying adaptation problem PRO identified. PRO's historical mechanism—prescription opioid disruption produced heroin use, then fentanyl—directly undercut CON's "supply dictates demand" claim by showing disruption substitutes deadlier products rather than shrinking the user pool, and CON never squarely answered this. CON's strongest argument was the unsuspecting-teenager/counterfeit-pill scenario. PRO's response was honest (harm reduction does not perfectly protect naïve users) and comparative (neither does interdiction—record seizures have not prevented those pills' ubiquity). This framing shift—asking which approach saves more lives rather than which is perfect—held throughout the debate and was never overturned. CON's boat-and-leak analogy was memorable but functioned mostly as rhetoric rather than a new causal argument, and it was repeated without elaboration. PRO's rebuttal 2 answers were more concretely grounded (X-waiver removal, telehealth expansion, mobile units) than CON's speculative technology pivot. The clipping of PRO's closing is a real execution cost but does not change the outcome because PRO's decisive clash was already established in earlier rounds. On balance, PRO gave cleaner comparative reasoning and more honest engagement with the live pressures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 on supply disruption: by invoking the documented pattern of prescription opioid crackdown driving users to heroin and then fentanyl, PRO directly dismantled CON's "supply dictates demand" mechanism and turned it into evidence for the harm-reduction side. CON never provided a satisfying counter to this substitution dynamic, which remained live through the closing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B built the stronger case by repeatedly demonstrating interdiction's structural futility against synthetic fentanyl and showing that MAT/harm reduction can deliver immediate, scalable mortality reductions within five years. Side A’s emphasis on counterfeit pills and enforcement leverage was effectively neutralized, and its late supply-control optimism added new elements without resolving the core comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.4 and B2.6 directly inverted A’s supply-friction and leverage claims with substitution and underground-use evidence, forcing A onto defensive ground that persisted through the Q&A answers.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by establishing that synthetic fentanyl's lack of an agricultural bottleneck makes supply-side interdiction structurally futile within five years, while providing concrete, observable policy mechanisms (X-waiver removal, telehealth, mobile units) showing MAT and harm reduction can scale quickly. Side B identified real vulnerabilities in unsuspecting counterfeit-pill users and treatment scaling, but never overcame A's empirical turn that counterfeit pills are already ubiquitous despite record interdiction, and offered only speculative technological optimism to explain why decades of enforcement failure would suddenly reverse. A's rebuttals were sharper, more directly comparative, and better grounded in observable dynamics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's turn of the counterfeit-pill argument, demonstrating that the ubiquity of fake pills despite record seizures and DEA warnings proves interdiction has already failed to protect unsuspecting users, converting B's strongest example into evidence for the proposition.
