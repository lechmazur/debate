- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory across all rounds. Its core mechanism was coherent and consistently advanced: pooling proprietary real-time rival data into a shared optimizer structurally enforces price alignment not through explicit agreement but through rational self-interest — deviation triggers a predictable financial penalty, which substitutes for an actual handshake. That mechanism was introduced cleanly in the opening and sharpened throughout. Side A also landed the most decisive reframe in the debate: the motion requires scrutiny, not automatic liability, which largely defuses Side B's chilling-effect and slippery-slope warnings without conceding anything. Side B's strongest moves were Twombly (actual agreement required) and the hotel analogy, but both were answered effectively. Side A correctly distinguished passive public benchmarking from real-time competitor-fed engines, and noted that hotels do not pool proprietary rival data into a single pricing engine. Side B also repeatedly claimed landlords "routinely deviate," but the examples offered (concessions on individual units, seasonal adjustments) describe marginal variation within an algorithmically anchored baseline rather than genuine independent pricing. Side B's closing simply restated earlier arguments without crystallizing a decisive comparative. Side A's closing, by contrast, sharpened the key stakes and clarified that "scrutiny means investigation, not automatic liability" — the cleanest resolution of the live clash.

Once that distinction was planted, Side B had no answer: the proposition only asks for antitrust investigation to apply, not for shared algorithms to be automatically illegal, which renders Side B's innovation-chilling concern largely irrelevant.
