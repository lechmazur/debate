### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a concrete, mechanism-driven case—scale effects (30% voluntary blocking vs. 95%+ default), incentive flips under legal liability (over-blocking to avoid suits), and specific breakage examples (SSO, 3D-Secure, fraud detection)—and used it to consistently pressure PRO's weakest point: what replaces ad revenue "tomorrow." PRO (B) never answered this with anything beyond vague appeals to "ethical monetization," "micro-contributions," and "institutional grants," which CON exploited devastatingly in Rebuttal 2 and Closing ("no local newsroom makes payroll next week on hypothetical grants"). CON's pressure question also exposed a genuine internal contradiction in PRO's opt-out mechanism recreating "consent theater," which PRO's answer (A4.1: "flips the default") never squarely resolved. PRO's framing on power imbalance and dignity was rhetorically serviceable but stayed abstract and moralizing rather than engaging the concrete funding and technical-breakage mechanisms CON kept driving home. Both sides followed Q&A format correctly, but CON's answers were sharper and more directly responsive, while PRO's answers restated assertions rather than closing the gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.4), especially the line exposing that PRO's own answers prove the cost — opt-out to support local news or fix logins recreates consent theater at the OS level, and micro-contributions/grants are not same-day revenue — decisively neutralized PRO's funding and consent-theater claims without PRO ever recovering in Closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that a universal default changes blocking from a voluntary minority practice into an ecosystem-wide funding shock, while Side B never established a credible transition for ad-supported public goods. Its answer of micro-contributions and grants was speculative and conceded that sites might die. A also made the stronger technical comparison about trackers sharing mechanisms with authentication and fraud prevention, though its unsupported 30% and 95% figures deserve discounting. B’s privacy case remained meaningful but relied too heavily on asserting that regulation would produce precise filters and ethical funding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to the local-news pressure was decisive: B was asked for an immediate replacement for advertising and offered only hypothetical contributions and grants, allowing A to frame the mandate as removing free access before alternatives exist.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively dismantling Side A's economic and technical assumptions. Side B's distinction between the current ~30% voluntary adoption rate of ad-blockers (which publishers already price in) and a 95%+ universal mandate (which would cause advertisers to abandon the open web entirely) fatally undermines Side A's claim that the market would smoothly adapt. Furthermore, Side B effectively argues that a legally mandated "robust" filter would incentivize OS vendors to over-block and slow-roll fixes for essential services like SSO and fraud detection to avoid liability, a causal mechanism Side A fails to adequately rebut. Side A's reliance on hypothetical "micro-contributions and grants" to save local news in the face of an immediate revenue collapse cements Side B's victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they explain how scale changes the economic outcome (30% vs 95% block rate) and how legal liability for "robustness" perversely incentivizes over-blocking and slow-rolling fixes for broken logins and fraud checks.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B built a tighter, more consistent case around the "defaults are destiny" concession that A itself made, and used it relentlessly across rebuttals, pressure questions, and closing to argue that the status quo default is not real consent. B's pressure questions exposed a genuine tension in CON's case (blocking as simultaneously too clumsy to work and too effective at destroying revenue), and B's answer citing existing Firefox ETP/Safari ITP as real-world proof that robust-but-compatible blocking is already achievable was a strong, grounded rebuttal that CON never squarely answered — CON's response distinguished "clumsy" from "effective" but did not engage the concrete counterexample. CON's arguments (security monoculture, revenue collapse, blocklist censorship, litigation risk) were reasonable and kept the debate live, but they leaned on assertion (revenue collapse, breakage) without directly rebutting B's contextual-ads/podcast counter-model or the ETP/ITP evidence, and CON's closing mostly recapped rather than resolving the destiny/default trap B set. B's closing crystallized the clash more decisively and memorably than A's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.3), which answered the pressure questions by citing Firefox ETP/Safari ITP as existing proof that robust blocking need not break the web, then used that to spotlight CON's unresolved contradiction between "too clumsy" and "too effective" — a point CON's Rebuttal 2 only partially defused.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B built the clearer case that current defaults negate meaningful consent, create concrete privacy and security harms, and persist because browser vendors face conflicts of interest. B also plausibly answered the frozen-standard and capture objections with functional requirements, audits, and competing implementations. A kept publisher funding as a serious unresolved cost, while B sometimes blurred ad blocking into tracker blocking, but A overstated monoculture, censorship, and total economic collapse without enough comparative support.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B most decisively turned A's “defaults are destiny” premise against the status quo, then answered A's implementation dilemma by distinguishing a legally required outcome from a single mandated codebase. That undercut both the paternalism and frozen-technology objections, although A correctly showed that clumsy blocking and major revenue loss are not logically contradictory.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively capturing Side B's "defaults are destiny" concession and using it to dismantle the consent argument. Side A also successfully trapped Side B in a contradiction regarding the efficacy of system-level blockers, highlighting the absurdity of fearing they are simultaneously too clumsy to work and too effective to survive. Side A's concrete examples (Firefox ETP, Safari ITP, emissions tests) grounded their rebuttals much better than Side B's abstract fears of regulatory capture and litigation, making the comparative case for the proposition much cleaner and more persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they answered the pressure questions with concrete outcome standards and then immediately weaponized Side B's own questions to expose the contradiction in fearing both token compliance and perfect compliance.
