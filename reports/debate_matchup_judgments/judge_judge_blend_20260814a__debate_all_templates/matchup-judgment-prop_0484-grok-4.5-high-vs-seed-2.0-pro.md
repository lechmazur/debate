### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO built a coherent moral case—distinguishing total territorial erasure from mere severe harm—and this line survived CON's "landlocked state" pressure question reasonably well. But CON's practical case proved more durable across the exchange: the claim that vote-buying gets worse under concentrated high-weight votes (only a handful of islands need be captured) was never squarely answered, and the central feasibility objection—that major emitters, who hold the actual power to cut emissions, would simply refuse to accept the rule or abandon the forum—was met by PRO only with vague assertions about "liability" and "isolation" rather than a real mechanism. CON's closing crystallized this unresolved gap effectively ("the lifeguard leaves the beach"), while PRO's closing largely restated its framing without neutralizing the walkout threat. CON's Nile-delta example and GDP-vs-risk-measurability contrast also gave concrete, memorable comparative weight that PRO's rebuttals blunted only partially.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that multiplying a single island's vote weight concentrates leverage (only a few votes need be bought rather than many) directly undercut PRO's claim that risk-weighting merely "raises the price of delay," and PRO never returned to rebut this specific mechanism before closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by turning the debate from moral entitlement to practical climate outcomes. Side B clearly defended national erasure as categorically distinct, but never adequately established that risk scores would be governable, that concentrated voting power would resist capture, or that indispensable emitters would remain bound to a forum that sharply reduced their influence. Side A’s certainty and numerical claims were sometimes unsupported, but its feasibility comparison remained the cleaner path to a vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest response was that weighted island votes concentrate leverage in a small number of aid-dependent swing states, making coercion easier rather than merely more expensive. Side B repeatedly said higher weights raise the price of delay, but did not resolve the capture mechanism or compare that cost with the much larger cost of emissions cuts.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A wins because they effectively neutralized Side B's biggest impact (the emitter walkout). In Rebuttal 2, Side A provided concrete, pragmatic reasons why major emitters would stay at the table (liability, trade chaos, residual dominance). Side B completely ignored these warrants in both of their final speeches, instead falsely claiming that Side A only made "moral arguments" and left the walkout threat "unaddressed." Because Side A's defense stood, their framing that the status quo guarantees SIDS erasure carried the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1/A4.2) cleanly distinguished territorial erasure from severe subnational harm and provided the pragmatic mechanisms for why emitters wouldn't walk out, decisively answering B's pressure questions.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Both sides built vivid, coherent cases, but B identified and sustained the decisive analytical distinction that A never fully answered: that risk rankings tolerable for adjustable, donor-controlled finance are qualitatively different from fixed, permanent voting power that can trigger a full walkout of major emitters. B's pressure question forced this into the open, and its A1/A2 answers in Rebuttal 2 directly reasserted and sharpened this mechanism, while A's parallel answers ("we already rank risk for finance, so it's fine for votes" and "nothing is risked because the status quo is already worthless") largely repeated earlier assertions rather than engaging the voluntary-vs-binding distinction or the walkout mechanism. A's "nothing to lose" framing was rhetorically powerful but conceptually risky, since it required accepting zero marginal value in residual finance/pressure flows, a claim B explicitly contested and A never rebutted with a real counter-mechanism. B also left A's slippery-slope point (other vulnerable groups demanding the same weighting) effectively unanswered by A, reinforcing B's structural concerns. A's rhetoric (the house-demolition/arsonist framing) was the most memorable moment of the debate, but B's countering "co-owners of an unfenceable atmosphere" reframe blunted its force and was never squarely re-answered by A. Overall B did more of the comparative weighing work the judging frame rewards, while A's case, though emotionally compelling, relied on repeated assertion rather than progressive rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 distinction between finance rankings (voluntary, donor-adjustable) and voting weights (permanent, power-conferring), later reinforced in the A1 answer of Rebuttal 2, was the most decisive exchange: it isolated exactly why A's "we already tolerate imprecise risk rankings" argument does not transfer cleanly to formal voting power, and A never neutralized this distinction.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by establishing a concrete comparative harm: weighted voting could drive away the emitters and funders whose participation supplies the remaining finance, technology, and emissions cuts. Side B powerfully indicted the status quo but relied on the implausibly absolute claim that islands receive nothing now and therefore cannot be made worse off. It never adequately explained how greater formal weight would compel powerful states to comply rather than exit. A’s exit prediction was somewhat overstated, but its distinction between imperfect delivery and no delivery remained the strongest live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it distinguished flexible aid rankings from fixed voting power and directly identified what islands could lose—existing adaptation finance, loss-and-damage mechanisms, and continued pressure on major emitters. This undercut Side B’s central claim that changing the rules carried no downside.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully reframing the debate around the practical requirements of international cooperation, effectively arguing that giving small islands a veto over major emitters would cause those capable actors to walk away, resulting in zero emissions cuts and zero adaptation finance. Side B decisively won the clash over the risk metric by pointing out that imprecise metrics are tolerable for voluntary aid under donor control but unacceptable for fixed, binding voting power.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first answer in Rebuttal 2 (B4.1) cleanly dismantled Side A's claim that we already use risk metrics for loss and damage, by distinguishing between voluntary, donor-controlled finance and permanent, binding voting power.
