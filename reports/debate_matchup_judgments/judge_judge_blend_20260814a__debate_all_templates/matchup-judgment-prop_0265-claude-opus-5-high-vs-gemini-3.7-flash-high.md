- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won by keeping the decisive clash on advertised-vs-owed prices and the race-to-the-bottom comparison: honest all-in menus still look dearer under CON’s footnote model, and CON never squarely neutralized that incentive problem. Side A’s answer that the $20 is the “true cost of the food” was evasive, and its labor-transparency and tipping-confusion points were largely neutralized by Side B’s argument that restaurants can still explain the bundled price in words and itemize downstream.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s containment of the conditional-fee objection—arguing it is the exception where a flagged line is the only available honesty, not a reason to license misleading headline prices in the ordinary case—and its sustained, unanswered race-to-the-bottom frame about the honest restaurant losing the customer on the street.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a decisive market-based impact (the race to the bottom where honest operators are punished by lower sticker prices) that Side B fails to adequately answer.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the party of six trap. Side B tried to use PRO's concession on conditional large-group fees to invalidate the entire motion, but Side A sharply distinguished between conditional and unconditional charges, showing that the exception does not justify widespread drip pricing for ordinary tables.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): B won by narrowing the live choice: after both sides accepted pre-order notice via a same-type menu line, ambush/bait-and-switch was off the table and PRO had to defend inclusion itself. B’s cleaner path was that loud disclosure cures the real harm while preserving itemization against double-tipping and house-vs-staff opacity; that PRO’s rule still loses walk-ins to untouched tipping stickers; and that “all” mandatory charges cannot cleanly fold conditional large-party/patio/card fees without overcharging or silently amending the motion. A’s decoy-price slogan stayed sharp and the tax distinction was fair, but A’s carve-out on conditionals and weaker answer to surviving anchoring left B with the easier comparative vote. Clipping cost B some closing breath in R2 but not the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s “all” pressure and R2 follow-through: conditional party fees force overcharge or abandon the motion’s wording, plus inclusive stickers still lose to voluntary-tip competitors.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by proving that the motion’s word “all” cannot accommodate conditional charges without overcharging some diners, and by showing that inclusive pricing fails to solve price anchoring because voluntary-tipping restaurants would still display lower sticker prices. Side B never fully answered these burdens, retreating to a narrower “baseline” reading that effectively rewrote the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal and closing crystallized that PRO had quietly abandoned the word “all” by exempting conditional party fees as “distinct booking terms,” and that the real deceptive anchor—untouched voluntary tipping—would survive PRO’s rule unchanged.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by exposing the practical and textual flaws in Side A's proposal. B successfully traps A on the word "all" in the motion, forcing A to abandon conditional fees to avoid absurd overcharging. B also decisively counters A's market competition argument by showing that inclusive pricing still loses to voluntary-tipping restaurants on sticker price, meaning A's rule only punishes honest operators. B's framing of "curing the surprise" rather than "dissolving the number" is rhetorically sharper and more aligned with actual consumer needs. Side B receives a minor execution penalty for a clipped final sentence in Rebuttal 2, but the substantive victory remains clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to Side A's "race to the bottom" argument, pointing out that because the motion doesn't touch voluntary tips, an honest inclusive restaurant ($26) will still lose walk-ins to a tipping restaurant ($22), proving A's rule only disarms candid operators without solving price anchoring.
