### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully proving that tuition is "sticky" due to fixed costs, meaning universities cannot simply lower prices as Side B assumed. This validated A's core claim that institutions would instead manage risk by screening out marginalized students. A also decisively won the policy comparison, demonstrating that extending prospective eligibility rules is mechanically superior to extracting retroactive penalties from current budgets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2, which used the fixed costs of nursing and social work clinicals to dismantle B's claim that lowering tuition is a cheap, easy lever, perfectly setting up A's closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): CON successfully undermined PRO's core mechanism by showing that for high-cost accredited programs price is sticky, making quiet admissions screening the likelier response than tuition cuts. CON also maintained a cleaner alternative—extending existing prospective debt-to-earnings aid rules to nonprofit programs—which targets the same bad actors without retroactive extraction or perverse screening incentives. While PRO landed powerful moral framing about risk asymmetry, CON's sticky-price and prospective-versus-retroactive distinctions gave the cleaner reason to reject the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing sequence turning PRO's "sticky price" point into proof of the admissions-screening effect, and showing that PRO's own closing language endorsed the exact filtering CON had predicted.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully defending the core logic of the debt-to-earnings metric: universities control the debt side (tuition), meaning a student is only a liability if the university overcharges them. Side A cleanly turned Side B's "access" and "sticky price" arguments by pointing out that if fixed costs make a program too expensive to offer without trapping students in default, the university should not be selling it. Side B's reliance on macro shocks was neutralized by A's point that low-debt programs in the same region wouldn't flag, and B's preference for prospective aid cutoffs over retroactive cost-sharing failed to overcome A's framing that cutting off aid is also a harsh, retroactive-style penalty that avoids holding the school accountable for the specific losses it caused.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled the "screening" and "sticky price" scares by explaining that raising GPA cutoffs to avoid a modest loss-share is terrible math compared to lowering tuition, and that a first-generation student with responsibly priced debt is entirely safe under a relative metric.
