### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by turning Side A's core mechanism into a fatal flaw. Side A argued that transparency allows consumers to "strategize," which would force sellers to raise baseline prices. Side B successfully framed this as a concession that Side A's model relies entirely on consumer ignorance and "opacity as a business model."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 expertly leveraged Side A's "strategize" point, arguing that if a pricing scheme only works when consumers can't see the rules, it inherently undermines market comparison and competition.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): PRO won by showing that CON's efficiency defense depended on keeping consumers ignorant of the rules governing their own prices, and by offering a principled distinction between legitimate transaction-level pricing (time, inventory, public coupons) and illegitimate person-level profiling. CON repeatedly conflated transparent dynamic pricing with secret individualized extraction and never proved that hidden profiling was necessary to achieve lower prices for price-sensitive buyers. PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions crystallized this clash cleanly, while CON's answers to PRO's pressure questions were weaker and relied on existing law and vague market signals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal (A4), answering CON's pressure questions by distinguishing transaction-level factors from person-level profiling and demonstrating that public off-peak fares, coupons, and loyalty programs already achieve access-expanding segmentation without requiring secrecy.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by successfully turning CON's core efficiency claim into a liability. CON argued that disclosure would let buyers "strategize," but PRO correctly identified this as an admission that the pricing model depends on consumer ignorance. CON never resolved the paradox of how market discipline, reputation, or switching can police pricing that is explicitly hidden and individualized. PRO's Rebuttal 2 and Closing cleanly narrowed the clash to transaction-based vs. person-based pricing, showed how transparent alternatives preserve flexibility, and framed the decisive metric around market legibility. CON remained stuck on abstract deadweight loss claims, blurred the line between dynamic and personalized pricing, and offered circular answers to pressure questions about detection. PRO built the clearer, more responsive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 directly answered CON's pressure questions by drawing a sharp line between transaction-driven pricing (time, inventory, public rules) and person-driven profiling, then turned CON's "strategizing" admission into a decisive indictment of opacity as a business model. This exchange collapsed CON's market-discipline defense and crystallized the winning path.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A effectively leveraged the word 'usually' to demonstrate that the motion would sweep up routine, beneficial discounts. When Side B attempted to carve out exceptions for actions like cart abandonment, Side A systematically dismantled that distinction by showing those discounts also rely on opaque algorithmic inferences and would become unsustainable under forced disclosure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, which dismantled Side B's economic assumption that a dropped discount proves exploitation by clarifying that selectivity is precisely what makes targeted discounts sustainable.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): CON successfully undermined PRO's defense of "usually" by showing that algorithmic pricing powers common pro-consumer discounts and that PRO's visible-versus-opaque distinction could not cleanly separate harmful markups from benign targeted offers without becoming overinclusive or conceding the real target was narrower abuse. PRO's disclosure mechanism was shown to be either unhelpful boilerplate or destructive to beneficial discounting, while CON's alternative of regulating specific harms directly remained intact. PRO failed to fully resolve the dilemmatic framing that the motion was both overinclusive and underhelpful.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing framed the decisive dilemma: PRO's visible-action/opaque-inference line is unstable because benign targeted discounts and retention offers also rely on opaque algorithmic layers, forcing PRO either to capture routine competitive discounting or concede that the real objection is narrower abuse better handled by direct regulation of deception and unlawful discrimination.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully exploiting the word "usually" to demonstrate that the proposition sweeps in routine, pro-consumer discounting (cart abandonment, win-back offers, loyalty rates). CON consistently pressed that PRO's proposed distinction between "visible" discounts and "opaque" algorithmic markups collapses in practice, since algorithms drive both. CON also offered a superior regulatory alternative: backend audits and targeted rules against deception/illegal proxies, rather than consumer-facing boilerplate that fails to distinguish markups from discounts. PRO's case rested heavily on information asymmetry and fairness, but never adequately answered the economic trade-off that forcing disclosure destroys the selectivity that makes targeted discounts sustainable. CON's rebuttals were sharper, the economic reasoning more robust, and the closing crystallized the overbreadth trap decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 dismantling PRO's "visible action vs. opaque inference" test. CON showed that routine discounts rely on layered opaque inferences, meaning PRO's rule either kills beneficial offers or exempts them and concedes the debate. CON's point that selectivity funds discounts, and that backend audits catch discrimination better than consumer boilerplate, materially broke PRO's live route to victory.
