### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): PRO won on the two most contested analytical exchanges. First, PRO cleanly distinguished demand for cognitive *outputs* from demand for *human labor*, directly neutralizing CON's Jevons Paradox and "lump of labor" framing. CON never adequately responded to this asymmetry—demand for AI-generated documents can explode without requiring proportionally more human workers to produce them. Second, and most decisively, PRO's A4.2 rebuttal separated the spreadsheet analogy from AI: spreadsheets only computed, still requiring humans to design, analyze, and synthesize, whereas AI does all three. This is a sharp, accurate structural distinction that CON simply did not answer in B4 or the closing. CON's entire case relied on the spreadsheet-to-accounting-growth historical pattern, and PRO demonstrated that pattern does not apply when the same tool eliminates the labor bottleneck entirely. PRO also answered CON's pressure question on specific policy mechanisms (payroll tax shifts, portable benefits, agile retraining grants) directly and concretely, while CON's critique of those mechanisms remained generic. CON's strongest moments were the Jevons framing, the enterprise-adoption-slowness point, and the CAD/architecture analogy for the apprenticeship pipeline—but none of these survived PRO's rebuttals without a reply. PRO's closing reference to nonexistent IDs ([C2.1], [P1.5]) is a real execution penalty, slightly depressing the margin, but does not change the winner because the arguments underneath those labels were already live and clearly identified by context. Overall, PRO made the debate easier to decide in its favor by repeatedly sharpening the central asymmetry CON never neutralized.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory through two decisive moves that Side B never adequately answered. First, the "no higher cognitive rung" reframe in Rebuttal 1 effectively distinguished generative AI from the spreadsheet analogy at Side B's core: previous automation pushed workers up the cognitive ladder, but generative AI is already automating the analysis and drafting at the top of that ladder. Side B's response—that humans will pivot to trust, negotiation, and accountability—was plausible in principle but failed to answer the ratio math Side A pressed throughout: a firm needs fifty analysts to process data but only five partners to manage clients, so the forty-five surplus cannot simply pivot into relationship roles because those roles are strictly bandwidth-capped. Second, the bakery/subscription insight in Rebuttal 2 was the most damaging single move in the debate. Side A noted that small businesses newly able to afford cognitive services would simply buy an AI subscription, not hire displaced corporate analysts. Side B's reply—that the bakery grows, opens new locations, and needs managers—was weaker because it assumed enough growth to employ the displaced at scale, and even a thriving bakery needs far fewer human workers than the analysts it supposedly absorbed. Side A crystallized this cleanly: "The market expands in software revenue, not human employment." Side B's policy alternative of portable benefits and retraining credits in its A2 answer was actually more responsive and reasonable than its earlier framing of all safety nets as "anchors," but that concession slightly undercut Side B's own opening. Side B's historical democratization argument had genuine force and its "static vs. dynamic economy" framing was the strongest rhetorical move on its side, but it could not resolve the bandwidth cap problem or the subscription-not-analyst reality. Side A maintained consistent internal logic across rounds, adapted its argument as the exchange evolved, and left the decisive clash unresolved against Side B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 bakery/subscription point [A4.4]: when the opposition argued that cheaper AI democratizes services and creates demand for human workers (e.g., the local bakery suddenly affords a marketing consultant), Side A responded that the local bakery will simply buy the AI subscription itself rather than hire a displaced corporate analyst. This directly gutted Side B's core democratization mechanism in a way Side B never squarely answered, and it crystalized the debate's central asymmetry—productivity gains flow to AI owners and software revenue, not to displaced knowledge workers.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side B wins by consistently neutralizing the ratio-collapse claim through dynamic market expansion and entrepreneurship examples, directly answering pressure questions with portable benefits/retraining instead of evasion, and crystallizing a coherent dynamic-pie comparison in closing. Side A lands solid rebuttals on inelastic demand and policy timing but fails to rebut the new-enterprise reallocation path or supply a comparably grounded transition mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1 counters the 45-analyst pivot problem by showing cost collapse enables lean new firms, which A never squarely neutralizes.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B built the stronger path to victory by establishing a concrete mechanism for net white-collar job loss—the structural collapse of the support-to-leadership ratio—and by effectively undercutting Side A's market-expansion and entrepreneurship responses with the observation that small businesses and consumers will buy AI directly rather than hire displaced workers. Side A's historical analogies and dynamic-market optimism were plausible but remained abstract, and their "entrepreneurship wave" answer to the ratio collapse was speculative and squarely challenged as a mathematical impossibility. Side B's sustained "parachute" framing and sharper weighing of the clash made the decisive difference.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's counter-democratization argument in Rebuttal 2 that small businesses will purchase AI subscriptions directly rather than hire displaced analysts, which collapsed Side A's induced-demand and entrepreneurship claims.
