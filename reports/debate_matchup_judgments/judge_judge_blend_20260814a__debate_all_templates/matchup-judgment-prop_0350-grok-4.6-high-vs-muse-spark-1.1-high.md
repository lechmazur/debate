### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases with a consistent throughline (A's "turn down the current" vs B's "last three feet"), and both handled the Pressure Questions format correctly with proper A1/A2 labeling. But A edged ahead on substance: it more directly answered the reformulation and evidence-gap pressure with a causal mechanism (soda's low satiety driving net sugar reduction, category-level rules catching reformulated products) rather than relying mainly on tu quoque, as B often did ("you have not shown those rules did either"). A also neutralized the tobacco disanalogy by narrowing its claim to marketing/placement rather than banning food itself, and turned the regressivity charge into a stronger equity argument (diabetes disparities) that B never fully rebutted. B's strongest point—that a purchase drop is not proven to equal an obesity drop—remained live and unresolved, and its closing reassertion of "the eater decides" was rhetorically sharp, but it repeated the leakage argument more than it advanced new comparative weighing. On balance A gave the cleaner, more integrated path to the resolution as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to B's reformulation question (A4.3), reframing "the meal no rule reaches" as proof that category-level environmental rules outlast skill-based fixes, directly neutralized B's strongest pressure point and set up A's closing turn on the "200 decisions" argument.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate by showing why population-wide defaults and marketing limits operate precisely when time, attention, and willpower fail. B effectively challenged purchase reductions as an imperfect proxy for obesity, but never established that consumer-skills campaigns achieve better population outcomes and repeatedly narrowed A's broader policy package to soda taxes and checkout rules. A offered the stronger comparative mechanism: structural rules remove many repeated battles automatically, whereas B's approach still depends on sustained individual bandwidth.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A decisively turned B's claim about hundreds of daily decisions: the volume of decisions strengthens the case for removing cues and unhealthy defaults rather than expecting consumers to repeatedly deploy learned skills. B's substitution objection created useful pressure, but A plausibly answered that substitution is not one-for-one and that passive population reach remains superior.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won a highly competitive debate by successfully arguing that individual behavior campaigns rely on cognitive bandwidth that evaporates under fatigue, whereas environmental rules alter defaults passively. Side B pushed hard on environmental rules being 'leaky' and only covering a fraction of choices, but Side A's defense—that removing cues entirely prevents the willpower battles individuals would otherwise have to fight—was ultimately more persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response that a checkout ban doesn't teach you to resist candy, but rather "removes the battle you never needed to fight," decisively answered Side B's claim that skills are better because they travel.
