### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the comparison tied to large across-the-board increases and showing that sector-specific rates and phase-ins can cover many workers without imposing the same excessive floor everywhere. Side A’s breadth dilemma and sector-demand elasticity argument created real pressure, but depended on assuming boards must impose uniformly large rates and bundled mandates. B plausibly showed those are contingent design choices rather than inherent features, while A never fully neutralized the basic calibration advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1 most decisively answered A’s breadth dilemma: expanding boards across sectors does not recreate a uniform minimum when each sector can receive a different rate and phase-in. A later reasserted flat rates and mandate stacking but did not establish that these outcomes are structurally inevitable.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's 'scalpel' framing. A proved that sectoral boards still act as blunt instruments within sectors, and successfully argued that isolated sectoral price spikes trigger more consumer substitution (and thus job loss) than a broad, economy-wide price increase. A also provided a superior structural warrant for why boards inevitably bundle costly mandates (low-salience rulemaking vs. high-salience statutory compromise).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 completely turned B's elasticity question, demonstrating that a 10-15% concentrated sectoral spike allows consumers to substitute (e.g., cooking at home), destroying jobs, while a 2-3% broad minimum wage hike relies on inelastic economy-wide demand.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by maintaining the comparative frame more faithfully and demonstrating that even imperfect sectoral calibration raises pay with fewer employment losses than a uniform statutory floor. While Side B landed a sharp sectoral-elasticity challenge and a plausible political-economy critique, it could not fully establish that boards intrinsically require costly mandate-bundling or that the untouched-sector cushion disappears under any plausible coverage expansion. Side A’s concrete counterexamples and dilemma crystallization made the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 (A4), which directly answered Side B’s pressure questions on coverage and political economy, then crystallized the comparative dilemma and showed why calibration preserves employment advantages even across multiple sectors.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by maintaining the most direct link to the proposition: sectoral and regional calibration can deliver raises while reducing the shock imposed on fragile employers. Side A raised strong coverage, delay, and capture objections, but these more clearly challenged reach and administration than B's central claim about comparative employment losses. A also weakened its response by substituting a moderate federal increase for the large increase specified by the motion and by continuing to characterize wage boards as legally blocked after B directly explained how state wage-setting authority avoids that problem. Both sides used unsupported precision in discussing California, so neither gained much evidentiary credit there.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure on A's state-and-city comparator was decisive: states stuck at the federal floor cannot be reached through that strategy, while A's answer retreated to a moderate federal increase rather than defending a large across-the-board increase. B then paired this with a direct answer to the preemption objection, which A largely repeated rather than neutralized.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively grounding the debate in the real US institutional context. Side A's argument that hostile state legislatures would block wage boards even more easily than minimum wages neutralized Side B's best pressure regarding holdout states.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1 response in Rebuttal 2 flawlessly turned Side B's question about the 20 holdout states, pointing out that a wage board requires the exact same hostile legislature plus new institutional machinery.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): CON won by successfully reframing the debate from an abstract mechanism comparison to an institutional feasibility and coverage comparison. PRO's theoretical efficiency advantage could not overcome the decisive coverage problem: sectoral boards reach only a fraction of low-wage workers slowly and fail entirely where institutional capacity is weakest, while a minimum wage reaches all covered workers immediately. CON also showed that PRO's flagship California example exhibited the same price pass-through and closure channels that PRO claimed boards avoid, rendering the "fewer employment losses" claim doubtful even for the narrow slice covered. PRO's "per dollar of pay" closing frame was persuasively rejected by CON as the wrong metric for a proposition about raising pay for low-wage workers as a class.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 and 2 dismantling PRO's California fast-food example by showing that prices rose, franchises closed, and employment channels were identical to minimum-wage disemployment mechanisms—just with far narrower coverage, undermining PRO's claim that boards uniquely avoid these losses.
