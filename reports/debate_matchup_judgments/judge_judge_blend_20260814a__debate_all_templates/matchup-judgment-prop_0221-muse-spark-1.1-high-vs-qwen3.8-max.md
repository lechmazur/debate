### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a clearer, more concrete causal mechanism (2:1 diagnosis/correction time impossible at 16:1) and backed the cost comparison with a specific, defensible structural claim (50% more permanent salaries/classrooms vs. a coordinator plus shared rooms). When CON pressed on scale-related implementation costs, PRO turned CON's own "scale breaks quality" argument back on class-size reduction using the California hiring-surge example, and directly answered both pressure questions with a substitution model that neutralized the "double-spending" and "unpriced supervision" attacks. CON's rebuttals restated the friction/reliability theme effectively but never squarely dismantled PRO's substitution model or offered a comparably concrete mechanism showing how a 16:1 classroom produces returns rivaling structured 2:1 tutoring for the lowest-performing readers. That left PRO's marginal-return framing as the more resolved, less contested route to victory by the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) turning CON's "scale erodes quality" argument back onto class-size reduction via the California hiring-surge point, combined with the "substitution not addition" scheduling model that defused the double-spending pressure question CON raised.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the comparison on marginal gains per dollar: targeted tutoring supplies concentrated corrective practice with cheaper labor, while class-size reduction requires many more full-salary teachers and rooms while spending broadly on students with less need. Side A plausibly identified turnover, supervision, scheduling, and reliability costs, but mostly asserted that these would erase tutoring's advantage without showing why they outweigh class-size reduction's much larger structural expense. Some of B's precise empirical claims deserved discounting, but its mechanism and comparative cost argument remained stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was its answer that tutoring can substitute into existing rotations or intervention blocks rather than create double spending, followed by the comparison between managing tutors and permanently expanding certified staffing and facilities. A continued emphasizing implementation friction but never established that this friction reverses the per-dollar comparison.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B won by keeping the decisive comparison live: permanent 50% certified headcount and rooms versus concentrated 2:1 feedback with cheaper labor, and by showing 16:1 still cannot buy the mechanism tutoring buys even after real scale frictions. A raised serious reliability and logistics costs, but never established larger gains per dollar for class-size reduction itself; the case stayed stronger as doubt about tutoring than as an affirmative win on the motion’s metric.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 1 and Pressure Q2: scale quality collapse boomerangs harder onto forced mass teacher hiring, and 16:1 still cannot produce daily continuous oral-reading/error-correction time for struggling readers.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON (Side A) adapted better under pressure. PRO's opening mechanism (concentrated dosage vs. diluted attention) was strong, but B conceded in Rebuttal 1 that scaled tutoring requires paid, coached, embedded staff—effectively admitting away the "cheap delivery" cost advantage that was central to the opening. A exploited this directly ("you can't have it both ways") and pressed for a concrete cost comparison; B's answer to the pressure question ("costs vary by district, but the comparison does not") was an evasion on exactly the quantitative claim the motion turns on. A also introduced and sustained a mechanism B never fully neutralized: pull-out/embedded tutoring double-counts instructional time (either stealing from core subjects or requiring new schedule capacity), whereas CSR recaptures lost minutes across the whole day for all students. A's "28 to 20 in high-poverty K-3" targeting answer also defused B's "diluted attention" framing by matching PRO's own logic of targeting scarce dollars efficiently. B's closing denominator/numerator framing was rhetorically clean but didn't resolve the unanswered cost-quantification gap or the double-counting critique, leaving CON with the more resolved, harder-to-rebut path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1) turning B's own concession about needing "paid, embedded, scripted tutors" into an admission that the per-dollar cost advantage collapses, combined with B's evasive non-quantified answer to A's Q1 pressure question—this exchange most decisively shifted the debate toward CON.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by keeping the comparison centered on marginal gains per dollar and explaining why targeted remediation avoids spending on students already proficient. Side A raised credible implementation concerns and argued forcefully for durable whole-class benefits, but repeatedly assumed an ideal 28-to-20 reduction and asserted an unproven same-budget equivalence. Its specific workforce and scheduling arithmetic was also less grounded. B acknowledged tutoring overhead, answered the pressure questions directly, and preserved the core asymmetry between concentrated instructional dosage and diffuse ratio reduction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its response that Side A compared fully funded, threshold-crossing class-size reduction against degraded volunteer tutoring. The point that most class-size dollars may buy only marginal ratio changes directly weakened A’s 30-hours-for-everyone framing, which A answered mainly by reasserting rather than establishing its same-budget premise.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON won by forcing the cost and scale comparison onto realistic paid, coached, high-turnover staffing rather than cheap volunteers, then showing that same budget buys full-day core instruction gains for all students (targeted K-3/high-poverty) versus short add-on dosage for a subset. PRO's targeting/concentration frame stayed live but never recovered a clear residual per-dollar edge once the cheap-delivery premise was conceded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal that funded tutoring becomes a parallel workforce whose loaded cost erases the claimed efficiency edge versus one certified teacher cutting 28-to-20.
