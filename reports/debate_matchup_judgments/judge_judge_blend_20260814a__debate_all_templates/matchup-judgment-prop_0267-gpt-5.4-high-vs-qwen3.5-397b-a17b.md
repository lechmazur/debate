### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): PRO won on three decisive grounds that CON never adequately neutralized. First, PRO correctly hammered the motion's actual language throughout—"allow more" and "rather than rely mainly on" do not mean replacing indoor venues, so CON repeatedly attacked a position PRO did not hold. This made CON's stability and weather arguments miss the target; PRO correctly noted indoor venues continue operating and the question is what to add on top. Second, PRO's geographic equity argument—what happens to neighborhoods that cannot support restaurant rents or long leases—was the most damaging live route in the debate. CON's only answer was "targeted subsidies for indoor ventures or accept slower growth," which introduced a new mechanism late and was unconvincing. PRO's crystallization ("under their approach, it stays dark until investors arrive; under ours, it can be activated next month") was the sharpest comparative framing of the entire debate and went unanswered. Third, PRO successfully reframed the activity-vs-economy clash by showing that nightlife economies are constituted by foot traffic, small transactions, and repeated use—activity is not decoration but the substance of revival itself. CON's repetition of "activity is not economy" became decreasingly useful as the debate progressed because CON never defined what economic revival required that sustained foot traffic, spending, and inclusive entrepreneur access would not provide. CON's genuine strengths—enforcement costs, permanence as a signal to investors, year-round reliability—were addressed by PRO with permit-fee cost-sharing, designated pitch design, and the correct observation that bad-weather indoor trade is unaffected by the motion. PRO's two clipped speeches are real execution costs that prevented full development of the jobs and safety arguments, but what was visible in those clips was substantively stronger than CON's responses. CON's speeches were shorter overall, repetitive on the stability/driftwood framing, and never developed a live route that survived PRO's motion-language defense.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q2—arguing that flexible vendors create the first demand layer that proves a location works before fixed investment follows, and crystallizing it as "under their approach, it stays dark until investors arrive; under ours, it can be activated next month"—was the most decisive exchange. CON's closing response ("street food masks underlying economic weakness rather than fixing it") was a repetition rather than a genuine answer to why waiting for anchors is a viable strategy in under-resourced neighborhoods.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a clear, practical timeline for urban renewal where street food acts as the necessary first layer of demand. Side B fails to adapt to Side A's clarification that permits would be targeted to areas with existing infrastructure, and Side B's concession that lower-rent areas must rely on "slower growth" or subsidies cements Side A's comparative advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A2 in Turn 7, which brilliantly turns Side B's attack on "transience" into a strength by explaining how temporary vendors test locations, prevent sunk costs, and seed future investment.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): A won by establishing a coherent theory of change: street food acts as a low-risk, fast-deploying seed that activates marginal spaces—transit hubs, event edges, and lower-rent neighborhoods—where indoor venues are not viable. A directly answered B's enforcement and cost concerns by showing permits could target designated pitches with existing infrastructure and self-financing fees, while turning B's "transience" argument into a resilience advantage that avoids sunk costs. B's stability and infrastructure arguments were substantive but never squarely answered A's core challenge: under an indoor-first model, areas that cannot support restaurant rents simply stay dark until capital arrives. B also repeatedly treated the motion as an elimination of indoor zones rather than a diversification away from over-reliance on them, which weakened its comparative framing. A's closing crystallized this geographic and barrier-to-entry gap cleanly, leaving a live route to victory that B's stability frame could not close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (A4) was the decisive exchange. By answering B's pressure questions head-on with A1/A2 labels—showing vendors could cover service costs through permit design and that flexible seeding builds sustained demand without requiring day-one leases—A sharpened the "landlord-first vs. city-first" comparison and exposed B's inability to revive nightlife in places that cannot anchor permanent venues.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by consistently exploiting a structural vulnerability in Side A's own case: A admitted at [A2.2]–[A2.3] that trucks primarily serve patrons after venues have already created the crowd. B identified this as a self-defeating concession—if trucks are downstream of fixed venues, cities cannot rationally "rely mainly" on the downstream layer. B pressed this across every subsequent round ("the accessory vs. the engine") and A never escaped it. A's "feedback loop" response in Rebuttal 2 was asserted rather than demonstrated; claiming trucks sustain the anchor crowd contradicts A's earlier framing that trucks serve the crowd venues already assembled. B's pressure questions were sharper and more strategically targeted than A's, specifically exposing the toilet/security/waste infrastructure gap and the destination-demand dependency. A had genuine rhetorical strength—the "eyes on the street" safety frame and the inclusivity/barrier-to-entry arguments both had intuitive appeal—but A leaned on imagery ("the street that lives vs. the zone that waits") in places where the comparative economic argument was actually losing. B's durability claim ("what survives winter, off-peak nights, and resident scrutiny") was never seriously answered. B also engaged more precisely with the motion's critical word "mainly," repeatedly showing that A had at most proven trucks are a useful complement, not a primary strategy. A's closing was rhetorically energetic but did not resolve the core logical vulnerability. B's closing was tighter, crystallizing the live clash rather than restating rhetoric.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 ([B2.2]–[B2.3]) was the most decisive exchange. B turned A's own symbiosis argument against A: A had said trucks serve patrons "after bars and venues create the crowd," which B correctly reframed as proving trucks are an accessory, not an engine. This established the burden A could never fully discharge—that trucks should be relied upon mainly—and it anchored B's case for the rest of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully demonstrating that food trucks are a dependent layer of the nightlife ecosystem, not the primary engine. B effectively weaponized Side A's claim that trucks serve patrons after venues close to prove that indoor zones must remain the main focus to generate the initial destination demand.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal, which caught Side A's admission that trucks serve crowds after bars and used it to logically prove that trucks are an inherently downstream accessory, making them unfit to be the primary strategy.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by proving that street food depends on indoor anchors for its crowd, turning Side A's own "symbiosis" and "2 a.m. extension" claims into evidence that trucks are accessories, not engines. Side A's "sequence" argument failed to overcome this causal dependence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where it converted Side A's claim that trucks "extend the economic window" after bars close into proof that trucks are downstream complements, making it impossible to rely mainly on them under the motion.
