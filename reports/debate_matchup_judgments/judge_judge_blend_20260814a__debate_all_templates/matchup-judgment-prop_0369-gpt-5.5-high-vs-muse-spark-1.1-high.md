### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the live clash on burden allocation and error costs clearer across the full exchange. The reversible-vs-irrevocable framing plus A’s use of B’s own “defaults should track intent” language created a cleaner decision path than B’s repeated insistence that transparency plus preference memory already solves everything. B made a coherent alternative standard (informed choice, not anti-fee steering) but was hurt by two clipped speeches and never fully neutralized the asymmetry A pressed: money lost after checkout vs one click before. A’s later turns narrowed rather than widened and crystallized who pays when inertia wins; that comparative weighing decided a competitive round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A3–A4 pressure and answers: reversible pickup mistake caught before payment vs irreversible delivery overcharge, plus forcing B’s “intent” principle against apps that reset to expensive delivery regardless of past pickup history.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing and sustaining a decisive asymmetry: a wrong pickup default is reversed with one tap before payment, while a wrong delivery default costs the user money after checkout. Side B’s transparency alternative assumed away the behavioral inertia that apps currently exploit, and B’s accessibility counterexamples were blunted by A’s use of proximity and order history under a “more often” standard. A cleanly co-opted B’s “intent” principle to show the current system violates it, and B’s clipped speeches plus inability to neutralize the reversible-error framework left A’s core route to victory intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 answer to B’s pressure questions, collapsing the “intrusive data” and “transparency alone” objections by showing apps already have the data and that disclosure fails against inertia, paired with the sustained reversible-error framework that absorbed B’s default-bias-symmetry objection.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively dismantling Side B's core argument regarding the asymmetry of errors. Side B argued that defaulting to pickup creates a reversible error caught at checkout, while defaulting to delivery creates an irrevocable financial loss. Side A effectively pointed out the double standard in this framing: it assumes the user is attentive enough to catch a pickup default but inattentive enough to blindly pay a higher delivery total. Side A demonstrated that if default bias is strong enough to cause financial harm, it is also strong enough to strand users in unwanted pickup orders. Side A also successfully defended the distinction between remembering individual user preferences and the motion's broader mandate to default to cheaper options based on crude signals like distance. Despite minor clipping at the end of Side A's first two speeches, their logical rigor and sharp rebuttals secured the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they exposed the logical contradiction in Side B's treatment of default bias, arguing that inertia cannot be predatory only when it produces the expensive choice and harmless when it produces the cheap one.
