### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A (PRO) wins because it builds and maintains a cleaner comparative case across every round. Its core structural argument—that geographic accident should not determine who shoulders a continental burden—is never adequately answered by CON. CON's alternative is essentially an improved Dublin system, and PRO's "Dublin plus a bigger check" frame sticks because CON never explains why better-funded frontline processing would prevent the same political radicalization, Dublin-suspension threats, and recurring crises. PRO's decisive move comes in Rebuttal 2: the "managed imperfection vs. concentrated dysfunction" framing and the double-standard argument ("CON demands perfection from quotas while accepting catastrophe from the status quo") are both clear and never neutralized. PRO also handles its pressure questions honestly—conceding secondary movement exists but reframing it as manageable dysfunction rather than systemic failure—while correctly turning the enforcement question back on CON: frontline states must also refrain from waving migrants north, and reluctant states must fund generously, so CON's model requires the same political will it says quotas cannot command. CON's strongest point—the asymmetry between financial enforcement (dock structural funds) and physical relocation (cannot force social integration)—is genuine and partially survives the exchange, which is why this is competitive rather than dominant. But CON never resolves the structural flaw in its own alternative, and "geographic reality backed by financial enforcement" still leaves three coastal states absorbing the continental burden by accident of coastline. PRO's closing crystallizes this cleanly without introducing new material. Both sides use legitimate general knowledge and avoid brittle fake precision; no format violations occurred.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3–A4.5): the enforcement symmetry turn is the most decisive exchange. PRO shows that CON's model requires exactly the same political will it argues quotas cannot command—reluctant states must fund frontline processing and frontline states must not wave migrants north—while CON's financial-vs-physical asymmetry, though real, still cannot answer why its alternative avoids the recurring crises and radicalization the status quo has already produced. The "which imperfection do you choose" frame that follows crystallizes this asymmetry into a clear voting issue.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): A won a clear but competitive debate by making the feasibility clash more decisive. B persuasively framed geography-based responsibility as unfair and crisis-producing, but A gave the cleaner answer that mandatory quotas face two practical barriers B never fully solved: secondary movement driven by language, labor markets, and diaspora, and the difference between enforcing money transfers and forcing physical reception and integration. B's managed imperfection framing was strong, but its answer to 2015-style noncompliance relied mostly on stronger penalties without explaining how that produces actual housing, acceptance, or durable settlement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's fiscal-versus-physical enforcement rebuttal was the most decisive exchange: B argued all systems need compliance and quotas can be backed by structural-fund leverage, but A sharply distinguished docking money for border support from compelling hostile states and communities to receive and integrate relocated asylum seekers.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side A won the debate by cleanly resolving the clash over enforcement. Side B attempted to equalize the burdens by arguing both models require political will and enforcement, but Side A persuasively distinguished between the automatic mechanism of docking EU funds versus the impossible task of forcing local governments to physically house and integrate migrants.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively exposed the flaw in Side B's enforcement symmetry argument by showing that Brussels can unilaterally withhold funds, but cannot unilaterally build housing or force social cohesion against a local electorate's will.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B wins a clear but competitive debate on the strength of two connected moves that Side A never fully neutralized. First, the 2015 relocation scheme failure is used not merely as a historical anecdote but as structural evidence: the mechanism fails because mandatory physical relocation requires overriding democratic consent on a uniquely sensitive question, and the costs of resistance (sovereignty weaponization, institutional backlash) are worse than the costs of the status quo. Second, and most decisively, Side B's counter to Side A's enforcement claim—that freezing EU funds did not actually produce relocation compliance in Hungary and Poland, it merely entrenched resistance—stands as the most important live point at the end of the exchange. Side A asserted the "enforcement architecture now exists," but this claim was already answered by Side B's sharper empirical observation that the punishment hardened the defiance rather than dissolving it. Side A's best move was the asymmetry argument in Rebuttal 2: both systems face defiance and secondary movement, so why not at least physically distribute the burden? Side B responds adequately ("quotas add a constitutional crisis on top of those shared problems") but does not fully extinguish the physical-space pressure. However, Side B consistently drew a meaningful distinction between contribution-based solidarity—funding, personnel, voluntary relocation—and mandatory physical relocation, which successfully rebuffed the caricature of "just wiring money to Lampedusa." That distinction meant CON was not defending abandonment but a coherent alternative, and Side A never argued it away. The format cuts to both sides' openings and closings are roughly symmetric and do not change the outcome.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to PRO's enforcement claim in Rebuttal 2 [B4.5–B4.6]: "The PRO celebrates that funds were frozen to Hungary and Poland—but those governments still refused relocation. The punishment entrenched the resistance it was meant to dismantle." This directly attacks the single new mechanism PRO introduced in its first rebuttal (fund conditionality) and does so with the same empirical record PRO invoked. PRO never recovered a clean answer to why financial coercion would produce physical relocation compliance when the historical record of the same coercion shows it deepened entrenchment instead.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): A won a clear but competitive debate by making feasibility and political consent decisive. B persuasively argued that frontline states face a real physical bottleneck and that money alone cannot solve overcrowding, but A repeatedly showed that mandatory quotas require coercive enforcement, risk deep EU institutional backlash, and still do not reliably prevent secondary movement. B’s answer that stronger conditionality would fix 2015 mostly reinforced A’s point that quotas depend on punitive confrontation rather than genuine solidarity. A’s alternative was imperfect, but A framed it as more realistic assistance without triggering the added constitutional crisis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange was the response to B’s enforcement claim: B argued failed quotas only needed financial conditionality, while A turned that into the core liability, saying coercion would entrench defiance and convert migration policy into a legitimacy crisis without ensuring durable relocation.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's enforcement mechanism and effectively neutralizing the secondary movement argument. Side A successfully argued that tying funds to compliance creates a constitutional crisis without actually resulting in relocation, turning Side B's core structural fix into a liability.
