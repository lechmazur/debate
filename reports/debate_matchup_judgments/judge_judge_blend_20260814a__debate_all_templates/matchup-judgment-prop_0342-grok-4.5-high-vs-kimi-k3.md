### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): A won by reframing the debate correctly as an instrument question (is "core subject" the right vehicle, not whether the skill matters) and sustaining a sharp "fork" throughout: the durable competencies PRO cites (provenance, incentive-mapping, emotional resistance) are already taught in history/civics/English, while the genuinely novel content (spotting specific deepfake/voice artifacts) expires faster than any curriculum cycle can track. The decisive moment came when B, answering pressure questions, conceded the assessment would certify "method, not perfection" — A immediately exploited this in Rebuttal 2 to argue the core subject would manufacture certified false confidence without demonstrated competence, and B's closing never directly rebutted that specific charge, instead reasserting prior claims. A also never let go of the unfunded-mandate/equity-cuts-both-ways point, which B answered only by restating that optionality is worse, without addressing the resource-capacity problem. B's case was emotionally vivid and internally consistent on urgency and coverage, but never closed the gap between "students need this skill" (undisputed) and "a mandated core subject is how they actually get it," which was the operative burden A forced onto the table from the opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 turning B's own Pressure-Question answer ("the test measures method, not perfection") into proof that core-subject assessment manufactures unverifiable, bureaucratically-blessed false confidence — a concession B never recovered from in the closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by separating universal need from the proposed instrument. Side A established urgency, equity, and the weakness of optional instruction, but B showed that core enrollment does not itself establish competence and offered mandatory, assessed integration into existing subjects as a more adaptable alternative. B’s durable-versus-perishable fork remained substantially live: general provenance and incentive analysis fit existing disciplines, while technique-specific detection risks rapid obsolescence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the integration challenge with graded cross-curricular instruction, then argued that Side A’s process-based test could certify resistance without measuring whether students actually resist manipulation. This sharpened the central coverage-versus-competence distinction.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by successfully decoupling the universally acknowledged need for AI literacy from Side A's specific mechanism of a mandated core subject. Side B's "fork" argument—that durable skills are already taught in existing cores, while novel detection skills expire too fast to be standardized—was devastating. Furthermore, Side B effectively weaponized Side A's concession that testing would measure "method, not perfection" to prove their own opening point about manufacturing false confidence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 argument that graduates failing to spot fakes proves the skill is a "losing target" (since professionals also fail), rather than proof that a new core subject would succeed, cleanly neutralizing Side A's strongest empirical claim.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but PRO built a tighter trap and closed it more decisively. PRO's pressure question ("is your objection the label or the funding?") forced CON into a corner: CON's proposed alternative (mandated sequences with assessment, teacher training, continuous updating) is functionally indistinguishable from "core" status except for the word "permanent." CON's answer—that permanence itself, not funding, is the objection—never explained why a "rewritable, department-owned" mandate would resist the exact budget-driven decay CON's own opening logic (and PRO's B1.4) predicts for anything short of protected core status. PRO exploited this gap cleanly through rebuttal 2 and closing ("a sequence schools can quietly cut will be quietly cut"), while CON's closing mostly reasserted the permanence-as-risk framing without closing that loop. CON's opposing points about ossified content and opportunity cost were reasonable but were partially defused by PRO's surface-vs-spine distinction, which CON never fully dismantled. CON's clipped opening (an explicit execution fault) also cost it some of the sustained development PRO enjoyed. Overall PRO progressed the debate more effectively, converted its pressure question into a decisive closing frame, and left the cleaner reason to vote for it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B3.1 pressure question, combined with the B4.1-B4.6 rebuttal and B5.1/B5.4 closing, which exposed that CON's "mandated sequence" alternative was core status without the label and equally vulnerable to the decay mechanism CON itself criticized in optional modules — a bind CON never fully resolved.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): B narrowly won by preserving a credible mandatory, assessed, cross-curricular alternative. A compellingly established universality and accountability, but too often treated B’s strengthened embedded standards as the unchanged status quo and asserted rather than proved that only a freestanding permanent subject could be protected. B better explained why contextual practice could improve transfer while avoiding ossification and added timetable costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.2 most decisively answered A’s attempted collapse of the alternatives: B distinguished mandatory embedded reform from passive status quo and showed that widespread misinformation does not itself prove that a new department is the missing solution.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by effectively separating the fast-changing 'surface tells' of AI from durable underlying mechanisms, defeating Side B's ossification argument. Side A also systematically dismantled Side B's 'embedded modules' alternative by showing that distributed ownership leads to a lack of accountability and specialized training, forcing Side B into a damaging concession about 'incomplete transfer' in the status quo.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively won the structural clash by comparing AI literacy to writing—a skill used everywhere but still requiring its own core department—and pointing out that Side B's 'mandated sequences' would consume the same hours without offering protection from cuts.
