### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A built and sustained a decisive gray-zone framing throughout the debate: cables are being damaged now, under conditions where conventional forces are structurally incapable of responding proportionately (a warship cannot fire on a fishing vessel dragging an anchor). Side B never escaped this trap. Crucially, B's own answers to the pressure questions effectively conceded A's core claim—shadowing vessels and identifying patterns over time means the cable is already severed before any deterrent accumulates. A then exploited this explicitly in closing. The marginal-procurement argument was another consistent A advantage: the debate is about the next frigate versus cable redundancy, not about eliminating fleets. Existing hulls can escort repair vessels and patrol; the incremental hull offers diminishing returns against the single-point-of-failure gaps redundancy closes directly. B's strongest thread—that resilience requires the very conventional margin the proposition seeks to shrink—had real force, but A answered it cleanly by distinguishing between the existing fleet (available for escort) and marginal new procurements (the true subject of the motion). B also hurt itself by opening well under the word limit, leaving its core causal story underdeveloped. A's rhetoric was sharper throughout, especially the crystallizing image of the carrier battle versus the trawler, which encapsulated the key asymmetry memorably. The result is a clear but not dominant A win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A's pressure question Q1 (what can a warship do against a fishing vessel dragging an anchor?) combined with B's answer in Rebuttal 2. B conceded that the response is to shadow, record, and wait for patterns to emerge—which A then turned in closing to show that the damage is done before the deterrent accumulates. B's own answer proved A's structural point that conventional presence cannot prevent gray-zone cable cuts in real time, only respond slowly after the fact.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by making the marginal tradeoff and gray-zone threat environment central. A convincingly argued that existing conventional forces cannot reliably prevent ambiguous cable attacks using anchors or trawls, and that redundancy and repair capacity reduce the consequences before deterrence or attribution can work. Side B’s conventional-control argument was plausible, but it leaned too heavily on open-conflict assumptions and never fully answered why the next visible platform beats direct resilience against the attacks already described.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was pressing that warships can shadow or punish only after damage is done, while diverse cable paths and rapid repair make a single gray-zone cut less catastrophic from the outset.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A systematically dismantled Side B's reliance on conventional naval presence by emphasizing the gray-zone nature of cable threats. Side A's explanation that redundancy forces attackers to make multiple cuts—thereby destroying the plausible deniability required for gray-zone operations—was a decisive, unanswered reframe. Furthermore, Side A effectively weaponized Side B's admission that warships must wait to establish "patterns" of sabotage, noting that by the time a pattern is proven, the critical infrastructure is already dark.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 response explaining how redundant paths neutralize gray-zone tactics by forcing attackers to coordinate multiple cuts, thereby destroying the plausible deniability that makes such attacks viable.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B won on two arguments that remained live through the closing. First, the deterrence/signaling problem: Side B consistently argued that invisible hardening cannot deter because a planner who does not know routes are hardened cannot adjust expected payoff. Side A's answer—that detection risk during emplacement raises attacker costs—was narrow and did not fully solve the signaling gap. Second, the degradation-versus-paralysis problem: Side B correctly pressed that "degraded" does not equal "inert," and Side A's entire case required that threshold. Side A asserted paralysis more than demonstrated it, and never produced the doctrine or bandwidth threshold its own Q2 invited. Side B also landed the most decisive exploitation of the exchange: when Side A answered Q2 by conceding the investment is bounded and "reverts to commercial operators," Side B correctly identified this as a structural concession—"disproportionate by construction"—that undermined the proposition as written. The "much more" framing from the motion was never adequately defended after that moment. Side A's multiplier argument (cable protection amplifies existing platforms) was logically coherent and kept the case alive, but it depended on the unproven paralysis premise. Side B's opportunity-cost and deterrence visibility arguments remained the cleaner comparative path to a decision, and both were well-maintained across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 observation that Side A's own answer to Q2 constituted a concession: PRO admitted "much more" is a bounded, self-limiting spend that reverts to commercial operators once chokepoints are secured. Side B's sharp response—"disproportionate by construction"—directly turned the motion's own language against PRO and was the moment the debate turned most clearly.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): B won by making the central comparison clearer: cable attacks are serious but PRO did not prove that degraded connectivity becomes paralysis, while CON showed platforms retain layered communications and broad deterrent value. A had a plausible multiplier story, but leaned too much on catastrophic isolation and did not sufficiently answer redundancy, execution difficulty, or opportunity cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive in separating cheap mapping from costly coordinated execution and framing the burden as degraded communications versus actual paralysis.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A won by successfully answering the "invisible deterrence" pressure and logically demonstrating why the dependency of visible forces on data makes cable protection a force multiplier. Side B relied heavily on rhetorical binaries (gutting vs marginal, paralyzed vs perfect) and ignored Side A's direct answer that adversary reconnaissance would reveal the hardened infrastructure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1 response explaining that adversaries discover "invisible" hardening during reconnaissance, which alters their operational calculus, cleanly neutralizing Side B's main deterrence objection.
