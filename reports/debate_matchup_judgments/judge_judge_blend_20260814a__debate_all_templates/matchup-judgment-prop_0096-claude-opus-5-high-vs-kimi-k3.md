- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by strictly adhering to the motion's text and exposing Side B's failure to defend "broad contract boilerplate." Side A successfully argued that while B's proposed substantive limits (floors) cap harm, they still permit the silent extraction of a voice, making opt-in consent a necessary addition rather than a competing alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized B's 'substance vs. ceremony' push by pointing out that 'consent and floors do different work,' noting B's floor still allows synthesis without asking.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): A wins on burden: PRO only needs consent necessary, not sufficient. B never defends the target boilerplate "all media hereafter devised" and instead argues for better specific, bounded contract terms – which PRO shows is compatible with opt-in. A answers the reuse harm by requiring specific scope (this film, languages, term) not perpetual checkbox, and answers cheap-signature pressure by noting boilerplate is invisible while opt-in at least creates a visible refusal point. B's best hits – non-response logistics and non-waivable floors – are mitigated and still allow synthesis into 40 languages without ever asking. Clipped B R2 also costs execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Exchange A2.1 alleging contradiction (cheap vs hard) vs B2.1-B2.4 showing porous where protection needed, rigid where not, plus reframing non-response as nobody deciding, not refusal – the pivot that forced A to clarify bounded opt-in vs checkbox costume and admit floors can coexist, sharpening the final necessity-vs-sufficiency weighing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully deconstructing the mechanism of "opt-in consent." A persuasively argued that under unequal bargaining power, an individual checkbox is either easily coerced (for day players) or a logistical barrier (for non-response), and that the actual protections PRO relies on (bounded scope, retention caps) are substantive contract terms rather than the procedural act of opting in. Side B argued strongly for the moral necessity of consent and exposed flaws in A's collective floor, but ultimately conceded that a checkbox can be "boilerplate in costume," allowing A to crystallize that the substantive limits do the real work while the opt-in gate is mere ceremony. A's minor clipping in Rebuttal 2 was a slight execution cost, but A's closing perfectly recovered the argumentative thread.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's exploitation of Side B's admission that a hiring-day checkbox can be "boilerplate wearing a checkbox costume," which allowed A to prove that bounded scope and non-waivable limits (contract terms) do the actual protective work, reducing the opt-in gate to mere ceremony.
