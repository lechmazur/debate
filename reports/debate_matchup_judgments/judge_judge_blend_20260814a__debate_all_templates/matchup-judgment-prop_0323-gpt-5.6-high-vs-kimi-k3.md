### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-organized cases and handled the Pressure Questions round with proper A1/A2 formatting, but B's structural rebuttals were more damaging and less fully resolved by the end. B's key moves—that the motion only hides two of many status signals, so the contest re-anchors to slower, incumbent-friendly metrics (followers, verification); that unknown creators rely on visible momentum precisely because they lack an incumbent's built-in audience, making hidden counts a "closed audition"; and that public counts are the raw material for outside detection of manipulation, so hiding them "clears the crime scene" for the platform with the worst policing record—were never fully neutralized. A's replies (the "fairer initial test" framing, "platforms already have the data internally") were plausible but conceded ground (e.g., "no reform must abolish every comparison to be worthwhile") without resolving the tension between claiming hidden counts help newcomers and B's claim that visible momentum is precisely what lets newcomers compete against incumbent distribution. A's strongest and cleanest point—the private/public harm distinction and the timing-of-encounter argument—held up well and kept the debate competitive, but B's cumulative pressure on discovery, transparency, and algorithmic dependency, reinforced by sharper closing rhetoric, gave it the more convincing overall path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's discovery argument in Rebuttal 1/2 ("a viral post from 200 followers is indistinguishable from a dud until the algorithm decides otherwise") directly exposed a tension in A's case between "fairer test for newcomers" and "newcomers need visible momentum to overcome incumbent distribution," which A's replies mitigated but never fully resolved.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by successfully framing the debate as a choice between crude but visible crowd judgment and entirely opaque algorithmic judgment. Side B also capitalized on a late mechanical contradiction by Side A regarding how opt-ins would actually preserve data for researchers if defaults are as sticky as Side A claimed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing correctly identified that if defaults are sticky (Side A's core premise), relying on an opt-in toggle to preserve researcher transparency completely collapses, effectively dismantling Side A's defense against the loss of bot detection.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A won by making the decisive clash sharper and more decisive for the proposition as written: public like/repost counts function as an immediate post-level social-proof instruction that compounds early distribution into herding, while private analytics and other signals remain; the default stickiness argument is asymmetric (compulsory exposure vs reversible opt-in). B landed real pressure on platform opacity, re-anchoring to follower/view hierarchies, and collective bot-spotting, yet never fully recovered from A's circularity critique on discovery (the platform must already surface a post before any count can influence a stranger) or the contamination problem with aggregate numbers as "transparency." Both stayed disciplined and progressive across rounds; A simply left the cleaner comparative reason to adopt the specific reform rather than demand it cure every hierarchy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1 + A1/A2 on discovery: platform already decides initial distribution, so the count cannot be what first lets unknowns break through; it only converts that encounter into self-reinforcing proof.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but B's rebuttal chain more effectively survived scrutiny. A's strongest move — "available survives a toggle" — never fully answered two live problems B pressed repeatedly: (1) the "credible signal" framing A itself used (strong creators opt in) logically implies weak/absent numbers under default-off, recreating exactly the stigma A claimed to eliminate, a contradiction B's Q2 and B4.1 exploited without a clean resolution beyond an asserted "storefront sign" analogy; and (2) the collective-reversibility asymmetry — viewers cannot restore information a poster never thought to make visible before it mattered (e.g., emergent virality, fundraisers, warnings), which A's "eager opt-in" claim assumes away via foresight it cannot guarantee. B's closing crystallized this cleanly ("exposure is individually reversible; concealment of shared data is not collectively reversible"), while A's closing largely re-asserted its framing ("public is the disease") without closing that gap. A's opening being clipped before its conclusion is also a real execution cost. A had more vivid rhetoric and an emotionally resonant anecdote, but B's case was the one that survived the exchange with fewer live holes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure-question/rebuttal combo (B3.2 into B4.1) exposing that A's own "credible signal" logic for opt-in creators implies the opposite stigma for non-opt-in users, which A's "tax on deviance" answer never fully neutralized.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): B wins by systematically breaking down the mechanics of A's proposed default-off toggle. B exposes a critical dilemma: if viewers can override the default to see metrics, the teenager's vulnerability remains public; if viewers cannot, valuable shared information is lost to inertia. Furthermore, B successfully weaponizes A's own concession that successful creators will 'eagerly' opt in to prove that A's world recreates the exact stigma A sought to eliminate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 analysis of the 'stigma' argument, pointing out that A's admission that strong creators will opt-in ensures that hidden counts will still be read as weak counts through adverse selection.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): A won by locking the clash on defaults and who holds the switch: benefits survive an opt-in while public harms require the scoreboard's forced visibility, and stigma is asymmetric once concealment is the norm. B landed real points on shared signals and challenger social proof, but never escaped the consistency trap after conceding defaults matter and some protections are warranted. A's cleaner answers on pressure and tighter weighing made the decision easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 stigma asymmetry (concealment as ordinary default vs. deviant exit) against B's flip-argument on adverse selection.
