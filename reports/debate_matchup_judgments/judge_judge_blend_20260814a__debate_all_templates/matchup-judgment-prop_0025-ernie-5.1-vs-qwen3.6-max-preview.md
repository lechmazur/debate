### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): PRO entered the debate with structural advantages the proposition itself supplies—built-in sector exceptions and real-world implementations across France, Italy, Portugal, and a German telecom pilot—and kept returning to those assets every time CON pressed on flexibility or daytime compression. The core empirical claim (national adoption did not collapse output or require blanket bans) was never squarely falsified; CON's best response was that stable macro output could mask ground-level compression, which is logically possible but speculative rather than evidenced. PRO also correctly framed the "voluntary and compensated" provision as a power-imbalance correction, not a flexibility killer, and used the A1/A2 format correctly in Rebuttal 2 to engage CON's pressure questions head-on. CON's strongest live route—that compliance-driven HR behavior will produce blanket prohibitions that eliminate the very flexibility PRO promises—was genuinely damaging and never fully neutralized; PRO's counter that "employers can't ban voluntary work" understates how rational liability avoidance works in practice. CON also scored on the workload-versus-timestamp distinction, correctly noting that capping communication windows without reducing deliverables does not make work disappear. These arguments kept CON competitive. However, CON could not displace the accumulated weight of national-scale evidence PRO cited, and its compression/blanket-ban scenarios remained mechanistically plausible but empirically unconfirmed. PRO's Rebuttal 1 clipping is a real execution cost, and PRO's closing was thin (well under the cap and mostly recapping earlier rounds rather than crystallizing the decisive weighing), while CON's closing better sharpened the operative clash. Still, the balance of evidence plus the proposition's built-in flexibility architecture give PRO the cleaner overall path to a vote, producing a clear but competitive margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's Q1 (what mechanism stops HR from issuing blanket bans?) and PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2. PRO argued that employers won't ban voluntary work because they'd lose evening labor employees genuinely want, and cited France/Italy as proof. CON responded in Rebuttal 2 that blanket prohibitions are the "predictable, rational response" to liability exposure—not evasion. This back-and-forth was the crux of the debate: PRO's "it worked in practice" evidence partially held, but CON's compliance-behavior mechanism remained partially live because PRO never adequately explained why liability-averse HR would absorb payroll complexity rather than simply prohibit after-hours contact.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): PRO built a more coherent and consistent path to victory. Its core argument—that voluntary guidelines fail because power dynamics make them toothless, and that statutory liability shifts incentives at the organizational level—held up across the full exchange. PRO's historical analogies (overtime, anti-harassment rules) were plausible and never convincingly rebutted; CON's attempt to distinguish overtime as incentivizing longer hours was not developed enough to land. CON's strongest move was the complaint-burden inversion: if only junior workers can trigger enforcement against the bosses who pressure them, the law entraps the protected class. This was creative and genuinely forced PRO to respond. PRO responded credibly in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing by framing organizational liability as analogous to payroll compliance—audits and fines target the company, not individual whistleblowers. CON never adequately addressed why this mechanism would fail the way wage-theft enforcement fails, leaving PRO's response live. CON's central "sieve" argument—that exceptions make the line undrawable—was the weakest plank throughout; the existence of narrow carve-outs is standard in labor law and does not logically expand to cover all knowledge work. CON's most damaging structural failure was never offering an enforceable alternative, a gap PRO's Q1 exposed and CON never closed. CON's rhetoric was sometimes crisp ("compliance theater with better branding") but recycled the same structural beats too often without narrowing the dispute. PRO's framing—"urgency is actually earned" vs. "burnout disguised as dedication"—was more memorable and mapped cleanly onto the core weighing: a statutory floor that shifts institutional incentives outperforms aspirational goodwill that structurally collapses under competitive pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answers to the pressure questions. When CON asked who files the complaint, PRO correctly argued that organizational liability—timestamped logs, inspectorate audits, fines on the company—mirrors how wage-theft regulation works, removing the burden from the individual worker and placing it on the employer's balance sheet. CON never explained why this mechanism would fail, leaving PRO's enforcement model standing and the complaint-burden critique partially answered.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): PRO wins by directly answering pressure questions with enforceable mechanisms (employer liability, narrow auditable exceptions) that neutralize CON's core objections on gaming and burden-shifting, while CON offers only aspirational culture change and leaves its alternative unspecified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2 structure) that clarifies liability lands on the organization via audits, not junior complaints, directly resolving the enforcement clash.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by exposing that PRO's enforcement mechanism still depends on junior workers filing complaints against senior managers, preserving rather than flipping the power imbalance. This structural critique, combined with the "invisibility" penalty for junior staff who legally decline after-hours work, left PRO's liability model under-defended. PRO never fully explained how proactive audits would systematically detect violations without vulnerable complainants, making their cultural-shift claim speculative next to CON's concrete perverse-effects story.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing crystallized that PRO's employer-liability model actually weaponizes power asymmetries by forcing junior employees to trigger enforcement, making the law a shield for managers and a trap for subordinates.
