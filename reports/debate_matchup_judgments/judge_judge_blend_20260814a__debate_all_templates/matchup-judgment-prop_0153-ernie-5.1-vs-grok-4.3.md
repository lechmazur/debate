### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) held the stronger live route to victory because it consistently pressed the question CON never squarely answered: prohibition does not stop surrogacy, it merely removes every legal protection from women who enter underground arrangements. CON's "structural refusal" argument is coherent in theory—refuse to create an enforceable interest in gestation, and you remove the legal machinery of exploitation—but it only works if a ban actually prevents the practice. Because CON never established that surrogacy demand disappears under prohibition, the argument tacitly concedes that desperate women still enter secret contracts, just without advocates, restitution rights, or court-ordered exits. PRO's pressure question Q1 exposed this gap cleanly, and CON's A1 response ("prohibition refuses to create any enforceable contract") confirmed the evasion rather than answering it. PRO's strongest throughline—hidden market versus accountable, regulated one—remained live at the end because CON's replies mostly restated the recruitment-effect concern rather than explaining how banning actually protects the women who will still pursue arrangements. CON did land real points: payment selects for financially vulnerable women, economic dependency persists after signing, and regulatory ratchet is a genuine political risk. These arguments were never fully neutralized. But PRO's UK/Australia examples, independent-advocate model, and coercion-clause framework—even where the specific mechanisms were asserted rather than verified—provided a more practically grounded comparative story. The clipping of PRO's Rebuttal 1 is a genuine execution cost that slightly closes the margin; PRO recovered in subsequent rounds but lost ground in that exchange. Overall the debate was genuinely contested, but PRO made the decisive comparison clearer: the choice is not between exploitation and safety, it is between invisible exploitation and state-supervised, legally exitable arrangements, and PRO gave better reasons why the latter is preferable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question Q1—"how does prohibition protect women from debt-driven pressure better than a state-funded advocate who can legally halt a contract?"—was the most decisive exchange. CON's answer in A1 effectively conceded that prohibition offers no affirmative protection; it simply refuses to create the contract that would trigger any protection. PRO then crystallized this in the closing: "structural refusal guarantees their invisibility." CON never recovered from the implication that banning does not protect vulnerable women—it just removes the only mechanisms that could.

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): PRO established a clearer live route to victory by detailing concrete regulatory mechanisms—court-ordered suspension, full restitution, and voidable coercion clauses—that directly answer CON's concerns about sticky consent and post-implantation coercion. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side B wins by doing the more complete comparative work the proposition requires. Side A's philosophical argument—that economic desperation makes consent under a surrogacy contract structurally coerced—is genuinely strong and rhetorically brilliant, and its closing lines ("a receipt," "the same evil dressed in regulation") are the most memorable moments in the debate. However, Side A never credibly addresses what happens to the same economically desperate woman under prohibition. Altruistic surrogacy as an alternative is introduced once and abandoned. The "shrinks the domestic market" argument in A4.3 is asserted but not developed; it does not answer what the desperate woman actually does when domestic paid contracts are illegal. Side B consistently returned to this comparative gap: prohibition removes documented consent procedures, exit rights, and reporting duties while leaving the same economic desperation intact, making coercion invisible rather than correctable. Side B's A1/A2 responses in Rebuttal 2 are structurally direct and land the key point that state-funded counsel has no ongoing compliance stake, which Side A never fully neutralizes—it can only argue that economic need persists, which Side B already concedes while asking what prohibition does about that need. Because the proposition is explicitly about legalization WITH strong protections, and Side B consistently defended why that combination beats the alternative while Side A's best argument proved exploitation exists without demonstrating prohibition reduces it, Side B carries the decisive comparison. The margin is real but not wide, because Side A's rhetorical execution and structural coercion argument created genuine pressure throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was over the "who pays for counsel" clash. Side A forced the concession that independent counsel means nothing if the surrogate's underlying economic need persists. Side B's response—that the state has no financial stake in the outcome, making its funding structurally different from agency-paid counsel, and that the comparison is not between perfect regulation and perfect prohibition but between visible correctable coercion and invisible uncorrectable coercion—was the stronger practical answer. Side A could only reply that economic desperation remains, which Side B had already built into its framework, leaving Side A without a comparative path to victory on this crucial subpoint.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side B won by making the central clash about whether regulation can cure economic coercion, then repeatedly showing that counsel and cooling-off periods do not change the poverty-driven incentive to sign. Side A had a strong harm-reduction frame about visibility, enforceable rights, and underground markets, but it never fully answered B’s market-expansion argument or the point that withdrawal rights before implantation do not make the alternative to signing bearable. B’s empirical grounding was somewhat overconfident, especially on India and trafficking, but its comparative mechanism and rhetorical crystallization were stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response that “a cooling-off period means nothing when your landlord is waiting for rent,” followed by the distinction between ordinary labor and pregnancy, most effectively damaged Side A’s safeguards-based route to victory.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by maintaining a clearer comparative framework throughout: strong regulation with state-funded counsel, cooling-off periods, and pre-implantation exit rights creates visible, enforceable protections that reduce net coercion relative to prohibition, which simply pushes surrogacy into unmonitored underground and cross-border markets. Side B’s economic-coercion argument was forceful but remained largely static after the opening, and B never squarely rebutted A’s mechanism showing how licensed domestic channels with mandatory reporting shrink demand for trafficked supply chains. A’s direct answers to B’s pressure questions and the “visible versus invisible coercion” crystallization provided the cleaner, more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 answers to Side B’s pressure questions, demonstrating that one-time state-funded counsel creates no ongoing compliance pressure and that licensed domestic verification and reporting requirements shrink trafficked supply chains, thereby neutralizing B’s core attacks on regulatory capture and cross-border exploitation.
