### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by presenting a coherent narrow guarantee: statutory succession of one existing license to one heir, without resale or duplication. Side A’s strongest objection—that this resembles a legacy tool—ultimately reinforced B’s decisive distinction between discretionary access and enforceable access as of right. Side A raised legitimate pricing, privacy, and perpetual-duration concerns, while B’s clipped rebuttal was an execution cost, but A overstated commingling as inseparability and never justified deletion over a severable entitlement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer to the ownership dilemma was decisive: an heir can succeed to the deceased’s restricted license without receiving the platform account or creating a secondary market. Side A’s reply that this was merely “a legacy tool with a statute” conceded the legally enforceable guarantee that the motion sought.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B wins by cleanly isolating the core clash: whether access should be a legal right or a corporate courtesy. Side A offers strong pushback on the technical difficulties of separating mixed data and argues that "lifetime" access was the bargained-for price. However, Side B effectively neutralizes this by pointing out that Side A's own alternative concedes families should have access, just without the guarantee. Side A's attempt to trap Side B in the Pressure Questions backfires, as Side B correctly notes the motion itself specifies the assets are "non-transferable." Side B's final teardown of Side A's concert ticket analogy effectively wins the argument over deceptive pricing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing response to Side A's concert ticket analogy, noting that a ticket's expiration is printed on its face while an ebook's death-clause is hidden in fine print.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A built a more compelling normative case centered on consumer expectations (the "Buy now" button vs. hidden death clauses) and the crucial distinction between a legal right and a discretionary platform courtesy. Side A effectively parried Side B's privacy and commingling concerns by strictly limiting the scope to assets "purchased at checkout" and using strong analogies, such as a letter tucked inside a physical book. While Side B scored points by highlighting that PRO's guarantee functionally resembles a mandated legacy tool rather than full property transfer, Side A successfully argued that the legal guarantee itself is the decisive mechanism preventing corporate windfalls and revocation. Side A's minor clip at the very end of Rebuttal 2 was a slight execution cost but did not materially damage their already complete argumentative structure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where they dismantled the privacy attack by distinguishing between purchased assets and free inboxes, and cleanly answered the pressure questions by defining the mechanism as a simple succession of the existing license without resale rights, neutralizing the secondary market fear.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by framing inheritability as a distinct product right rather than an automatic feature of anything purchased. Its pricing, consent, privacy, and administrative-cost objections created cumulative pressure that Side B only partly answered. Side B compellingly attacked adhesion contracts and explained severability, but conceded that transferable copies can carry a premium, gave an incomplete answer on who bears transfer costs, and relied heavily on asserting that durable access is property. Three clipped turns also weakened B's execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest turn was converting B's acknowledgement of premiums for transferable ebooks into evidence that inheritability has a cost and that the motion mandates an upgrade buyers did not purchase. B answered that prices can be equal and markets coexist, but never fully displaced the product-differentiation argument.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A consistently outmaneuvered Side B by framing the digital purchase as a deliberate, open-eyed choice of a lifetime license over an inheritable physical copy. Side A also effectively pressed Side B on the prohibitive costs of digital probate, which B struggled to answer practically. Furthermore, Side B suffered severe execution costs, having its first rebuttal, second rebuttal, and closing all clipped for exceeding the word limit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal weaponized B's concession about "modest premiums" for DRM-free media, successfully framing the motion as a state-mandated retroactive upgrade that would tax future consumers.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A wins by successfully reframing digital assets as durable property rather than consumed services, dismantling Side B's physical analogies. A's argument on the default rule of inheritance is highly persuasive, exposing B's position as a trapdoor that requires unusual sophistication from ordinary consumers. While A suffered minor clippings at the end of several turns, the substantive arguments were fully delivered, and A's superior rhetoric and sharp rebuttals on pricing and privacy secured a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the "finished meal" and "gym membership" analogies by distinguishing between time-bound services and durable, replayable files, effectively neutralizing Side B's core conceptual framing.
