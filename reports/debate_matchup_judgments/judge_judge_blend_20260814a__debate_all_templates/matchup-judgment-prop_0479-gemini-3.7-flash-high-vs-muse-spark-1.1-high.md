### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built a sharper, more technically grounded case. Its two decisive moves were (1) the manipulation-timing argument — that a manipulator only needs a distorted price to hold through a brief decision window, so arbitrage correction arriving afterward cannot undo an already-made policy choice — and (2) exposing that PRO's own answer to the counterfactual-pricing question ("unchosen-branch trades are voided") actually concedes that markets never generate a real price for the rejected policy path, gutting PRO's central claim that conditional markets solve the endogeneity/counterfactual problem raised in the opening. PRO's rebuttals (time-weighted averaging, arbitrage self-correction) were reasonable but did not squarely neutralize either point, and PRO's closing did not revisit or repair the conceded gap. PRO still landed a real point about panel capture being cheaper than market manipulation, but CON directly addressed this with the auditability/FOIA/accountability asymmetry, keeping that clash live rather than one-sided. Overall CON progressed the debate more effectively, used the pressure-question round to force a damaging admission, and closed by crystallizing that admission, giving it the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4-A4.5), which seized on PRO's own A1 answer that unchosen-branch trades are voided — showing this means no capital-backed price is ever generated for the rejected counterfactual policy, undermining PRO's core claim that conditional markets solve long-horizon/endogenous policy comparisons.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won a competitive debate by showing that making prices policy-relevant creates manipulation incentives whose external policy payoff can exceed trading losses, while major policies also resist clean settlement and require causal explanations and tradeoff analysis. Side A effectively clarified that markets would supplement rather than replace deliberation, but arbitrage and time-weighted prices did not fully answer sustained distortion or the difficulty of reducing multidimensional policy success to contracts. B overstated the problem with voided conditional branches, which kept the margin moderate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to the arbitrage defense was decisive: a contractor may rationally accept market losses when moving policy protects a vastly larger outside interest, and correction after the decision cannot reverse the policy consequence. A’s time-weighted-window fix reduced spike risk but did not resolve that payoff asymmetry.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON decisively won by exposing fatal logical flaws in PRO's core mechanisms. CON set a brilliant trap regarding conditional markets, forcing PRO to admit that unchosen policy branches are voided, which CON correctly pointed out means no capital is risked to price the counterfactuals needed for ex-ante comparison. Furthermore, CON's manipulation argument was devastating: when outside policy payoffs dwarf market costs, sustaining a distortion across a time-weighted window is trivial for special interests, and arbitrageurs only correct the price after the irreversible policy decision is made. PRO's closing failed to address these decisive blows, relying instead on generic rhetoric about groupthink.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4 and A4.5), where CON weaponizes PRO's own answers to prove that voided counterfactuals yield no comparative information and that time-weighted averages merely lengthen the attack window for highly motivated manipulators without preventing the policy damage.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but PRO adapted more effectively to the live clash. PRO's decision-market mechanism (voiding unchosen bets) directly neutralized CON's "resolution paradox," and CON's counter (strategic gaming to force voiding) was never developed into a working mechanism, especially after PRO noted governments aren't mechanically bound to market prices. On manipulation, PRO offered concrete structural answers (identity verification, subsidized correctors, sustained-loss cost) while CON largely repeated "trivial lobbying overhead" without engaging those specifics. CON's strongest surviving point—that markets can't causally diagnose failure—was real, but PRO's "formal input, not replacement" framing and "forces experts back to work" answer blunted it enough that CON's attempt to spin it as a fatal concession in closing felt more rhetorical than decisive, since PRO had staked that division-of-labor position from the opening. PRO's closing "cost of error" frame gave a cleaner, more memorable decision rule than CON's largely recap-style closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 response to the counterfactual/resolution paradox (splitting into conditional markets that void on the unchosen policy) most damaged CON's opening thesis, and CON never fully recovered a working counter-mechanism against it in later rounds.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A persuasively framed markets as incentive-compatible checks and answered manipulation with the profit available to corrective traders. However, B showed that conditional markets remain distorted by endogenous policy selection and that unchosen contracts cannot be scored. More decisively, A conceded that prices cannot diagnose causes and must send experts back to work, without adequately explaining why governments should consequently demote expert panels as the motion requires. B also answered A’s linked-market proposal by showing how proliferating contracts fragments liquidity and worsens thin-market problems.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4 was decisive: B turned A’s claim that markets merely trigger further expert investigation into a concession that causal expertise remains indispensable, then directly answered the proposed failure-mode markets with the liquidity-fragmentation problem.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by providing a much stronger economic and institutional defense of prediction markets. Side B's rebuttal on manipulation—explaining that deep-pocketed manipulators simply create massive arbitrage opportunities for informed traders, effectively subsidizing their own opposition—decisively neutralized Side A's core attack. Side B also effectively turned Side A's counterfactual and accountability arguments against them by highlighting that experts face zero financial cost for being wrong, whereas market participants have skin in the game. Side A's reliance on the "naked probability" critique failed to overcome Side B's framing of markets as a necessary, calibrated check on expert overconfidence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 on manipulation, where they compared fighting a price-insensitive lobbyist to short-selling an overvalued stock, clearly explaining how subsidies and expected value draw in informed traders to correct the price, dismantling Side A's claim that rational traders would flee.
