Side A argued well on the friction and cost of the mandate, but could not overcome Side B's clear explanation of this "interpretive gap."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 flip of Side A's physician comparator was highly effective, pointing out that physician-delivered results come with a built-in interpretive layer, which is exactly what DTC testing removes and the mandate seeks to replace.

A's emphasis on the "unknown unknowns" problem—where misinterpreting probabilistic data or variants of uncertain significance prevents users from seeking post-result guidance—neutered B's reliance on consent forms and voluntary follow-up. A also effectively turned B's physician comparator, arguing that counseling simply replaces the interpretive layer DTC testing removes. While B raised valid concerns about friction, counselor scarcity, and regulatory scope, A adequately addressed these as manageable implementation costs outweighed by the severe, predictable harms of unguided genetic data delivery. A's case was more epistemically grounded, directly resolved the central clash, and offered a cleaner comparative framework. B's autonomy arguments were competent but too generic to overcome the specific comprehension hazards A highlighted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 exchange turning the physician comparator and dismantling the consent-form defense. A sharply argued that "comprehension is not a signature" and that DTC testing specifically removes the clinical interpretive layer, making pre-result counseling a necessary functional replacement rather than paternalistic gatekeeping. This directly undermined B's autonomy framework and voluntary opt-in model, leaving B without a workable mechanism to protect users who misread complex genetic data.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by making the central comparison clearer: support for interpretation may be valuable, but Side A did not prove it must be a mandatory gate before results. Side A had a plausible timing argument about the unsupported interval, but it never fully answered why mandatory counseling changes behavior better than strong contextual delivery, especially given supply and autonomy costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal was decisive: it undercut the prophylactic-surgery example by pointing to existing clinical confirmatory safeguards, then reframed the debate as support versus compulsion rather than support versus abandonment.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A wins by cleanly dismantling Side B's impact scenarios. Side A points out that extreme harms like prophylactic surgery are already prevented by clinical oversight, forcing Side B to retreat to minor harms like alerting family members or changing screening schedules. Side A effectively argues that these minor choices do not justify mandatory gatekeeping, carrying the "valuable is not compulsory" frame through to the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 sequence dismantling the harms: first showing that clinical medicine prevents irreversible surgery without confirmation, and then explicitly calling out Side B's retreat to "alerting a relative" as a weak justification for paternalistic gatekeeping.

PRO's initial claim that raw results lead to irreversible surgery was effectively neutralized by CON's point that clinical medicine already requires confirmatory testing. When PRO retreated to preventing screening changes or family alerts, CON successfully framed these as reasonable autonomous choices rather than catastrophic harms. CON's consistent framing of "valuable is not compulsory" and the comparison between mandatory gatekeeping versus informed delivery provided a clearer, less coercive path to addressing misinterpretation, while PRO relied on optimistic assumptions about supply expansion and unproven claims about mandatory timing guaranteeing comprehension.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 decisively neutralized PRO's core harm scenario by explaining that no surgeon operates on raw DTC results without clinical confirmation, forcing PRO to retreat to weaker harms. CON then turned PRO's concession on telehealth into a winning comparison, showing that the proposed support mechanisms work better as voluntary resources than as mandatory gates.
