- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides built plausible, well-grounded cases without fabricated evidence, and both used the pressure round effectively. The decisive exchange was B's concession that a blackmail report should outrank a bad date "regardless of payment" (B2.5), which A correctly identified as fatal to CON's triage-by-subscription model. A exploited this cleanly in Rebuttal 2 and the closing, showing that if severity should govern response, gating the actual countermeasure (device-level blocking) behind a paywall is incoherent — the victim is "first in line" but still blocked from the tool without paying. B's attempted escape (queue-ordering vs. provisioning) never resolved why a top-priority blackmail victim should still need a subscription to get the actual protection, leaving that route live for A. B's strongest unanswered point — that free, common verification stigmatizes and effectively pressures marginalized users who refuse ID — was a good original angle, but A's closing, though clipped by the word-limit format note, had already begun dismantling the core funding logic and framed the debate's central asymmetry ("pricing survival") memorably before being cut off. That clipping is a real execution cost for A, but it does not erase the substantive ground already gained. On balance, A forced the more damaging concession and controlled the central clash more effectively, giving it a clear but not dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's exploitation of B's concession that severity should trump payment (B2.5/B4.1) versus B's unresolved distinction between "queue ordering" and "actual provisioning" of the safety tool — this exchange most directly exposed the core tension in CON's model.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by separating free access from automatic, unscreened intervention and showing that safety can be triaged by threat while funded through non-safety purchases. B’s clipped closing and mistaken C-reference labels were execution costs, but not enough to erase its clearer comparative case.

It directly accommodated screening and severity triage, while Side A continued rebutting an unlimited on-demand model the proposition did not require.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases, but CON's throughline proved more resilient. PRO's strongest weapons—equity for vulnerable users and the "network contagion" idea that a paid safety tier does nothing while the free pool stays a hunting ground—were powerful but never fully answered the funding-mechanism question CON kept raising: where does the money for genuine document verification, manual fraud review, and cross-device ban enforcement come from if the "best" versions must be free? CON also caught PRO quietly shrinking "identity verification" from ID checks to a cheap liveness selfie once the cost problem was pressed, a definitional retreat PRO never fully owned. The decisive moment came in the final exchange: PRO's closing spun CON's Rebuttal 2 concession ("a fee alone doesn't stop...") into "CON finally agreed, that's the whole debate," but CON's closing cleanly exposed this as spin ("if a fee is not the mechanism, removing it is not one either"), reasserting that free-by-default removes the funding for enforcement without creating any replacement capacity. That rebuttal-of-the-rebuttal was sharper and more damaging than anything PRO offered in response, giving CON the last clean word on the central mechanism dispute.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing turn on PRO's "that's the whole debate" spin—"If a fee is not the safety mechanism, removing the fee is not one either"—directly defused PRO's most aggressive late move and reasserted CON's funding-mechanism argument at the point it mattered most.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making safety a network-wide baseline obligation and showing that paywalls do not stop unverified scammers from targeting free users. Side A compellingly explained that robust enforcement is costly, but repeatedly assumed premium safety fees are the necessary funding source and never adequately answered B’s alternative of cross-subsidizing safety through boosts, filters, and ordinary platform revenue. B’s account of broad adoption making suspicious behavior easier to detect provided the cleaner comparative path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B most decisively exploited A’s concession that charging users does not itself stop an unverified account from messaging hundreds. The “subscriber bunker inside an open hunting ground” framing directly undermined A’s funding model, while A never established why safety teams must be financed specifically by withholding the strongest protections from free users.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing that safety on dating apps is inherently networked: paywalling verification and blocking leaves the majority free-user pool vulnerable, which undermines protection even for subscribers. A effectively rebutted B's cost concerns by showing baseline verification and blocking are cheap to automate, and by offering alternative enforcement mechanisms and funding through standard operating costs and luxury features. B's funding argument remained abstract and never solved the structural problem of an unverified free pool that predators could exploit at scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was over the "hunting ground" problem: A's pressure question and repeated rebuttals showed that CON's premium model still allowed one unverified scammer to message hundreds of free users, rendering even subscriber safety theatrical, while PRO's free-default model made mass unverified activity anomalous and freeze-worthy.
