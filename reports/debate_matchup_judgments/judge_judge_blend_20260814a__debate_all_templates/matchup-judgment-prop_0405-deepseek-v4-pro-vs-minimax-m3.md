### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B wins a competitive but clear debate on two decisive clashes. First, causation: B's argument that sidewalk riding is precisely the foreseeable risk the geofencing is meant to prevent undermines A's "intervening criminal act" framing. A's intervening-cause doctrine and the stolen-car/Toyota analogy are weaker in a context where the company actively deploys devices into public space knowing casual misuse is routine—unlike a manufacturer whose product is stolen. Second, and most decisively, B's Rebuttal 2 A2 answer turns A's own proposed fix against it: mandatory rider identity verification and insurance at registration is itself a form of expanded operator gatekeeping responsibility. B argues persuasively that this concedes the operator is the indispensable node—gatekeeping only works if it carries a genuine cost for failure, i.e., legal liability. A never squarely answers this turn; it restates the regulation-not-litigation distinction without explaining why a company would invest in real enforcement if there is no legal downside for inadequate gatekeeping. A's strongest live argument—mobility loss—remains somewhat speculative, relying on a vague reference to 1980s liability chilling without enough grounding to decide the comparison. Both sides had speeches clipped, affecting them roughly equally, so no penalty is assigned to either. B's overall construction is tighter: each rebuttal narrows the clash rather than widening it, and the closing crystallizes the control-cost alignment argument coherently.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 A2 answer is the most decisive exchange. B demonstrates that A's proposed alternative—mandatory rider ID and insurance verification at the point of rental—is itself a form of expanded operator responsibility, making A's argument self-defeating. A's gatekeeping-without-liability distinction is never explained well enough to survive this turn: without legal exposure for failures of enforcement, operators have no financial incentive to make the gatekeeping genuine rather than perfunctory. This move turns A's best constructive argument into evidence for the proposition.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate. B had a strong rider-agency frame and useful analogies, but often narrowed the case to extreme deliberate evasion by riders. A better defended the motion as more responsibility, not sole or automatic liability, and tied operator control, profit, foreseeable sidewalk misuse, prevention incentives, and victim compensation into one coherent standard. B's insurance/regulation alternative helped, but A persuasively argued it still depends on operator gatekeeping and records.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered proximate cause by tying the injury to the operator leaving a controllable scooter active in a restricted zone, then turned B's rider-insurance proposal into a concession that the operator is the indispensable gatekeeper for identification and prevention.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won the clash on causation and control by distinguishing micromobility from traditional vehicles, emphasizing the operator's real-time ability to kill throttles. Side B's defense leaned on an implausible scenario of riders causing crashes by carrying 'locked' scooters, which failed to refute Side A's core point that companies actively choose to leave motors live in restricted zones. Side A also effectively neutralized Side B's regulatory alternative by arguing that gatekeeping without liability lacks actual enforcement teeth.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly dispatched Side B's 'mandatory insurance/ID' alternative by arguing that without tort liability, operator gatekeeping is just a 'paperwork exercise' with no incentive for accuracy.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins on three converging grounds. First, PRO correctly scoped the motion throughout: "more responsibility" means shared fault, not exclusive liability—the rider stays on the hook. CON spent significant energy attacking exclusive liability, which the motion never demanded. That is a real drift from the proposition as written. Second, PRO's handling of the analogies held up better. CON's best response was that dram-shop liability requires knowledge of a specific patron's visible intoxication, whereas scooter companies face an anonymous public. PRO's A1 cleanly answered this: bars don't need to identify which specific patron will crash, only that serving visibly intoxicated people as a class creates foreseeable risk. Statistical foreseeability from a company's own trip data is structurally identical. CON's counter—"3% riding on curbs is not a specific identifiable hazard"—is weaker because the whole premise of shared-fault liability is class-level foreseeability, not individual prediction. Third, CON was clipped in three out of five substantive speeches (Opening, Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2), creating real execution costs: arguments that might have sharpened the superseding-cause doctrine or narrowed the geofencing dispute were simply never delivered. PRO was clipped only in its closing. PRO's pressure questions were sharper (trip-data foreseeability, what duty an imperfect tool actually creates), and PRO answered CON's questions with clean A1/A2 labels and direct responses. CON's "first mistake vs. last mistake" distinction was never fully neutralized, and the "rider is the proximate and moral wrongdoer" line, while rhetorically crisp, does not defeat the well-established multi-party causation framework PRO consistently invoked. On balance, PRO maintained a cleaner route to victory across the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 [B4.1–B4.2]: directly answered CON's "person-specific knowledge" challenge by showing that dram-shop liability never required identifying the specific patron who would crash—only that serving intoxicated people as a class creates foreseeable risk. This dismantled CON's sharpest attack on the analogies and reframed foreseeability as statistical and class-based, which fit the trip-data argument PRO had already planted in its pressure question.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): A won by keeping the motion modest: more shared responsibility, not exclusive liability. A showed foreseeable risk, company control over deployment and speed tools, and victim compensation, while B too often answered a stronger version of the motion as automatic insurer liability. B’s points about rider fault and imperfect geofencing were substantial, but did not fully defeat A’s reasonable-care and shared-causation framework.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange was answering the superseding-act claim with foreseeability plus control: if companies know from their own operations that sidewalk riding predictably occurs in chosen zones, rider illegality does not automatically end upstream responsibility.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B won by consistently dismantling Side A's legal analogies. Side B successfully argued that dram shop laws and negligent hiring require specific knowledge of an immediate hazard, whereas a scooter company only has aggregate statistical data, making the rider's intentional act a superseding cause. Despite several speeches clipping at the end, B's core logic remained intact and decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2 distinction between statistical foreseeability and person-specific knowledge, which cleanly neutralized Side A's dram shop and negligent hiring analogies.
