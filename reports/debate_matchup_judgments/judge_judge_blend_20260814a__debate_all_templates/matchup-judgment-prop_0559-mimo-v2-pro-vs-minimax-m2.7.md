### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): PRO wins by a clear but competitive margin. PRO's core harm narrative—users trapped by lock-in with no structural exit—was established early and CON never seriously contested it or offered an alternative. The incumbents-lobbying-against point (A3.1) was PRO's sharpest move: it exposed a genuine contradiction in CON's startup argument that CON never fully repaired. Saying "fear is a lower bar than proving startups benefit" concedes the question of incumbent incentives without answering why compliance costs would simultaneously crush new entrants and be opposed by the very incumbents who would benefit. PRO's OAuth/GDPR answer to the security challenge was plausible and grounded. CON's strongest moment was the "mechanism defeats itself" argument in B4—using PRO's own moderation concession to claim portability can't simultaneously solve zero-network problems and allow platform filtering. This was rhetorically effective and genuinely sharp. But PRO's answer was adequate: filtering specific banned actors is a narrow exception, not a rejection of the entire portability value. CON's claim that "the actual network effect stays behind" overstates what filtering entails. The email open-protocol distinction (B2.9) correctly noted a disanalogy, but the directional precedent that interoperability does not destroy industries remained live. CON's social-graph-is-not-a-phone-number point was valid but didn't neutralize the underlying lock-in argument. Both closings were clipped, creating a symmetric execution cost. On balance, PRO's undamaged core harm plus the lobbyist contradiction outweigh CON's clever but overstated self-defeat argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question B3.1 and subsequent Rebuttal 2 usage of PRO's own moderation concession was the most technically sharp exchange. CON argued that if receiving platforms can filter audience imports, portability no longer solves the zero-network problem and the mandate "defeats itself." PRO's answer—that filtering targets only specific bad actors, not ordinary users—was responsive and adequate, but the exchange meaningfully narrowed what portability actually delivers. It forced PRO to defend a more modest version of the mandate, which is the right kind of pressure a pressure question should generate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON)): PRO built a more durable path to victory across the full exchange. Its three core claims—co-creation, historical innovation precedent, and the chilling-effect argument—survived the exchange better than CON's did. The chilling-effect point (A4.1: competition disciplines platforms for all users, not only those who switch) was PRO's most decisive move; CON never directly answered why the minority/majority framing neutralized a systemic market-structure argument. PRO's GDPR and financial-sector references gave concrete grounding to the feasibility claim, whereas CON's parallel ("early internet mandated access discouraged investment") remained vague and was never substantiated beyond assertion. CON's coral-reef analogy was the debate's most vivid and intellectually honest moment, and PRO never fully proved that portability standards can preserve contextual value rather than merely transfer a contact list. That gap was real. However, PRO correctly noted that even imperfect portability creates competitive pressure where none now exists, and CON's response—that portability would deliver users a "hollow shell"—overstated the case by assuming degraded equals worthless. PRO's employer analogy in Q1 was sharp; CON's response (platforms build holistic ecosystems, employers don't) was adequate but not decisive. On balance, PRO answered the most important pressure more directly, maintained a coherent normative frame throughout, and left CON's strongest argument (ecosystem value) landing as a reason to design standards carefully rather than as a reason to reject the mandate entirely. Both closings were clipped, creating equal execution costs. Slight deduction for PRO's opening being clipped, but this did not affect outcome materially.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.1 response to CON's Q1 was the most decisive exchange. CON's question tried to frame mandatory portability as a minority fix imposing costs on a satisfied majority. PRO's answer reframed competition as a systemic discipline that benefits all users regardless of switching behavior—a market-structure argument that CON never squarely answered. This turned CON's most pointed pressure into an own-goal: the majority's apparent satisfaction under lock-in is precisely what PRO's policy is meant to change, not evidence against it.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON)): Side A wins by systematically resolving Side B's practical objections with strong real-world comparisons (financial APIs, GDPR, phone numbers) and by exposing a logical tension in Side B's case. Side A rightly points out that if a ported social graph is truly rendered a "hollow shell" without the original platform's context (Side B's "coral reef" analogy), then platforms shouldn't fear competitors successfully stealing their core asset. Side A also delivers a decisive answer on how the mere threat of exit disciplines platforms to the benefit of all users.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1 in Rebuttal 2 cleanly neutralizes the "minority imposes costs on the majority" argument by explaining that the latent threat of competition disciplines the market on behalf of everyone, even those who stay.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins because it established that social graphs are dynamic, platform-dependent processes whose value is destroyed by extraction, not static user assets like phone numbers; this core distinction survived every exchange, including direct answers to B's pressure questions, while B's analogies repeatedly failed under scrutiny and its key speeches were clipped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2's coral-reef rebuttal plus A4's answers to the employer and standards questions together neutralized B's main routes to victory.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON)): Side A wins because it built the cleaner path to victory: it framed complexity as a solvable implementation challenge rather than a disqualifying barrier, showed that competitive benefits extend to all users through disciplining incumbents, and directly answered CON's security and proportionality concerns with concrete analogies. CON's coral-reef argument was vivid, but PRO's employer analogy exposed an unstable philosophical foundation in CON's investment-ownership framework, and PRO's sharper handling of the Pressure Questions round resolved live burdens more decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange around A's Pressure Question Q1 (employer analogy): it forced CON to special-plead for platforms, undermining the coherence of CON's claim that infrastructure investment entitles permanent ownership of user relationships.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): PRO wins by successfully framing the debate around user agency and market competition, effectively turning CON’s "investment security" argument into coercive lock-in. PRO’s handling of the security and complexity objections was stronger, pointing to existing regulated frameworks and standards bodies to show feasibility, while CON’s "coral reef" analogy, though sticky, became repetitive and failed to neutralize PRO’s point that the mere option to exit disciplines platforms for all users. PRO’s rebuttals were sharper, directly answered pressure questions with clear weighing, and offered a cleaner comparative path to victory centered on competition over quality versus captivity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 directly answered CON’s pressure questions by introducing the "chilling effect" and explaining how exit options discipline platforms even for non-switching users, while effectively neutralizing the security objection by comparing mandated portability to existing audited financial API frameworks. This turn successfully shifted the weighing from technical feasibility to market structure and user leverage.
