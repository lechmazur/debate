### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Both sides executed the format well and engaged directly with pressure questions, but B built the more resilient case. B converted A's concession that "no objective boundary" exists between journalism and entertainment into a decisive reductio: if that's true, A's defense of accountability journalism necessarily also shields the most exploitative lurid retellings, undercutting A's own civic-value framing. B also answered the prior-restraint bind cleanly by distinguishing pre-publication veto from post-release liability/notice duties (an established legal pattern in defamation and privacy law), which survived A's "licensing regime" rhetoric without a substantive rebuttal of the pre/post distinction. A's strongest counter—warning that B's survivor-representative and profit-sharing apparatus is itself a quasi-censorship bureaucracy—was rhetorically forceful but didn't dismantle B's narrower, survivor-centered standing rule that explicitly excluded complicit relatives and preserved a public-interest exemption. On the "remedies gap" question, A did name real torts (IIED, public disclosure of private facts) in rebuttal 2, which B's closing somewhat overstated as unanswered, but B's underlying point that truthful dramatization without newsworthiness defenses can slip through was never squarely closed off by A. Overall B narrowed and defended a coherent middle position more successfully than A rebutted it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's turn 4 (A1/A2) answering A's pressure questions—distinguishing post-release liability from prior restraint and limiting profit-sharing/standing to survivor-designated representatives—directly neutralized A's strongest structural objection and forced A into rhetorical escalation rather than substantive refutation in the final rebuttal.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) demonstrated a concrete, recurrent harm and defended a coherent, limited reform package—notice, survivor-centered participation, narrow post-release liability, and public-interest exemptions—that survived Side A’s prior-restraint and censorship objections. Side A (CON) landed serious concerns about bureaucratic overreach and perverse incentives, but never reconciled its insistence that no line exists between journalism and entertainment with its desire to limit protection to civic-minded work, allowing B to force it into defending the genre’s most exploitative outputs. Crucially, when pressed to name a law covering truthful dramatizations of public records, A could only cite torts explicitly limited to “private, non-record facts,” effectively conceding the core remedies gap and retreating to the claim that the gap is a “foundational principle.” That concession left B with the live route to victory on the motion’s threshold.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Pressure Question Q2 and the subsequent exchange over the “remedies gap,” where Side A could not name any law that reaches truthful use of public records such as 911 calls or trial transcripts, and was forced to defend the absence of a remedy as a principled feature of open inquiry rather than answer the pressure.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO wins on the decisive clash: CON admits it must defend the entire spectrum of true crime because no line exists between journalism and entertainment [B4.1], which PRO leverages to show CON protects the most lurid uses, while CON fails to name a remedy for truthful dramatizations using 911/rape material [A3.2/B4.2]. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built its case around a single, well-executed structural claim: any enforceable rule specific enough to stop "commodification" will inevitably function as a veto over truthful, public-record speech, exercised either by resourced families or risk-averse platforms. CON's pressure question (conflicting family consent) forced PRO into an answer in Rebuttal 2 that actually confirmed this critique — PRO's own remedy (removing "sensationalized, graphic re-enactments," restricting an objecting relative's likeness) is precisely the content-based editorial control CON predicted, even as PRO insisted this wasn't censorship. CON exploited this self-contradiction decisively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing. PRO never resolved the tension between "public reporting proceeds unimpeded" and "producers must remove content a family calls gratuitous." PRO's case also leaned on an overstated "zero recourse" claim that CON specifically rebutted with plausible existing doctrines (intrusion, false implication, harassment/stalking law), and PRO's later speeches cited nonexistent reference IDs (e.g., [C2.4], [P1.3], [P2.5]), a real grounding/discipline flaw. CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly; PRO's closing largely reasserted earlier framing without repairing the internal contradiction exposed by the pressure exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3–A4.4), pointing out that PRO's own answer to the pressure question — demanding removal of "graphic re-enactments" and likeness restrictions after claiming disagreement wouldn't silence reporting — is itself the content-based veto CON warned about, turning PRO's clarifying answer into a self-inflicted concession.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by exposing that PRO could not design a constraint regime that stopped exploitation without converting family grief into a de facto veto over truthful public-record speech. PRO offered market-failure framing and moral urgency, but when pressed to specify enforceable rules (notification, duty of care, likeness limits), CON showed those standards necessarily require platforms to make content-based editorial judgments that risk-averse studios would over-comply with, chilling valuable investigative and cold-case coverage. CON consistently distinguished actual legal wrongs (defamation, intrusion, harassment, doxxing) from the protected speech boundary of accurate dramatization that causes anguish, and used PRO's own R2 examples to confirm the veto problem. PRO's treatment of this as a concession rather than a principled limit weakened its comparative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question 2 (A3.2) and the subsequent R2 response (A4.3–A4.4) forced PRO to specify enforceable rules, after which CON demonstrated that PRO's own examples—removing "sensationalized, graphic re-enactments" and restricting unconsenting likenesses—are exactly the content-based editorial judgments that function as family vetoes, collapsing PRO's distinction between public-interest journalism and commercial exploitation.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): PRO kept a live route that CON never neutralized: routine commercial re-dramatization against explicit family objections is not covered by defamation, privacy, or IIED, CON admits this as a speech boundary [B2.4][B4.1] and offers only counter-speech/boycotts. Because the motion is ethical rules OR legal constraints, PRO's market-failure + duty-of-care narrowing (notification, limits on gratuitous likeness/reenactment) satisfies the burden while answering the veto/chilling claim more directly than CON answers the ethical-rules prong.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.2-A2.3 exposing the legal void plus A4.4 forcing CON to concede in B4.1 that truthful traumatic dramatization has zero legal recourse, turning CON's speech-boundary defense into admission of the harm PRO needs.
