### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-argued cases, but A's case held together more consistently across the exchange. A's strongest move was reframing the cost-benefit question using the motion's own language ("even at major commercial cost"), which blunted CON's repeated emphasis on commercial devastation as if that alone defeated the resolution, and A's reversal of the "boomerang" argument (unused leverage is a bluff; use the chokepoint before self-sufficiency locks in; adversary's transition was coming anyway) was never fully answered. CON landed a genuinely sharp rebuttal by turning PRO's own opening line (equipment hard to smuggle, chips are not) into a structural bind on the mechanism, and its civilian/military market split in Rebuttal 2 elegantly resolved the "legacy nodes suffice yet losing sales is catastrophic" tension A pressed. But CON's overall case developed an internal inconsistency: it argued indigenous fab drives are mostly civilian/macroeconomic (undercutting its own "boomerang" claim that controls specifically catalyze a dangerous competitor) while also conceding frontier accelerators are needed for training even if deployment uses legacy chips (undercutting the claim that the equipment chokepoint is militarily irrelevant). A's closing pressed the "can't have it both ways" charge, and while it didn't explicitly dismantle CON's civilian/military split, CON's own answers left enough tension unresolved that A's chokepoint-and-urgency framing came through as the more stable, decision-relevant story.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The pivotal exchange was CON turning PRO's own admission ("unlike finished chips, these machines are not easy to smuggle") into a weapon against the equipment-restriction mechanism (B2.3-B2.4), met by PRO's scale-based response distinguishing sustainable industrial fabrication from narrow, interceptable gray-market trickle, combined with PRO's reframing that the resolution already stipulates accepting major commercial cost (A4.3), which shifted the debate onto ground more favorable to PRO.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending the motion’s modest burden: meaningful delay rather than perfect denial. Its industrial-scale distinction plausibly showed why smuggled chips, cloud access, and legacy nodes may enable experiments or limited systems without replacing a sovereign, maintainable frontier supply base. Side A’s civilian-versus-military market distinction was sharp and answered the alleged contradiction, but its victory still depended on categorical claims that a few thousand easily smuggled accelerators suffice, legacy nodes impose no meaningful military constraint, and restrictions would effectively bankrupt exporters. Those claims were less grounded and overstated a targeted restriction as a blanket embargo. B also weighed temporary commercial losses against an irreversible loss of the fabrication chokepoint more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal most decisively answered the bypass argument: leakage may support demonstrations, but irregular, revocable access cannot sustain replacement, updates, deployment, and continued advancement at military scale. This preserved a credible slowdown even after conceding imperfect denial.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by convincing showing that restricting manufacturing equipment fails to slow adversarial military AI and actively harms the restricting government's own security. CON established a clean alternative pathway: adversaries can smuggle the small volume of finished frontier chips needed for training and rely on unrestricted legacy nodes for battlefield deployment, rendering the equipment control militarily irrelevant. It further showed that the "major commercial cost" would starve national champions of civilian-driven R&D revenue while snapping the dependency tether and accelerating adversarial self-sufficiency. PRO never adequately overcame the training-versus-deployment distinction or the civilian-versus-military market separation, leaving its core causal mechanism unpersuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's revelation that finished frontier chips—already conceded by PRO to be far more smugglable than equipment—could fulfill an adversary's low-volume training needs, while legacy nodes handled deployment, bypassing the need for indigenous leading-edge fabs entirely.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and executed the pressure-question format cleanly. PRO's opening laid out a clear chokepoint mechanism and framed the tradeoff as security-over-commerce, and its rebuttals were direct. However, under pressure PRO leaned on increasingly precise but ungrounded technical claims ("degradation in weeks, not years," "two to three years to obsolescence," "hundreds of billions" in subsidies) that read as brittle specificity rather than well-supported reasoning. CON's case was more disciplined: it consistently attacked the motion's actual advertised benefit (a clean 5-10 year strategic delay), built a layered account of leakage (existing fabs, gray-market service, stockpiling, laundered compute, forced-but-effective domestic substitution), and tied the commercial cost directly back into the security ledger by noting that global revenue funds allied R&D. The decisive moment came in the closings: PRO tried to redefine success as "mere friction is enough," and CON's closing explicitly and correctly called this a goalpost shift away from the originally promised decisive delay, reasserting the clean weighing frame of "certain major cost vs. uncertain, porous benefit." PRO never squarely answered this reframing charge. CON's cost-benefit framing was clearer, more consistent across rounds, and left the judge an easier standard to apply.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing identification that PRO's own admissions of adaptation, laundering, and degraded-but-functioning fabs amounted to "friction, not the clean developmental break needed to justify major mandated commercial loss," directly exposing PRO's shift from a promised 5-10 year delay to a vaguer "asymmetric advantage" standard.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): B won because the proposition requires slowing development, not achieving airtight denial. A persuasively identified leakage and commercial harm, but its workarounds still entailed costly fragmentation, degraded production, stockpiling, and domestic substitution—the strategic friction B sought. B more clearly weighed that delay against military risk, though its unsupported claims about degradation occurring within weeks and rapid obsolescence kept the result competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s closing most effectively turned A’s circumvention case: reliance on laundered cloud compute, impaired fabs, and emergency substitution demonstrated slowdown rather than disproving it. A’s attempt to require a clean five-to-ten-year break imposed a stronger threshold than the motion itself.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON showed that the promised 5–10 year strategic delay would be eroded by existing installed capacity, stockpiling, compute laundering through third-country clouds, and gray-market maintenance, while the commercial costs to allied R&D and innovation were certain and substantial. PRO’s causal chain relied on optimistic assumptions about rapid machine degradation and adversary inability to adapt, which CON blurred with plausible workarounds. PRO’s closing retreat to “friction is enough” conceded the decisive delay claim, and CON crystallized the clash as certain self-harm versus uncertain, porous slowdown.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s answers to PRO’s pressure questions in Rebuttal 2 [A4.1–A4.2], introducing the compute-laundering mechanism and residual-service maintenance to undermine the “clean strategic pause” narrative.
