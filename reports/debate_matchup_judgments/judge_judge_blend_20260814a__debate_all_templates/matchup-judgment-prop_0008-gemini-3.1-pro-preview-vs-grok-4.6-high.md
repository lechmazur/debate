### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON built a consistent, mechanistic case: a generational ban keeps the product legally on shelves while permanently splitting equally competent adults, and that split creates a durable "legal reservoir" (older siblings, partners, coworkers, and eventually 35-year-old peers) that undermines the claimed immunization effect. PRO's central rebuttal—that shattering the "peer-to-peer" pipeline prevents addiction from ever forming—never squarely answered CON's repeated point that initiation and supply also run through siblings, workplaces, bars, and partners, channels that don't shrink as the banned cohort ages. CON's Rebuttal 2 also exposed a real tension in PRO's position: grandfathering is defended as pragmatic harm-avoidance for already-addicted adults, yet PRO simultaneously claims the newly-banned cohort simply won't seek proxies because they were never hooked — but some in that cohort will still initiate through non-peer channels, and PRO's closing never reconciled this. PRO's rhetoric (biochemical trap, "manufactured demand," dismissing the two-tier checkout as trivial aesthetics) was vivid but didn't neutralize the enforceability critique, and the closing mostly restated the peer-pipeline claim rather than repairing the exposed gap. CON's pressure question and follow-up answers were tightly targeted and directly resolved the live tension, giving it the cleaner, more defensible path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.4 in Rebuttal 2, catching the contradiction between PRO's "pragmatic grandfathering" rationale and PRO's claim that the banned cohort simply won't develop demand — a tension PRO never resolved in the following speeches.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won by giving the clearer comparative mechanism: a generational ban progressively separates young initiators from convenient legal suppliers, reducing addiction before durable adult demand develops. Side A compellingly identified leakage, unequal treatment, and a lawful older reservoir, but mostly showed that the ban would be imperfect rather than that it would perform worse than conventional age restrictions. B also outweighed the fairness concern against preventing addiction and premature death, though its claims of eradication were overstated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange concerned future adult demand. A argued that excluded 25- and 30-year-olds would sustain proxy and illicit markets; B answered that most would never develop that demand because access was restricted during the usual initiation period. A identified alternative supply routes but did not establish that they negate the ban's increased friction or comparative benefit.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing a live contradiction in PRO's case and showing that equal regulation is more coherent. PRO justified grandfathering by warning that abruptly cutting off dependent adults would ignite a black market, yet insisted that a legal reservoir beside permanently excluded adults would not create proxy demand because the banned cohort would never get addicted. CON demonstrated that initiation pathways extend beyond immediate peers and that PRO's own logic implied the generational ban would subsidize the very leaks it claimed to eliminate. PRO never resolved this tension, leaving CON's argument that "two adulthoods cannot be enforced" as the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (Turn 8), where it showed that PRO's pragmatic justification for grandfathering to avoid black-market withdrawal is incompatible with their claim that a still-legal older market beside excluded adults will not generate proxy sales, since some in the banned cohort will still initiate through non-peer pathways.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-defended cases, but PRO's mechanism was more directly responsive to the motion's "phase out" language: existing smokers retain access while the legal customer base is capped, giving a definite endpoint that CON's "historic lows via conventional tools" never quite matches (PRO's "historic lows still restock the market" line went largely unanswered). PRO also handled the two hardest pressure points more effectively than CON handled its own: it neutralized the autonomy/equality attack by reframing the policy as product retirement rather than an incapacity judgment ("the 40-year-old is not being judged wiser; they are being allowed to finish a habit the state already sold them"), and it drew a workable distinction between sporadic straw-purchase leakage and the sustained, repeat-customer supply chain needed to fuel a real black market. CON's strongest shot—accusing PRO of contradiction between "nicotine hooks fast" and "a stray pack won't create black-market demand"—was a sharp and repeated pressure point, but PRO's answer (scale/duration of customer relationship matters, not mere exposure) blunted it enough that it never became decisive. CON's autonomy argument stayed rhetorically vivid across rounds but didn't evolve to counter PRO's retirement-analogy reframe, leaving that clash resolved in PRO's favor by the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's "shop that can sell to the same person every week for decades" distinction, offered in response to CON's black-market/straw-purchaser attack and reinforced through the A1/A2 answers, most effectively contained CON's strongest and most repeated line of attack.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the central comparison clearer: conventional restrictions eventually convert experimentation into lawful, routine demand, while a generational ban permanently closes that transition and shrinks legal supply over time. Side A raised real autonomy and illicit-market concerns, but repeatedly assumed that leakage would become a full replacement market without sufficiently proving that scale. Its claim that even one leaked pack creates a lifelong illicit customer also overstated addiction and weakened its contradiction argument. B directly answered both pressure questions and offered the cleaner weighing between imperfect enforcement and endless legal replenishment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that leakage already exists under conventional rules, but only conventional rules later turn those users into permanent legal customers, most decisively answered A’s black-market case. A’s “older neighbor becomes the shop” line was vivid, yet it did not overcome B’s point that this supply is riskier, less scalable, and diminishes as eligible cohorts age out.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built and sustained a clearer path to victory by showing that conventional age limits structurally restock the tobacco market, while a generational ban closes the conveyor belt of legal customers. Side A directly engaged Side B’s strongest objections: the black-market fear was blunted by distinguishing occasional leakage from a replaceable customer base, and the autonomy objection was reframed as product retirement rather than a permanent age-based caste. Side B repeated the “contradiction” and “equal rights” frames without fully answering Side A’s structural “permission slip” argument, leaving Side B’s rebuttals less decisive in the later rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal, which distinguished a “stray pack” from a sustainable weekly customer base and reframed equal adulthood as compatible with product retirement, decisively blunted Side B’s black-market and equal-rights attacks.
