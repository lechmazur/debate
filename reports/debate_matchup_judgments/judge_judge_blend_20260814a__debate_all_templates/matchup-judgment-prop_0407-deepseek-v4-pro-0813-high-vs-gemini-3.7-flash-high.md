### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but A built a more coherent and durable framework: a burden-shifting "material work-nexus" standard with explicit carve-outs for safety, confidentiality, harassment, and mission subversion. When B offered its strongest hypotheticals (anti-vax nurse, white-nationalist banker, DV-hostile counselor), A successfully folded them into its own exception rather than being refuted by them, which blunted B's central "unworkable standard" attack. B's best moment was the cosmetic-vs-paralytic dilemma in Rebuttal 1 and the closing pounce on A's admission that employers must "absorb" costs from mere disapproval — a real and sharp exploitation of a concession. But A had already pre-limited that admission to non-material "heckler's veto" cases, and its closing reframed the cost-bearing question as the crux the debate should turn on, which is a fair and clarifying response rather than an evasion. B's litigation-cost objection remained a valid but generic critique that could be leveled at almost any burden-shifting reform, and it never landed a clean example that survived A's exception-folding. Both sides answered pressure questions in proper A1/A2 format, though B's answer to A's Q1 was less direct than its crisp "No" to Q2, a minor execution cost. Overall A's case was more resilient under sustained pressure and gave a cleaner throughline for a reader to adopt.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 dilemma ("cosmetic vs. paralytic") was the sharpest single move, but A's Rebuttal 1/2 response of absorbing B's hypotheticals into its own "material effect" exception was the more decisive exchange, since it defused B's most concrete counterexamples and forced B to rely on the more abstract litigation-cost argument for the rest of the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): B kept a clearer live route: a work-nexus burden shift that still allows firing for role-linked trust, safety, or mission harm while blocking mere disapproval. A pressed litigation and association hard and exploited B’s “absorb the hit” language, but never solved its own limit problem on open-ended “team cohesion,” and its dilemma (cosmetic vs paralysis) was repeatedly narrowed rather than broken. B answered pressure more directly and crystallized the cost-allocation clash better.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R2 answers plus weighing: early-stage role-specific nexus vs heckler’s veto, dissolving A’s cosmetic/paralysis dilemma.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins because it demonstrated that CON's own flagship examples would be fireable under PRO's role-linked "material effect" standard, collapsing B's claim that the rule is either cosmetic or paralyzing. A maintained a consistent burden framework—employers must justify firing with work-related harm rather than moral disapproval—while B partially evaded A's pressure question about what lawful conduct is actually safe under at-will rules and never overcame the power imbalance the proposition targets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal [A2.1] showing that CON's examples (anti-vaccine nurse, bank officer, counselor) would be permissible terminations under PRO's standard because they are role-linked, which neutralized B's "unworkable dilemma" before it could solidify.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): CON identified the debate's real fault line early — that "materially affects job performance" is either so broad it swallows the protection (reputational/trust harm counts) or so narrow it forces employers to absorb foreseeable-but-unmeasured harm until courts rule years later — and sustained this dilemma through pressure questions and both rebuttal rounds. PRO's answers (burden on employer, "objective nexus," role-based clarity) never squarely resolved the timing problem CON kept raising: employers must decide before harm is measurable, and workers can't know in advance which category their conduct falls into. PRO's principled framing ("labor, not private life") remained compelling as a value claim but did not close the operational gap CON exploited, especially the ex-ante/ex-post bind restated cleanly in CON's closing. CON's closing crystallized the clash decisively while PRO's closing largely restated principle without directly meeting the standing dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's dilemma in [B2.7], pressed via Q1/Q2 in Pressure Questions and reaffirmed in Rebuttal 2 ([B4.3]-[B4.5]) and Closing ([B5.1]-[B5.2]): if "material" includes foreseeable trust/reputation harm, protection shrinks to nothing; if it excludes prospective harm, employers must absorb damage until proven in court. PRO's A1/A2 answers in [A4.1]-[A4.2] asserted an "objective nexus" standard but never explained how it functions before harm is measurable, leaving the dilemma unresolved.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by forcing and sustaining a structural dilemma about the motion’s operative term: if “materially affects job performance” includes foreseeable reputation, client trust, and cohesion harms, the promised protection collapses; if it excludes them, employers must absorb real losses or litigate after the fact. PRO had the stronger moral framing and got a narrow unfairness concession on pure zero-harm firings, but never resolved the ex ante decision problem, advance-knowledge chill, or role-fluidity objections. CON made the clash easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s dilemma (A2.7/A4.5/A5.1): material-includes-trust empties the protection; material-excludes-it forces waiting for measurable loss.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): CON wins by exposing a structural dilemma that PRO never resolved: if "materially affects job performance" encompasses foreseeable reputational harm, trust, and cohesion, the promised protection collapses; if it excludes them, employers must absorb losses or litigate preventive judgments. PRO repeatedly asserted materiality is a workable standard but never provided a clear rule for how workers or employers should navigate ex ante uncertainty, leaving its case dependent on an undefended legal optimism. CON conceded the isolated unfairness of zero-harm firings, but showed the proposition as written imports a litigation lottery and perverse screening incentives rather than a clean boundary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's dilemma framing—if material includes prospective trust and cohesion the protection shrinks, if it excludes them employers must suffer measurable loss before acting—was the decisive exchange, raised in Rebuttal 1, reinforced in Rebuttal 2, and never fully answered by PRO.
