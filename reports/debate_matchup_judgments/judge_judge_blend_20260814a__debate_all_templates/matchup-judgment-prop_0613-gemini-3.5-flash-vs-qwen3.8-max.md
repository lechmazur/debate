### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): CON built a sustained, tightening dilemma: any interface broad enough to let users truly leave (portable graph, live block enforcement) is also broad enough for audited third parties to harvest, infer, or misuse relational metadata faster than post-hoc penalties can fix; any interface narrow enough to be safe leaves rivals as "skins" dependent on the incumbent's terms. PRO's analogies (telecom blocking, open banking, email clients) were directly challenged as disanalogous to a live, multi-party social graph, and PRO never fully recovered—its answers to the pressure questions (system-wide block enforcement, tokenized revocable API access) were turned by CON into concessions that either require sprawling incumbent/regulator coordination or fail to stop metadata accumulation before revocation. CON's closing crystallized this either/or clearly, while PRO's closing mostly re-asserted its opening framing ("hostage-taking," "digital cages") without directly resolving the wide/narrow access dilemma CON had built. CON's handling of the third-party consent problem (a connection is jointly created, not one user's unilateral property) also went materially unanswered beyond a redefinition to "outbound contact list," which itself did not address group memberships and mutual permissions CON raised.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4-B4.5), which took PRO's own pressure-question answers (system-wide block enforcement, tokenized rate-limited API access with revocation) and reframed them as proof of the core dilemma—either the mandate requires ongoing incumbent/regulator coordination (undermining real independence) or it permits metadata accumulation faster than audits can catch (undermining safety)—was the most decisive exchange, and PRO's closing did not squarely answer it.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON won by making the live clash easier to decide: social graphs are multi-party permission structures, so the mandate faces a real dilemma between access broad enough to matter and access narrow enough to stay safe. PRO’s email/banking analogies and “qualified + audits” answers never neutralized the metadata/consent and post-breach enforcement problems, while CON’s later speeches tightened rather than restated the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON Rebuttal 2 using PRO’s own A1/A2 replies (system-wide block enforcement; tokenized APIs + revocation) to show the mandate either creates sprawling coordination dependence or leaves abuse faster than punishment can reverse.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side B wins because it framed a decisive dilemma Side A never resolved: interoperability broad enough to enable real competition is also broad enough to allow metadata abuse and consent violations, while narrow enough access leaves rivals as dependent skins on incumbent networks. Side B sharply distinguished social graphs from Side A’s phone/email/banking analogies by emphasizing their mutual, dynamic, multi-party nature, and exposed that Side A’s system-wide block-enforcement answer conceded continued incumbent coordination. Side A’s user-sovereignty rhetoric was potent but never squarely answered how retrospective audits could prospectively prevent metadata harvesting, and it hurt its own credibility by citing nonexistent reference IDs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 (B4.4–B4.6), where it turned Side A’s block-enforcement concession against PRO to show the mandate preserves incumbent control, then crystallized the access-vs-safety double bind: if the interface is broad enough to route around the incumbent, it is abusable; if narrow enough to prevent abuse, rivals remain frontend dependents.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but PRO more successfully answered pressure. PRO's opening framed lock-in as the core harm and proposed scoped, revocable "handles" rather than full data exports, then used the pressure round to offer concrete mechanisms (security-based qualification criteria excluding bad actors, encrypted-envelope/public-key exchange for E2EE) that directly neutralized CON's strongest technical objections about gatekeeping and encryption freeze. CON's opening and rebuttals raised genuinely weighty concerns—bilateral consent, the Cambridge Analytica precedent, and E2EE degradation—but after PRO reframed portability as visible-connection pointers rather than full dossiers, CON largely reasserted "mathematical impossibility" and repeated prior framing rather than directly engaging the aggregation/scale problem that was its strongest unexploited angle. CON's "contradiction" charge in Rebuttal 2 (qualification vs. open standards) also read as a rhetorical stretch rather than a clean logical catch. PRO's closing crystallized the "meaningful exit" framing more sharply, while CON's closing mostly recapitulated earlier assertions. The debate was close and both sides argued the actual motion, but PRO did more work turning CON's own examples into support and answered pressure questions with the more concrete, mechanism-based responses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions (A4.1–A4.3), where "qualified" was redefined via objective security criteria and E2EE was preserved through public-key/encrypted-envelope exchange rather than boundary decryption, most directly defused CON's two sharpest objections (state-backed gatekeeping and crypto downgrade) and shifted the debate onto ground PRO controlled.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO built the clearer path by making meaningful exit the decisive metric, consistently showing scoped/revocable handles plus qualification/audits/emergency cutoffs neutralize CON's privacy and security freeze claims without freezing tech. CON's bilateral-consent and E2EE objections stayed live and forceful but became repetitive implementation fears that PRO answered more directly under pressure; CON never showed why lock-in is preferable once safeguards exist.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 collapsing state-actor and encryption fears into objective standards + compatible crypto without key sharing or dossiers
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by establishing a concrete, ongoing harm—platform lock-in—and showing that portability could be narrowly scoped to revocable handles without exposing friends' private data. A directly defused CON's strongest fears in Rebuttal 2, offering plausible mechanisms for secure interoperability (modern encryption standards, technical qualification criteria, and emergency abuse suspensions) that made CON's objections appear as manageable implementation problems rather than inherent impossibilities. CON's bilateral privacy and security freeze arguments remained rhetorically sharp but were successfully narrowed and never fully overcame PRO's core framing that meaningful exit requires real portability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4), which decisively answered CON's pressure questions by showing qualification could exclude malicious state actors through objective security criteria and that interoperability could use encrypted envelopes without decrypting at boundaries—collapsing CON's central encryption and gatekeeping fears into solvable design questions.
