### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): A built a tighter, more resilient case around the distinction between authorized synthetic performance and unauthorized/deceptive attribution, arguing that a disclosure mandate mislabels production method rather than the facts (payment, authorization, truthfulness) that actually matter to consumers. A's boundary-problem pressure (where does "AI-generated" begin/end) and its flip of B's own live-vs-prerecorded analogy ("that distinction ordinarily requires no legal warning") landed as clean, mostly unanswered damage. A also scored with the enforcement-mismatch point: if existing sponsorship rules are already flouted due to weak enforcement, stacking a new AI-disclosure duty on the same enforcement apparatus doesn't obviously fix anything. B's case—rooted in "parasocial trust" and the idea that the voice itself, not just the claims, is what consumers rely on—was emotionally compelling and had real intuitive pull, and B's materiality-based line-drawing attempt was a fair try at answering the boundary problem, but it didn't fully rebut A's sharper turns, especially the analogy reversal and the "label answers the wrong question" critique. Execution-wise, A's rebuttal turns were clipped twice for length, which is a real cost and slightly blunts full delivery, but the substance landed before the cuts and A's final closing was clean and crystallized the clash effectively, while B's closing was also clipped and ended mid-thought. On balance, A's tighter logical architecture and unanswered rebuttals give it a clear, if competitive, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's turn of B's own live-vs-prerecorded analogy against it ("listeners are not generally owed legal notice that a spot was prerecorded... because that production fact is immaterial") in Rebuttal 1, which B never squarely answered in Rebuttal 2, was the most decisive single exchange.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON demonstrated that the proposed mandate sorts advertisements by production method rather than by material deception, showing that an AI label would both over-warn authorized truthful endorsements and under-protect misleading human-read ads. PRO never adequately bridged the gap between the remedy (disclosing vocal synthesis) and the claimed harm (loss of trust in personal endorsement), because authorization and claim accuracy—rather than recording technique—determine whether a listener is deceived. CON’s sharper weighing, cleaner responses to pressure questions, and forceful crystallization in a complete closing gave it the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal and closing crystallized that an AI disclosure mandate answers the wrong question: it reveals production technique but not whether the host authorized the message, used the product, or was paid, meaning it would stigmatize honest synthetic ads while leaving misleading live-read ads unmarked.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON wins the materiality clash. PRO frames synthetic voice as deception of trust, but CON shows disclosure is mis-targeted: an authorized AI read can be truthful and an authentic live read can lie about use. That makes AI-label over-warn and under-protect compared to enforcing sponsorship disclosure, false-testimonial bans, and unauthorized-likeness rules. PRO's answers to the threshold pressure (entire performance vs editing) remain vague and collapse into CON's point that authorization, not production method, determines who endorses. Enforcement burden also favors CON: adding a second easy-to-evade label does not fix evasion of the first.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's R2 A1/A2 sequence that an AI label tells you nothing about payment, product use, or genuine authorization while a liar can still pass a live human read – exposing PRO's transparency rule as answering the wrong question and leaving the boundary arbitrary.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO built a tighter, more consistently defended case: the specific harm (audiences believing a trusted host personally delivered an endorsement when a clone did) is distinct from financial-connection disclosure and from fraud claims about product accuracy, and PRO kept returning to that precise gap. CON's strongest weapons—definitional line-drawing and small-creator burden—were partially blunted when PRO offered a workable mechanism (safe harbors for ordinary editing plus a default "just add a short label when in doubt" rule), which answers the chilling-effect worry more directly than CON's rebuttals acknowledged. In the pressure round, PRO's A1 cleanly showed that host consent to cloning doesn't resolve audience-facing deception, a point CON's closing never fully neutralized, instead repeating that fraud/contract/publicity law "already" covers the scenario without engaging the narrower authenticity-of-speaker point. CON did land a real hit by noting PRO's own example ("I use this every morning") is itself a false statement already actionable under fraud law, exposing a soft spot in PRO's illustration, but this only dented one example rather than the broader distinction PRO kept drawing between accurate-claim-but-false-attribution cases. Overall PRO answered pressure more squarely and sharpened the comparative stakes better in closing, giving it a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 ("Consent between the host and advertiser does not eliminate deception toward the audience... Authorization answers whether the advertiser may use the likeness, not whether consumers know whose judgment they are hearing") most damaged CON's claim that existing consent/fraud frameworks already close the gap, though CON's counter-exploitation of PRO's own "I use this every morning" example was a close second.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A wins because it identified and sustained a coherent regulatory gap: authorized synthetic endorsements can accurately describe products while deceiving audiences about whose personal judgment is being delivered. Side B never squarely answered this distinction, repeatedly reverting to product-fraud and contract-law remedies that govern advertiser-host relations rather than the audience's informational deficit. A's rebuttal 2 crystallized this by exposing B's own admission that fraud law struggles when the clone is authorized and claims are accurate. While B's line-drawing and small-creator burdens were live concerns, A offered workable safe harbors and showed the core deception remained scalable and invisible without the rule. Across the exchange, A progressed the clash more effectively and left its central comparative advantage standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was in Rebuttal 2 when Side A answered Side B's pressure question by showing that host authorization does not eliminate deception toward the audience, then turned Side B's own framework against it: B simultaneously claimed existing fraud law already covered the harm while asking what fraud could target when an authorized clone makes accurate claims, thereby admitting the very gap A had identified. A's framing that the harm is the concealed substitution of the persuasive act itself—rather than a false product claim—proved irrepressible through the remaining speeches.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO wins on the gap: authorized, fact-accurate clones mislead about who delivered the endorsement, which fraud/publicity law covering product falsity and host consent does not fix. CON's definitional and small-creator chill arguments are real but B answers with safe harbors for editing and the low-burden fix of labeling when in doubt, while CON never answers B's pressure about why a consumer is not deceived when host authorizes. Clearer weighing and direct A1/A2 answers give B the live route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 A1-A2 exposing that consent between host and advertiser does not cure audience deception, and that fraud requires false product fact not false attribution, which directly collapses CON's existing-law-duplication claim.
