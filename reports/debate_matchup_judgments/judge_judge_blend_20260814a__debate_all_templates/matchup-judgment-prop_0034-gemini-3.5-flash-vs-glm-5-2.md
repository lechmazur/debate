### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Its decisive structural critique — that the proposition contains no satire, commentary, or public-interest exemption, making it a blanket prior-restraint mechanism — was established in the opening and never adequately neutralized. PRO's answer in Rebuttal 2 (satirists can still use "traditional human impressions, text, animation") handed CON its sharpest line of the debate: restricting a specific expressive medium while permitting others is still a speech burden, no different in principle from saying "criticize the government, just not with modern tools." That reframe [A4.2] was memorable, grounded, and directly answered PRO's own words. CON's second durable argument — that affirmative consent changes the legal theory but not the enforcement infrastructure — also survived the exchange. PRO asserted that consent enables "swift, automated takedown systems and statutory remedies" but never demonstrated how that operates differently from the disclosure mandates and fraud prohibitions CON proposed as a targeted alternative; detection and platform compliance costs are the same under either regime. PRO had genuine wins: the proactive-versus-reactive framing on enforcement was real, and the biometric precision distinction from traditional human impersonation is a meaningful conceptual move. But neither of these was developed far enough to displace CON's core overbreadth problem, which persisted because the proposition as written has no contextual standard. CON's closing was clipped — a real execution cost — but the key arguments were already established and remained live. PRO's closing was adequate but repetitive, crystallizing the framing without advancing the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 [A4.2], responding to PRO's answer that satirists may still use "traditional human impressions, text, animation, or non-cloned synthesis": CON turned this answer directly against PRO by observing it is tantamount to saying critics may speak freely, just not with modern tools — a concrete, memorable formulation that recast PRO's apparent concession as a confirmation of the speech-burden charge CON had leveled from the opening.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B won by making the decisive clash about overbreadth and speech costs. Side A built a plausible dignity, labor, and consent baseline case, and answered that satire can use non-cloned tools, but never fully solved the fact that the proposition contains no exception for parody, commentary, education, or public-interest uses. Side B also pressed that consent does not by itself solve detection and enforcement burdens for ordinary people, weakening A's practical advantage. A's case remained morally forceful, but B gave the cleaner reason that a blanket permission gate is too broad relative to targeted remedies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was showing that A's distinction between human impressions and AI clones is not present in the motion, so the consent rule still captures AI-assisted satire and commentary; A's reply preserved other speech options but did not defeat the burden on that expressive medium.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): B won by proving the proposition is structurally overbroad and its central promise illusory. B showed that a blanket affirmative-consent rule with no contextual exemptions captures legitimate speech—satire, commentary, education—and that PRO's defense (satirists can simply use other tools) effectively concedes a medium-specific speech restriction. B also neutralized PRO's claim that consent empowers ordinary citizens by demonstrating that detection, enforcement, and platform compliance costs remain identical; consent changes the legal theory on paper without making remedies practically accessible. The result is prior restraint on protected expression without a corresponding real-world benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's dismantling of PRO's parody defense: by showing that telling satirists to use "traditional human impressions" instead of AI is like restricting modern expressive tools while keeping old ones, and that automated takedowns still require the same detection infrastructure PRO claims makes current law inadequate, B exposed that the proposition restricts speech without delivering its promised practical remedy.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won on three durable grounds that CON never adequately neutralized. First, the timing argument: fraud and defamation laws punish after damage is done, but a voice clone reaches millions before any takedown. CON's repeated answer—"existing laws already cover this"—was squarely rebutted by PRO's "you cannot un-ring a deepfake" logic, and CON never resolved this structural gap. Second, PRO maintained a clean and consistent distinction throughout: criticizing a public figure is categorically different from commercially distributing a precise AI replica of their voice. CON's censorship narrative depended on erasing that line, and PRO kept redrawing it effectively. Third, PRO's Q2 in the pressure round exposed a real contradiction: if commercial platforms are irrelevant to fraudsters (who use private channels), why are those same platforms so essential to creators that a consent rule would paralyze them? CON's answer—distinguishing legitimate creators from shadow fraudsters—acknowledged the problem but didn't resolve the logical asymmetry. CON's A1 in Rebuttal 2 was the clearest evasion: PRO's Q1 asked CON to name one criticism impossible without commercially selling a precise clone; CON shifted to talking about artistic force being "diminished," which never answered the actual question. CON's strongest points—automated over-blocking, chilling effects on independent creators, vagueness of "identifiable individual"—were real but were treated by PRO as implementation challenges rather than objections to the principle, and PRO's treatment was more persuasive. PRO's "ask first, sell second" framing was memorable and clean; CON's "public figures get a veto" framing was vivid but rested on the distinction it failed to reestablish after PRO separated commercial distribution from critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 in the pressure round was the most decisive moment: "if commercial platforms are irrelevant to fraud, why are those same platforms so essential to creators that a consent requirement would paralyze them?" CON's subsequent answer conceded the distinction but failed to resolve the logical tension, leaving PRO's framing of platform dependency intact while undermining CON's claim that the rule would only harm legitimate creators.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. A made a strong identity, consent, and timing case, especially that after-the-fact remedies cannot undo viral misuse. But B more effectively exploited the proposition’s breadth: an affirmative-consent rule gives identifiable people, especially public figures, a practical veto over monetized satire, reenactment, and critical media using voice imitation. A’s answer that criticism can still occur without a precise clone narrowed the harm but did not fully answer B’s point that the medium itself can be expressive. B also gave a plausible account of platform overblocking and why the rule may burden legitimate creators more than shadow fraudsters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive exchange was answering A’s “is it impossible?” question by reframing the issue as diminished expressive force, then tying that to public-figure veto power on monetized platforms.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): PRO defended a narrow, bright-line rule: requiring consent before commercially distributing identifiable voice clones prevents harm at scale without banning criticism. CON never substantiated that satire requires precise AI vocal replicas rather than approximation or quotation, and never resolved the tension that platforms are simultaneously essential to creators yet irrelevant to harm. PRO's framing of consent as a boundary on commercial impersonation rather than a veto on speech held through the exchange, while CON's broad censorship narrative relied on conflating criticism with the sale of cloned identity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's separation of "criticizing a public figure" from "commercially selling their cloned voice" in Rebuttals 1 and 2, which CON repeatedly asserted was impossible to distinguish but never demonstrated with a concrete example.
