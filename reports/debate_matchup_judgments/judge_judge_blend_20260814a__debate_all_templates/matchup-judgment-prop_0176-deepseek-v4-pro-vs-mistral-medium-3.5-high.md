### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by making the comparison easier to decide: SWFs convert volatile, finite windfalls into smoothed, intergenerational capital, while direct distributions risk boom-bust demand shocks and lack a credible preservation mechanism. Side B’s institutional-corruption case was strong and well targeted, but it never gave a convincing model or example of successful direct distribution of raw commodity revenues in a comparable developing context. Its Alaska example was effectively turned because the dividend depends on an SWF, and its smoothing proposal seemed to require a fund-like reserve. A had some execution costs from clipped turns, but still answered the central pressure more completely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s rebuttal that Alaska is not evidence against SWFs but evidence for them was the decisive exchange: it undercut B’s cleanest example and reframed stable direct payments as an output of sovereign wealth management rather than an alternative to it.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won by decisively winning the clash on macroeconomic stability and expertly turning Side A's main example against them. Side B successfully demonstrated that Alaska's dividend relies entirely on the existence of its Sovereign Wealth Fund, neutralizing Side A's core argument. Furthermore, Side B provided an excellent economic explanation for why SWFs invest abroad (to prevent Dutch disease), shutting down Side A's critique of capital flight.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, answering Q2, brilliantly flipped Side A's attack on SWFs exporting capital by explaining that offshore parking is exactly the mechanism used to sterilize inflationary pressure and prevent Dutch disease.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by exposing the mechanical incoherence of Side B's alternative. B advocated for smoothed direct cash distributions without a fund, but A correctly demonstrated that smoothing volatile commodity revenue inherently requires a vehicle to store boom-year surpluses, which is precisely what an SWF does. A successfully flipped B's best example (Alaska) by showing the dividend is the output of a fund, not a standalone mechanism. While B raised strong institutional concerns about SWF looting, A adequately mitigated this by noting weak governance equally threatens cash distribution through ghost beneficiaries and electoral capture. Crucially, A's Q2 pressed B to name a single developing country that successfully distributed raw revenue without a fund; B's failure to answer that burden in Rebuttal 2 left A's macroeconomic and intergenerational case standing as the only workable model. A's clipped turns incur a minor execution cost, but the argumentative path to victory was cleaner and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttals successfully dismantled Side B's reliance on Alaska by clarifying that the Permanent Fund Dividend is mathematically impossible without the underlying sovereign wealth fund to sterilize and smooth the raw revenue. This turned B's flagship example into proof of the proposition and exposed the mechanical gap in B's smoothing-without-a-fund proposal.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B won by making institutional feasibility the decisive clash. Side A had a coherent macroeconomic and intergenerational case for SWFs, but relied heavily on examples that were either developed-country outliers or, in rebuttal, fragile-state funds that Side B effectively turned as evidence of raiding and misuse. Side B’s “single temptable pot” framing directly answered the preservation claim: an SWF only preserves wealth if the institution can actually resist capture. B also gave a cleaner comparative answer to Dutch disease by arguing phased payouts and basic payment infrastructure are lighter lifts than independent boards, withdrawal rules, and global portfolio governance. A’s closing was clear but mostly repeated the preservation theory without overcoming B’s governance attack.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s rebuttal to A’s institutional-design claim was decisive: it used A’s own Timor-Leste, Nigeria, and Azerbaijan examples to argue that paper safeguards fail in fragile states, undermining the central premise that SWFs reliably preserve wealth.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully turning Side B's own examples (Nigeria, Timor-Leste, Azerbaijan) to prove that SWFs in developing countries inevitably suffer from elite capture. Side A also provided a stronger comparative weighing mechanism: a mismanaged SWF leaves the public with nothing, while an imperfect cash transfer system still delivers present welfare and builds political accountability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 effectively turned Side B's SWF examples, and A's Rebuttal 2 provided the decisive comparative weighing: phasing cash payouts is a far lighter administrative lift than preventing a global portfolio from being looted by elites.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B wins by successfully turning Side A’s own examples (Nigeria, Timor-Leste, Azerbaijan) against them, demonstrating that paper safeguards routinely fail in fragile states where the political class controls the overseers. B establishes a cleaner comparative metric: institutional burden. Running a globally diversified SWF with independent boards is a far heavier governance lift than phased cash payouts via existing mobile infrastructure. B’s “citizen-as-auditor” framing effectively answers the transparency clash, showing that decentralized distribution makes systemic theft politically explosive, whereas centralized funds invite quiet looting. A’s defense that raids were merely “noticed and debated” concedes the core failure of the mechanism. B controlled the decisive clash on political economy and accountability, making the path to victory clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 1 and 2 dismantling A’s institutional design argument by showing A’s cited SWFs were raided despite safeguards, coupled with the comparative claim that cash distribution is a lighter administrative lift than complex SWF governance. This forced A into a defensive posture that never fully recovered.
