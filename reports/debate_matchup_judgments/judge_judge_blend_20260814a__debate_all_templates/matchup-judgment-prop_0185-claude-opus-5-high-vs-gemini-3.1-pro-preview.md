### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A compellingly established structural deficit bias and the value of visible enforcement, but repeatedly relied on emergency and capital-investment carve-outs beyond the motion’s specified automatic-stabilizer exception. Side B showed that strict rules either obstruct targeted crisis response and long-term investment or become gameable through broad exceptions. Its distinction between automatic income support and active responses to banking, infrastructure, or regional shocks remained insufficiently answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strictness dilemma was decisive: permit broad investment and emergency exceptions and politicians can relabel spending; deny them and the constitution can block valuable investment or responses to contested, slow-burning crises. Side A’s appeal to drafting flexibility did not resolve that tradeoff.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO built the clearer path by anchoring on the conceded debt ratchet and structural bias, then forcing CON to defend pure discretion as the cure; PRO turned Germany into evidence of workable escape clauses rather than paralysis and dismantled the 1990s surplus as temporary rule-assisted luck. CON raised real costs around asymmetry, courts, and gimmicks but never neutralized the ratchet or supplied a reliable boom-repair mechanism, leaving PRO's visible-vs-invisible failure comparison decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal on Germany (B2.1): the "straitjacket" visibly flexed for crises, collapsing CON's paralysis story while preserving the ordinary-year bind.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won because CON conceded the debt ratchet is a real structural problem under legislative discretion, then failed to identify any reliable discretionary mechanism that reverses it in booms. CON’s best example—U.S. 1990s surpluses—was effectively turned by PRO as a case where statutory rules, not pure discretion, produced consolidation, and the surplus vanished once those rules expired. PRO also successfully showed that constitutional rules with escape clauses can flex during genuine emergencies (Germany 2020/2022 suspensions), while binding in ordinary years. CON’s warnings about judicial overreach and shadow accounting were answered by PRO with the visible-failure versus invisible-failure frame: under rules, evasion requires public constitutional fights and accounting fictions that courts and press can expose, whereas discretion produces gimmicks plus overt deficits with no structural brake. CON never recovered from the concession that the disease PRO identified is real and that discretion is its cause.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s turning of CON’s U.S. 1990s surplus example in the closing: PRO showed the surplus arose during a period of statutory spending caps and divided government, and disappeared once the caps lapsed, proving that rules—not pure discretion—produced the consolidation, and that without them discretion reverted to deficits.

These rebuttals created a live, unanswered route to victory for CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's demonstration that PRO's rule constitutionally prohibits the discretionary rescue tools (furloughs, bank recapitalizations) that halted recent depressions, while PRO's alternative of pre-funded resolution pools requires exactly the political foresight its opening claimed lawmakers lack.
