### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's assumptions about pricing. B's argument that bundles are anchored by the elasticity of casual fans—and that severing the bundle would leave devoted fans exposed to maximum price extraction—provided a decisive, unrefuted economic mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal brilliantly explained that casual fans anchor the bundle price, and dismantled Side A's market forces by noting upstream competition raises costs rather than disciplining retail prices.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): CON successfully showed that PRO's cheap granular pass rested on an unpriced promise rather than a tested mechanism. By turning PRO's own price-discrimination analogy against them and pointing out that piracy, rights competition, and regulation already exist yet have not produced cheap team passes, CON established that bundles are what anchor prices through casual-fan elasticity. CON also defended the structural necessity of bundle revenue for league balance and niche sport with the observable status quo, while PRO conceded cross-subsidy needs without substantiating that alternatives could fill the gap. PRO's volume-expansion counter remained plausible but speculative against CON's standing mechanisms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's reversal of PRO's airline analogy in Rebuttal 1, showing devoted fans are the inelastic captive segment and bundles actually discipline prices via casual-fan elasticity; this mechanism challenge survived PRO's market-forces answer and became the decisive frame.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won decisively by dismantling Side B's core economic premise. Side A brilliantly flipped Side B's price discrimination and airline analogies to show that inelastic, captive fans would actually be charged more in an unbundled model, while the bundle currently restrains prices by anchoring them to elastic casual fans. Side B failed to provide a convincing mechanism for why a retail monopolist would voluntarily price team-specific passes cheaply, relying on weak assertions about piracy and upstream competition that Side A easily neutralized. Side A's structural arguments about the shrinking revenue pot and its impact on competitive balance remained largely unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1, where it weaponized Side B's own airline price discrimination analogy to prove that captive, devoted fans would face higher prices under granular pricing, completely collapsing Side B's "cheaper pass" premise.
