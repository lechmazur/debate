### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): CON (Side B) won a clear but competitive debate on the strength of three decisive moves that PRO never fully neutralized. First, CON's circular logic catch in Rebuttal 2 was the sharpest analytical moment in the debate: PRO's own Rebuttal 2 admission that "perfect management cannot cure a demand for the pre-pandemic aesthetic" collapsed the management-can-fix-it defense that had underpinned PRO's entire rebuttal strategy. Second, CON identified the false binary in B4.1—the choice is not between parking and dining but includes bike lanes, public benches, and genuinely open parklets—and PRO's closing never touched this alternative. Third, CON's reading of the motion's presupposition (that it bakes in the illegitimacy of resident objections by default) was a genuine logical observation that PRO's "vocal minority" and "nostalgia" framing actually reinforced rather than answered. PRO did well on the economic anchor and "eyes on the street" arguments, and the ADA-compliance response to the accessibility charge was adequate. The parking-vs.-parklet reframe was rhetorically clever. But PRO was damaged by the sidewalk-not-curb distinction it never fully resolved (raised sharply in CON's Q1), and its repeated "argument by insult" move of dismissing residents as nostalgic or individually inconvenienced was called out early and never corrected. CON's closing—"a land grant with better branding"—crystallized the clash memorably without adding new material. PRO's closing mostly recycled existing points without sharpening the key weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 circular logic catch (B4.3–B4.4): PRO had argued that management tools resolve noise and trash complaints, but then admitted in A4.2 that management cannot cure all dissatisfaction and relabeled the remainder as "aesthetic nostalgia." CON exposed that this makes every resident concern unfalsifiable—if it persists, it becomes nostalgia; if it resolves, it was always manageable. This dismantled PRO's central rebuttal mechanism and was never answered.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B wins a clear but competitive debate. Side A landed strong attacks on privatization, accessibility, noise, and the false binary between parking and restaurant patios, but it often overread the motion as requiring cities to silence residents or allow unmanaged annexation of sidewalks. Side B better matched the proposition by arguing that flexible use can coexist with ADA lanes, hours limits, permits, and enforcement, while the broader benefits to restaurant survival, street activity, jobs, tax revenue, and neighborhood vitality remained live. Side A showed reasons for regulation and caution, but did not prove cities should return to pre-pandemic rules rather than manage flexible dining.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s most decisive exchange was answering accessibility and nuisance concerns as regulatory problems rather than reasons to reject flexible dining altogether, then weighing residual resident preference against broader public economic and street-life benefits.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won by exposing that PRO's case rested on a false binary (cars versus dining) and a circular framework that sorted every resident objection into either a manageable operational problem or dismissible nostalgic aesthetic. CON broke that binary by showing cities could reclaim space for genuinely public uses like bike lanes, benches, and open parklets. CON also landed a devastating methodological critique: PRO admitted management could not cure all dissatisfaction, then relabeled the remainder as mere preference for the pre-pandemic aesthetic, leaving no legitimate category for competing commons claims such as accessibility, sleep, and unobstructed pedestrian passage. PRO never recovered from this framework damage; their closing simply restated economic spillover and vibrancy claims without answering why resident input should be structurally overridden by default.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Its most decisive move came early: the parking-lane-as-already-exclusive reframe [A2.1] neutralized CON's "privatization" framing without being answered squarely. CON tried to limit this to sidewalk space specifically [B2.1], but never fully escaped the comparative logic—a parked car is also an exclusive, private use of the public right-of-way. On enforcement and accessibility, PRO's comparison to existing sidewalk café regimes was well-grounded and CON's "fantasy enforcement" claim was overstated given that regulated sidewalk cafés predate the pandemic. CON's strongest pressure came from the noise-timing observation [B3.1, B4.4]—10pm quiet hours don't protect families at 7–8pm—and PRO's A1 response didn't fully address this specific window, leaving a partial wound. The "flexible vs. rigid" distinction [B4.2] was CON's best structural argument, but PRO answered it adequately by noting that flexibility in layout design doesn't preclude fixed standards like minimum clear widths [A5.3]. CON's accessibility-as-civil-rights reframe was rhetorically strong [B4.5, B5.2] but depended on enforcement being uniquely impossible, a claim PRO consistently and plausibly contested. PRO's "minority veto" framing [A2.5, A5.4] placed CON in an uncomfortable position—CON never adequately explained why "some residents prefer the old rules" should be treated as a sufficient counter-weight rather than as an ordinary policy-change dissent. CON's closing essentially repeated its earlier lines without sharpening the comparative choice, while PRO's closing crystallized the live clash cleanly. On balance, PRO's argumentative burden was lower and better met; CON's strongest arguments were real but did not neutralize PRO's core comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal [A2.1–A2.2] exposing the parking lane as already an exclusive, private use of public space was the most decisive single exchange. It turned CON's "privatization" label against itself and established a comparative frame—which use of the curb better serves the public—that CON never cleanly answered throughout the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A made the strongest CON case around accessibility, dynamic sidewalk obstruction, and residential livability, but Side B better fit the motion’s moderate wording: allowing more flexible outdoor dining need not mean lawless or unregulated use. B consistently weighed manageable harms against tangible public benefits, answered the privatization point with the comparison to parking lanes and other regulated private uses, and framed A’s standard as too close to a veto by objecting residents. A’s “flexible inherently means ad-hoc sprawl” claim remained plausible but overdrawn, while B’s regulatory answer was sufficient to preserve the PRO route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response that privatization objections also apply to parking lanes, sidewalk cafes, vending, and other regulated uses was the decisive exchange, because it shifted the debate from “private use bad” to whether this use can be regulated and produces greater public value.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO won by leveraging the motion's built-in tolerance for resident dissent to frame CON as advocating minority veto, while successfully rebutting the privatization charge and showing that noise, trash, and accessibility concerns are manageable regulatory problems rather than unique fatal flaws. CON's rigid-versus-flexible accessibility distinction was analytically sharp but could not fully outweigh PRO's case for aggregate economic and civic vitality.
