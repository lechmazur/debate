- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built a constructive counter-mechanism (tax large secured borrowing as income plus death-as-realization) that directly neutralized PRO's own central example (buy-borrow-die), turning PRO's strongest evidence into support for CON's alternative rather than the wealth tax. PRO's pressure questions were sharp, but PRO's answers (A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2) retreated into a narrower $50M/1-3% proposal without resolving the annual-appraisal-of-illiquid-assets problem, and PRO's closing did not re-engage the collapsed-valuation concession or the yield/enforcement critique. CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly around "ability to pay = realized cash," while PRO largely restated its opening framing without repairing the exposed structural weakness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4) turning PRO's own admission in B4.1 (no refund if valuation collapses) into proof that a wealth tax taxes "fantasy" value and punishes the builder who kept the factory — this exchange most directly damaged PRO's capacity-to-pay justification and went unanswered in PRO's closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): A won a clear but competitive debate by pairing a targeted alternative with stronger implementation analysis. B established the fairness problem of indefinitely compounding wealth, but its valuation formulas, deferral, fractional equity, and exit penalties were largely asserted rather than shown to overcome annual appraisal, illiquidity, and flight. B also failed to answer A's request for a credible net yield and used nonexistent C reference IDs. A sometimes overstated the European record and used examples below B's eventual threshold, but its frequency-and-liquidity distinction preserved the central objection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.2 was decisive: A answered B's alleged valuation contradiction by distinguishing occasional valuations tied to death or collateralized transactions from compulsory annual valuations without a transaction, market evidence, or cash. B never fully neutralized that distinction.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the decisive comparison on ability-to-pay and administrability: the buy-borrow-die problem PRO named is closable by taxing large secured borrowing and death realization without annual universal appraisals of private firms. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but B (PRO) built the more resilient case. B narrowed the scope early (high thresholds, deferred liens, look-through rules) to defuse CON's administrative/France-style-failure argument, and directly exploited a contradiction in CON's carve-out complaints. Crucially, B's pressure question about the billionaire who holds $10B for 20 years without selling or borrowing exposed the central weakness in CON's transaction-based alternative: CON's answer ("he consumes zero economic resources... we'll capture it eventually") effectively conceded that under a pure transaction tax, persistent economic power and ability-to-pay go untaxed indefinitely, which B had framed as the core normative stake from the opening. CON's rebuttal that B's lien "concession" (crediting/forgiving on collapsed valuations) destroys the annual-tax premise is a clever rhetorical move but overstates the case — reassessing wealth to current value each year is coherent with an annual wealth tax, not a fatal contradiction. CON's administrative and valuation concerns remained real and were never fully neutralized, but B's scoped design plus its unanswered power/ability-to-pay argument gave it the cleaner path to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure question (B3.1) asking how a transaction-based tax captures the ability-to-pay and power of a billionaire who holds $10B for 20 years without selling or borrowing, and CON's weak answer (A4.1) that he "consumes zero economic resources" — which B's closing (B5.1-B5.2) exploited as CON effectively conceding the core normative gap in its case.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B won a competitive debate by preserving the central distinction between taxable transactions and wealth’s present economic and political power. A exposed real valuation and deferral problems, especially B’s awkward proposal to credit liens after value declines, but never answered why a never-selling fortune should remain untaxed for decades. B also offered plausible, if imperfect, responses to forced-sale and shell-entity concerns. A’s unsupported claims about foreign failures and its mistaken reference IDs slightly weakened an otherwise strong case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was forcing A to defend the untaxed $10 billion holder. A answered that no resources were consumed until a transaction occurred, but B persuasively replied that ownership itself already enables acquisitions, market control, political influence, and compounding advantage.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO kept the live clash on ability-to-pay and political power from held stock, not only realized flows. CON's practical critique (valuation, forced sales, Europe) was real and forced design concessions, but once PRO offered high thresholds plus interest-bearing liens with look-through and value adjustment, CON's best reply—that non-transacting holders "consume zero" and can wait for later transaction fixes—left the pure-holder opt-out standing. That failure undercut CON's claim that income-base repairs alone suffice, so PRO's narrower annual-stock rule won the comparative choice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO pressure Q1 + A4.5/A5 closing: CON never shows how transaction reforms tax a multi-year non-borrower non-seller, while the accruing lien still does.
