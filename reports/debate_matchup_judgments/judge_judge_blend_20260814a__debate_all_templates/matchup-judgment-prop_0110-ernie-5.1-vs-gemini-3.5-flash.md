### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by maintaining a cleaner path to the proposition throughout. A's core comparison—US at 18% GDP yielding worse life expectancy and infant mortality than the UK at ~10%—was never directly dismantled. The administrative overhead argument (15–20% vs 2–3%) was introduced early and never answered by B. A's Switzerland pressure question (Q1) was the sharpest exchange in the debate: it exposed that B's showcase system spends roughly twice what the UK does and mandates universal coverage, meaning it cannot serve as proof that private insurance delivers superior dollar efficiency. B's Rebuttal 2 answer to that question evaded the efficiency paradox entirely, asserting that Switzerland "buys" better access without addressing why higher spending is more efficient. B's strongest live arguments—wait times as a hidden cost and disease-specific survival rates—were real challenges, but A adequately countered them: wait times affect elective patients while prevention protects the majority, and clinical survival metrics represent a narrow slice of population health burden that elides the preventive gains public systems generate. B's social-determinants argument also partially backfired, since it would equally undercut the US private system's apparent underperformance. A consistently held the frame the proposition demands—outcomes per dollar across the whole population—while B kept substituting narrower questions about acute clinical care. B's closing added nothing new and remained largely rhetorical.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure question Q1 on Switzerland (Rebuttal 2 A1): by noting that Switzerland spends ~12% of GDP (double the UK) and mandates universal coverage, A exposed that B's flagship example proves the opposite of its intended point. B's response asserted that Switzerland "delivers better outcomes" without addressing the efficiency paradox, leaving the strongest counter-example in B's case effectively neutralized.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): B won because it stayed closer to the motion’s population-health-per-dollar metric, offering a clearer comparative story around lower spending, universal access, prevention, administrative overhead, and outcomes. A’s hidden wait-time cost and social-determinants critique were real pressures, but A leaned heavily on asserted clinical superiority and Switzerland without proving better population outcomes per dollar, and often shifted from efficiency to access quality. B’s evidence was sometimes overconfident, but its central comparison survived better than A’s.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal that Switzerland is high-spending, heavily mandated, and regulated undercut A’s main private-system example, while B’s broader response that selective wait-time and disease-survival claims do not outweigh population outcomes per dollar was the decisive exchange.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A established a clear cross-national pattern showing public systems achieve better population health outcomes at substantially lower cost, and successfully rebutted B's wait-time and innovation arguments by contextualizing them against the uninsured population and preventive advantages. Side B's reliance on Switzerland backfired when A exposed its higher spending and universal mandate, and B never adequately reconciled its efficiency claims with the US's poor population outcomes despite massive expenditure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure question and subsequent rebuttal dismantling Switzerland as a model of per-dollar private efficiency by highlighting its high spending and universal coverage mandate, which undermined B's central comparative example.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A built a coherent and structurally consistent case across all rounds: monopsony purchasing power, administrative overhead reduction, and prevention incentive alignment all pointed in the same direction and were maintained under pressure. Side B suffered a catastrophic execution failure in Rebuttal 1 (Turn 4), where the entire speech argued from the PRO position—explicitly agreeing that private systems have heavier administrative burdens, that prevention investment is structurally misaligned due to churn, and concluding "Public systems win it decisively." This is not a close call on the round; it constitutes self-inflicted elimination of Side B's rebuttal function in that speech. Beyond this structural error, Side B's own opening introduced the US example to support private-system superiority (superior cancer survival, cardiac outcomes), yet simultaneously acknowledged US spending is "roughly twice" peer nations per capita—an admission PRO correctly weaponized in A2.1 to show the US private-dominant system delivers poor population health per dollar by any reasonable metric. Side B never cleanly escaped this contradiction. Their Rebuttal 2 attempted recovery by leaning into price signals directing spending to "highest health return," but this argument was thin given PRO's repeated point that price barriers exclude the poorest patients from primary care, driving expensive emergency escalations—a structural efficiency loss B never squarely answered. PRO's pressure questions were better targeted, and PRO's A1/A2 answers were direct and on-format. Side B's questions were reasonable but their own answers in Rebuttal 2 relied on reasserting the same US cancer/cardiac cherry-pick that had already been damaged. The motion specifically demands "population health outcomes per dollar," and PRO consistently held the more defensible ground on that metric throughout the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's exploitation of CON's US example in A2.1 was the decisive rebuttal. CON introduced the US in B1.2 as evidence of private-system superiority via cancer survival and cardiac outcomes. PRO correctly turned this against CON: the US spends roughly double peer nations per capita yet lags on life expectancy, infant mortality, and chronic disease—exactly the population-health-per-dollar metrics the motion demands. CON's Rebuttal 1 then compounded the damage by essentially agreeing with PRO's framing, never recovering a coherent position on why the US example supports the CON case.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): B won decisively because it kept the debate focused on population health per dollar: administrative friction, bargaining power, universal access, and long-term prevention incentives. B's case was more coherent, better weighed, and easier to apply to the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal was decisive: it turned A's United States example by distinguishing selective high-end outcomes from population health per dollar, then tied private fragmentation to higher admin costs, delayed care, and weak prevention incentives.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won decisively because Side B's first rebuttal catastrophically conceded the proposition, agreeing that the U.S. example proves private systems fail on population outcomes per dollar, that private insurance imposes heavy hidden administrative burdens on providers, that churn creates a structural market failure in prevention, and that price rationing drives cost-inefficient emergency care, before explicitly concluding "Public systems win it decisively." This self-contradiction destroyed CON's credibility and made the remaining rounds non-competitive. Even setting that aside, Side A built a clearer, more coherent case around monopsony purchasing power, streamlined administration, and lifetime preventative incentives that Side B never squarely rebutted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal (Turn 3) was decisive: it turned Side B's own U.S. example against private insurance by showing that higher per-capita spending alongside worse life expectancy, infant mortality, and chronic disease management proves inferior population outcomes per dollar, and it exposed the massive provider-side billing friction that private fragmentation imposes.
