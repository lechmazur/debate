### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner path to victory across the full exchange. The decisive move came in PRO's A1 (Rebuttal 2): maximum requirements are calibrated to observed local demand, not arbitrary low caps. If two-car households reliably exist, the maximum reflects that—it only eliminates the forced overbuild above real usage. This directly deflated CON's best recurring example (the 1.5 spots/unit exurb scenario), which CON continued using as if it had never been answered in both the second rebuttal and the closing. CON's core equity argument—parking scarcity harms car-dependent low-wage workers—had real force, but PRO's response (harm is monthly guaranteed cost vs. occasional rare circling; caps track real demand) was sufficient to neutralize it without PRO needing perfect precision. CON never squarely disputed that minimum rules currently mandate overbuild beyond even peak demand; it only defended the value of buffers in the abstract. PRO's framing was also cleaner: government forcing universal monthly payment for parking that demonstrably sits idle is easier to oppose than the speculative circling harms CON emphasized. CON's A2 answer (developers have no incentive to build community-stabilizing parking) opened a new market-failure line without adequately engaging PRO's competition response, and the closing mostly recycled arguments PRO had already answered. PRO won the most important clash—what maximum requirements actually require—and held the comparative advantage in equity framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange: by clarifying that maximum caps are set at observed local demand rather than arbitrary low numbers, PRO directly disarmed CON's repeated 1.5 spots/unit example, the linchpin of CON's equity and access case. CON never recovered from this, repeating the same exurb/two-car-household argument in the closing as though the calibration response had not been made.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins because it successfully framed minimum parking as a guaranteed, universal hidden tax on all residents for mostly unused asphalt, while neutralizing CON’s core equity attack by showing maximums would be calibrated to actual observed demand rather than the arbitrarily low caps CON relied upon. PRO’s answers to pressure questions were direct and sharpened the economic mechanism, whereas CON never fully recovered from the “strawman cap” rebuttal or justified why developers must be forced to build parking that customers supposedly value enough to pay for.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1’s response that “That cap number was never the proposal” and that maximums are set at actual observed local demand rather than arbitrary 1.5-spot limits, which dismantled CON’s central concrete example and forced CON to rely on vaguer assertions about market failure for the rest of the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO opened with a well-structured, consistent case: minimum mandates are a regressive universal tax on the 1-in-5 households without cars, and maximum requirements don't ban parking, only stop forcing costly surplus. That framing held throughout. However, CON won the later rounds on the exchanges that actually matter most for the motion's qualifier. First, the "choice vs. imposition" distinction in A4.1 was sharp and went unanswered: bundled parking costs fall only on people who chose to enter new construction; spillover costs fall on every existing resident who made no such choice. PRO's closing falsely claimed CON never rebutted the regressive cost argument—a credibility hit. Second, CON's car-dependent-regions-specific rebuttal ("Nobody drives two minutes to the grocery store because parking is free. They drive because it is 100 degrees outside, there is no sidewalk, and the bus comes once an hour.") directly targeted the motion's hardest qualifier, and PRO never adequately responded. The hidden-subsidy mode-shift argument, PRO's strongest lever against car dependence, simply doesn't hold in regions where no alternatives exist. Third, CON's developer-accountability framing—that minimum mandates make the party creating demand internalize the cost, while caps let externalities spill onto the public—was structurally sound and PRO's only counter (calibrated caps and permits) never resolved the net parking shortage those cars still create. PRO's permit answer narrowly manages commuter spillover but doesn't address the scarcity problem from underbuilt resident parking. PRO was more consistent in messaging, but CON's rebuttal 2 and closing were more vivid, more specific to the car-dependent context, and left the cleaner decision rule standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.3): the "choice vs. imposition" distinction—bundled parking falls on people who voluntarily chose new construction, while spillover is imposed on everyone already living nearby with no say—combined with the pointed "no alternative modes" response ("nobody drives because parking is free; they drive because it's 100 degrees and the bus comes once an hour") together constituted the most decisive exchange of the debate, directly targeting the motion's car-dependent qualifier where PRO's hidden-subsidy argument was weakest.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side B won by demonstrating that in car-dependent regions, maximum parking requirements do not generate the benefits Side A claimed. Side A argued that eliminating mandatory parking would reduce driving by removing a hidden subsidy, but Side B successfully rebutted that in car-dependent regions people drive because transit, sidewalks, and walkability are absent—not because bundled parking is cheap—so caps merely externalize costs without cutting traffic. Side B also established a clear distributional unfairness: the policy gives a small rent discount to future tenants by imposing parking scarcity on existing residents who had no say. Side A never squarely answered why it is fair to burden current low-income drivers for the benefit of future movers, and its reliance on residential parking permits failed to neutralize Side B's point that reallocating existing street spaces does not solve scarcity. The decisive clash resolved against Side A because its causal mechanism collapsed under the "car-dependent regions" framework, while Side B's cost-shifting and spillover analysis remained live and unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal (B4.3-B4.4) dismantling Side A's claim that removing bundled parking would reduce unnecessary car trips by pointing out that in car-dependent regions mode choice is dictated by missing transit, absent sidewalks, and climate—not by artificially cheap parking—thereby collapsing PRO's congestion benefit and sharpening CON's frame that costs are moved, not eliminated.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully isolating the core trade-off: maximum caps shift parking costs from future tenants to existing residents via street spillover, without actually reducing driving in car-dependent regions that lack transit alternatives. CON's decisive rebuttal that permits do not create parking and that unbundling costs will not change behavior where no alternatives exist directly neutralized PRO's main mechanisms. PRO relied heavily on repeating the regressive tax point, dismissed spillover as hypothetical despite clear developer incentives, and offered a weak pivot that lower rents would let vulnerable workers afford private parking. CON's framing of uncompensated externalities dumped on current neighbors, combined with sharper answers to pressure questions and a more crystallized closing, provided the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 directly dismantled PRO's permit solution and congestion claim. By noting that permits merely ration existing spaces rather than create them, and by grounding the car-dependent reality (nobody drives short trips because parking is free; they drive because of heat, missing sidewalks, and hourly buses), CON severed PRO's causal link between unbundling parking and reduced driving while exposing the equity flaw in shifting costs to existing residents.
