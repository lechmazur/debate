### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides argued with real sophistication, but A better exploited the resolution's comparative structure. A's central move—that B's "bespoke" framework (mandatory reserves, segregation, redemption rules, insolvency priority) is functionally the banking/MMF playbook by another name—was pressed effectively in Rebuttal 1 and the pressure questions, and B never fully escaped it: B's answer that "labels aren't the point" is a real distinction (capital rules tied to credit risk vs. liquidity rules) but B's own proposed toolkit still sits much closer to the financial-institution paradigm than to "ordinary software" regulation, which the resolution's binary framing rewards A for. B's sharpest counter—that MMF-style redemption gating conflicts with a payment token's core function and can accelerate runs—was a genuinely damaging, underexplored point that A's closing answered only by asserting policy justification rather than engaging the run-acceleration mechanism directly, leaving a live but not fully decisive gap for B. However, B's second rebuttal was clipped by the word-limit format note, costing B a fully realized closing synthesis of its strongest framing, and A's closing more directly crystallized the stakes (insolvency vs. bounded software bugs) in a way that was easier for a reader to weigh. On balance, A's clearer, more consistently pressed comparative argument and better execution give it a modest edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 turning B's own "bespoke" checklist (reserves, segregation, redemption, insolvency priority) into an implicit concession of the resolution, versus B's counter in Rebuttal 2/Closing that redemption gating undermines the payment function and can accelerate runs—the latter point was never squarely answered by A.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won by consistently winning the comparative: financial tools like segregation and asset limits are necessary but do not equal bank/MMF institutional models built for credit creation, leverage, or fluctuating shares. PRO repeatedly treated any financial rule as a concession to its side and failed to justify mismatched burdens such as gating or capital buffers for fully reserved payment tokens; CON's answers to pressure questions exposed the fit problem more cleanly than PRO's repetitive "software is insufficient" framing. B's R2 clip was a minor cost but did not erase the sharper distinction work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1/B2.3 + B4.2: financial safeguards do not prove bank/MMF status; direct liquid-reserve rules solve run risk better than imported MMF gates that break the payment function.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON wins by exposing that bank and MMF institutional templates are mismatched for stablecoins: MMF-style redemption gating undermines the payment function, bank capital rules target credit risks that fully reserved issuers do not take, and algorithmic or decentralized structures lack an issuer that bank rules could even attach to. While PRO successfully shows stablecoins need financial safeguards, CON persuasively distinguishes payment-firm and custodian tools from bank or MMF institutional logic, showing that a bespoke framework is more precise than inherited labels. PRO’s attempt to treat any balance-sheet rule as a concession of bank-like status overreaches, and its reliance on Terra as proof actually illustrates why bank-model capital cannot fix a design with no reserves.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s counter that MMF redemption gating is the opposite of a stablecoin’s payment function and can accelerate runs, directly turning PRO’s clearest example of a distinctly MMF-specific requirement against the bank/MMF analogy.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO built a coherent function-over-form case (redemption promise, run dynamics, custody/liquidity risk) and successfully absorbed CON's strongest weapons. When CON tried to turn the "definitional retreat" objection into a resolution-mismatch argument, PRO's "more like, not identical" framing plus the MMF analogy (redeemable claims against pooled assets without loan-book capital) blunted it. Most decisively, PRO flipped CON's 2023 depeg example: instead of proving bank regulation imports contagion, it showed that honest backing plus code transparency still failed to prevent a run because custody concentration wasn't managed — exactly the gap PRO's framework targets. CON's pressure questions were sharp but PRO's answers (A1/A2) directly engaged them, while CON's own answer to PRO's Q1 ("standard commercial and contract law") never explained who sets a mandatory floor on reserve eligibility, liability disclosure, or redemption priority, leaving PRO's core objection unresolved. CON's case (proof-of-reserves, software/commercial law, systemic-risk-import) was original and well argued but rested on proving too much: the argument that regulated custodians can fail undercuts trust in any oversight, including CON's own reliance on audits and trusts. PRO's clipped final rebuttal is a real execution cost, but the substance had already landed before the cut.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's reuse of the 2023 depeg example (A2.6/A4.2) — turning CON's signature anecdote about custodian failure into proof that concentration/liquidity supervision, not mere backing or transparency, is what prevents runs — did the most damage to CON's central "software is enough" claim.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO built the cleaner path by treating the proposition as comparative function-based regulation of redeemable claims, neutralizing maturity-transformation attacks with proportionality and MMF analogies, and showing that CON’s own 1:1 trusts/audits still require the ex-ante financial safeguards ordinary software rules do not supply. CON’s best weapon (definitional “retreat” plus 2023 contagion) landed but never overcame PRO’s weighing that market runs and post-hoc fraud leave users unprotected. Clipping on A’s second rebuttal was a minor execution cost but did not erase the sharper live clash resolution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.2 (CON’s “true stablecoin” already requires legal/prudential safeguards, conceding software regulation is inadequate)
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by showing that stablecoins are financial redemption promises whose risks sit in reserves, custody, and liquidity, not merely in code. A decisively turned B’s 2023 depeg example to prove that transparency and market discipline are synonyms for bank runs rather than protections against them, and that liquidity and diversification rules are needed precisely because honest reserves can become trapped. B’s "ordinary software" model never identified an enforceable non-financial regime that could set reserve standards or guarantee holder priority in insolvency, and A’s money-market fund analogy provided a coherent proportional framework for "more like" regulation without imposing loan-book rules.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal, which exposed that B’s reliance on market discipline and real-time transparency during the 2023 depeg described a bank run, not a safeguard, and clarified that prudential regulation manages custodial concentration rather than guaranteeing no bank ever fails.
