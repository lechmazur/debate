### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won on three converging advantages. First, the "favor, not ban" framing held throughout: PRO correctly noted that an integrated suite offering documented APIs and clean export could still win, which defused CON's "chaotic patchwork" scenario. CON never found a clean counter to this—by the closing they had essentially conceded that abusive lock-in is bad and portability matters when offers are comparable, which PRO correctly identified as leaving only the default rule in dispute, and PRO's default is the stronger public governance answer. Second, PRO's "seam risk is a management problem; captivity is a power problem" framing survived the exchange. CON's response—that seam failures are live, not abstract—is true but does not defeat the structural point: service levels and prime contracts can assign responsibility for seam failures; no contract recreates bargaining power once data and workflows are captured. Third, PRO's Rebuttal 2 landed the sharpest single blow of the debate: CON's "reasonable export" standard is vacuous unless procurement structurally prioritizes portability, because incumbents define what is reasonable when exit is not a live threat. CON never filled that hole. On the other side, CON's strongest live argument—that small institutions cannot realistically supervise a modular software supply chain—was introduced in Rebuttal 1 but never developed into a decisive comparative weight against PRO's framework. CON also suffered a recurring format execution cost: three speeches (Turns 2, 4, and 8) were clipped for exceeding the word limit, leaving arguments incomplete at critical moments, including the closing sentence of Rebuttal 1 and the final clause of Rebuttal 2. These are genuine losses. The fire door analogy from PRO Rebuttal 1 was the most memorable individual move and went effectively unanswered in substance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 identifying that CON's "reasonable export" standard collapses without structural procurement pressure: "unless procurement prioritizes portability, 'reasonable' shrinks to whatever the incumbent can live with." CON had left this hole open since their opening, and PRO correctly turned it into the decisive closing wedge.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by effectively demonstrating that the present-day operational costs of 'seam risk' outweigh the future governance benefits of portability. Side B successfully diminished the marginal benefit of Side A's world by showing that the largest costs of switching (retraining, validation) remain intact regardless of open standards, while the marginal costs of multi-vendor environments cannot simply be contracted away.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's takedown of the "fire door" analogy in B2.5, pointing out that fire doors are pure safety additions, whereas portability adds interfaces, attack surfaces, and governance burdens.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by establishing a clearer weighing: favoring open standards disciplines vendors on long-run governability and bargaining power without banning functional integration, while CON's seam and complexity concerns were addressed as contract-manageable but never fully offset the conceded lock-in risks; B directly answered pressure questions and maintained the presumption comparison. A's clipped execution and vagueness on "reasonable" export weakened its rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2, directly answering A's pressure questions on reasonable export and responsibility while reframing lock-in as an operational failure that contracts cannot cure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): PRO built a coherent and progressively sharpened case across all rounds. Three things tipped the balance. First, PRO correctly identified that CON's own endorsed safeguards (data ownership, usable export, transition support, open APIs) constitute exactly the preference the motion calls for, just dressed as neutral "baseline." CON never escaped this compression: by endorsing those terms as mandatory, it tacitly conceded the core of the proposition while arguing about framing. Second, PRO's leverage argument was the most analytically decisive move in the debate: switching does not need to be easy to generate real bargaining power; it needs to be *possible rather than catastrophic*. "A penalty clause against an irreplaceable vendor is a plea; backed by portable data and interfaces it is leverage" is a clean, memorable formulation that CON's "contract and governance" response never materially refuted. Third, PRO's closing landed a damaging point CON could not answer: if portability is only a floor, incumbent vendors can design around its letter while preserving practical dependency. CON's "minimum floor, then buy the most coherent stack" model leaves the door open for exactly the lock-in scenario PRO diagnosed. CON's strongest arguments—daily operational coherence, finger-pointing in multi-vendor environments, switching costs still dominating—were real and consistently pushed. But they were somewhat circular: they described why switching is hard without showing why procurement should not require architectures that make it less catastrophic. CON's "shrinking the motion" accusation also landed initially, but PRO's rebuttal 2 reframed it as "governed integration vs. captive integration," which answered the shrinkage charge directly. CON scored points on PRO's own concession that integration should win when the safety advantage is "concrete and essential," but PRO's answering rule—judge reliability over the life of the service, not just launch—was adequate. On balance, PRO made the debate easier to decide in its favor across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal 2 and closing synthesis: the argument that CON's endorsed safeguards collapse its own distinction ("favoring portability vs. targeted protections"), combined with the closing line that a portability floor can be designed around by incumbents, left CON's operative model without a stable middle ground. This exchange was the pivot of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins a highly competitive debate by effectively weaponizing Side A's own concessions. When Side A admits that retraining and workflow redesign dominate switching costs, Side B successfully argues that open standards cannot offer the seamless exit leverage Side A promises. Side B's pragmatic focus on operational coherence edges out Side A's compelling but slightly idealized case for governed integration.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, specifically when B caught A's rule that integration should win if its safety advantage is "concrete and essential." B used this to seamlessly reframe the core features of integrated stacks as precisely the concrete daily safety case needed in schools and hospitals.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by building a clearer path from lock-in to weakened accountability in essential services, using pressure questions to force CON into conceding protections while showing why a mere floor allows design-around. CON maintains operational coherence but never neutralizes how absent portability makes sanctions ineffective when switching remains costly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-A1/A2 directly answers A's pressure on safety thresholds and sanctions, crystallizing that portability converts contract terms into credible leverage without requiring modularity extremes.
