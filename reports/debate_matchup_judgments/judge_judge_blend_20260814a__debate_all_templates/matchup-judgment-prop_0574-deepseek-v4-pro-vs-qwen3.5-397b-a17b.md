### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but PRO (Side A) holds the edge on the core weighing question despite three execution costs from clipping. PRO's most durable argument—that clearly labeled commercial exploitation of a specific dead artist's voice still harms identity even without deceiving anyone, and that fraud law and labeling therefore fail to address the real injury—was never squarely answered. CON's "prior restraint vs. accountability" frame is rhetorically effective and the documentary-reconstruction example genuinely showed that PRO's rule is over-broad in its margins. However, the documentary case actually supports PRO's position rather than defeating it: an estate asked for permission to reconstruct a lost performance for educational purposes would very plausibly grant it. CON's fraud-law substitute collapses precisely on non-deceptive commercial exploitation—a studio releasing a clearly labeled synthetic album in a dead singer's voice, over the family's objection—which is the motion's heartland and where CON had no real answer. PRO's "just asking" defense against the tollbooth accusation was underused but valid, and the intestate-estate answer (probate administrators, fiduciary duty) was direct and adequate. CON executed more cleanly across the rounds (no clips, crisper framing), which closes the gap, but the core liability-of-identity argument survives the full exchange in a way CON's substitute regime does not.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was around PRO's Q2 pressure question—asking CON to name one case where a performance indistinguishable from a dead artist should be allowed without permission and explain why it wouldn't exploit their identity. CON answered with the educational documentary scenario and the "labeling prevents deception" response. PRO then correctly identified that the harm is not merely deception but identity exploitation itself, and that a label on a commercial product doesn't undo the economic appropriation or the family's objection. CON never provided a mechanism to block clearly-labeled but non-deceptive commercial exploitation, leaving a live gap that fraud law cannot fill—and that gap is where the motion's core application sits.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): Side A wins narrowly. Side B made a strong overbreadth case about style, estates as veto points, and labeling or fraud law as less restrictive alternatives. But Side A more directly tied the motion to studios and labels commercially generating a specific dead artist's voice or likeness, and persuasively argued that disclosure does not cure the non-deceptive exploitation of identity. Side A's repeated clipping is an execution cost, and Side B kept the debate close, but Side B never fully solved the core permission and consent harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest rebuttal was that labels and fraud law only address deception, not the underlying unauthorized appropriation of a dead artist's recognizable identity; this directly weakened Side B's main alternative to permission.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by framing the motion as prior restraint that hands risk-averse estates a veto over legitimate cultural reuse, while offering less restrictive alternatives (fraud laws and transparent labeling) that target actual harms without silencing innovation. Side B (PRO) maintained a strong moral intuition that identity is not public property, but never sufficiently proved that non-deceptive, labeled AI uses cause enough harm to justify mandatory consent, and suffered significant execution costs from clipped speeches in the opening, first rebuttal, and closing that left key arguments underdeveloped and final crystallization incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answers to Side B's pressure questions, showing existing fraud laws already prohibit deceptive commercial use and that transparent labeling in educational contexts preserves access without granting heirs a veto, sharply weighing that prior restraint kills legitimate art while ex-post accountability punishes real abuse.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): CON (Side B) built a cleaner analytical path across the debate. Three live arguments survived the exchange in CON's favor. First, the double-standard point—tribute bands routinely replicate specific timbres for profit without estate permission—was never fully neutralized. PRO's "biological instrument" counter has intuitive pull but CON rightly answered that the distinction collapses into speed, not kind. Second, CON's reframe that a blanket permission rule hands estates a permanent rent-extracting monopoly was clever and PRO's response ("estates control commodification, not scrapers") inadvertently conceded the mechanism while calling it a feature. Third, and most decisively, CON's point that fair use is an expensive courtroom defense rather than a shield—so the threat of litigation chills legitimate studio projects before any court ever rules—went materially unanswered. PRO's scope-limitation move (the motion targets commercial studios, not parodists) partially defused the censorship concern but did not close it, because studios do make biopics and critical documentaries that would require permission under the motion. PRO's best unrebutted points—the permanent-displacement argument (models don't age, human mimics do) and the genuine legal gap (estates struggling against unauthorized AI singles)—were real contributions but insufficient to overcome CON's stronger analytical work on overbreadth and the double standard. PRO's rhetoric and moral framing were more vivid, but the judging criteria rewards argument over polish when they diverge. The clipping of several CON speeches is noted; it does not materially change the assessment because CON's surviving arguments were already sufficient.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point [B4.2] that "fair use is an expensive courtroom defense, not a shield" directly dismantled PRO's primary answer to the censorship concern. PRO had argued that fair use protects parody and commentary, so the permission requirement would not suppress legitimate expression. CON responded that the litigation threat alone chills creative projects before any court ruling, and that PRO's own concession—estates can block anything that "undermines the artist's values"—is a euphemism for suppressing unflattering or critical work. PRO never closed this exchange, and it remained live through the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): B won by making the overbreadth problem decisive: the motion covers “style” as well as likeness and risks giving estates a prior veto over homage, biopics, parody, and criticism. A had strong dignity and consent rhetoric, but often collapsed style into identity and relied on an underdeveloped claim that existing law leaves estates powerless. B’s narrower-alternative framing—target false endorsement and real misappropriation without licensing cultural influence—was the cleaner path to deciding the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s AI-versus-human distinction was most decisive: it argued that a human mimic can also sell timbre, so A’s “biological instrument” line does not justify turning vocal style into estate-controlled property, especially where false endorsement law already addresses passing off.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): CON built the stronger path by showing the motion collapses unownable style into protectable likeness without justification, demonstrating that existing law already targets genuine harms like false endorsement, and proving a blanket permission rule would hand estates a censorious veto over ordinary cultural expression. PRO’s moral framing remained resonant but never overcame the arbitrary double standard between AI and human mimicry or the overbreadth problem, and its reliance on fair use failed to answer the chilling effect on speech.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal, which exposed PRO’s “biological instrument” distinction as arbitrary and showed that the motion would turn vocal style into a permanent, state-enforced tollbooth for estates who never created the original art.
