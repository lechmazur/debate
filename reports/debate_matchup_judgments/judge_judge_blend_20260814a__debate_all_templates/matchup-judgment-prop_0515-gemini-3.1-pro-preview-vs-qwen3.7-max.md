### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A built a more durable case and answered the critical pressure more cleanly. Three core advantages carried the debate. First, A's historical three-cueing argument was never neutralized: B's answer—invest in teacher training—is compatible with mandates and does not explain why autonomy alone would prevent the return of debunked practices. B essentially conceded the historical problem and offered a reform that A could equally claim. Second, A's equity inversion—that vulnerable students (dyslexics, ELLs) are precisely the populations most harmed by unstructured autonomy and most helped by explicit sequenced instruction—was never squarely answered. B's culturally responsive materials point was cut off by A's sharp counter that cultural relevance is inaccessible to a child who cannot decode the words on the page, and B did not recover this. Third, in Rebuttal 2, B's answer to Q2 partially conceded the point by acknowledging that autonomous teachers would still use sequenced decodables—blurring the distinction B had been defending. By contrast, A's answers to B's pressure questions were specific: knowledge-building curricula integrate content into the reading block (answering cannibalization), and small-group intervention frameworks address pacing without penalizing struggling students. B's strongest individual move was the "floor becomes ceiling" / pacing-guide argument, but A addressed it adequately and it never rose to rebut the historical record claim. A's closing crystallized the stakes cleanly as "guarantee vs. gamble," a frame B never fully dismantled. B's closing recycled earlier points without sharpening the comparison. The debate was won on argument progression, not just opening strength.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal at [A4.5]—"cultural relevance is entirely useless to a child who has never been systematically taught how to decode the syllables in front of them"—was the most decisive exchange. B had argued that mandated curricula harm ELL and vulnerable students by forcing sterile, culturally disconnected decodables. A reversed the equity frame sharply: engagement in a text presupposes the ability to read it. B did not adequately respond to this in the closing, leaving A's equity argument standing and B's cultural-responsiveness point effectively neutralized.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): B won by making the motion easier to decide: structured instruction as a guaranteed floor versus autonomy as an instructional lottery. A raised real concerns about comprehension, motivation, pacing, and teacher judgment, but repeatedly attacked a rigid-script caricature rather than B's narrower claim that policy should prioritize evidence-based structure while preserving some teacher discretion. A's answer that teacher training alone prevents a return to three-cueing was less persuasive than B's systemic mandate argument, and A's concession that vulnerable students need explicit phonics and sequenced decodables weakened the case for prioritizing autonomy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was pressing A on what mechanism prevents debunked practices under autonomy, then framing teacher preparation as an unreliable gamble compared with a structured baseline mandate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B wins: stronger opening on science/equity, decisive pressure answers (knowledge-building + small groups), and clean guarantee-vs-gamble crystallization in closing that directly neutralized CON's historical and cultural-relevance claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4) directly answered both pressure questions and reframed stakes around historical lottery vs guaranteed floor.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A (PRO) wins on the strength of a more durable through-line. Its equity argument—that structured instruction guarantees a floor precisely for the most vulnerable students who cannot rely on environmental literacy support—was the debate's decisive claim and was never squarely neutralized. CON's best counter was that ELL and neurodivergent learners need vocabulary integration alongside decoding, producing "mechanical word-calling" otherwise. PRO's direct answer—that read-alouds and vocabulary work can sit alongside a structured phonics sequence, and that ELL students specifically need more explicit phonological instruction, not less—was grounded and largely unanswered. PRO's pressure question Q2 exposed a real tension in CON's ELL argument (stopping phonics to build vocabulary delays decoding without guaranteeing comprehension), and CON's A2 answer—that meaning-making is the actual goal—did not resolve the sequencing problem. CON landed real hits: the pacing-calendar enforcement mechanism (making "flexible framework" illusory in practice) and the "what happens when publisher tiers also fail?" challenge were genuine pressure. PRO's answer on the tiers question was adequate but thin. Still, CON's alternative model—an "arsenal of evidence-based modules" organized by teacher professional judgment—never clearly escaped PRO's lottery framing; it amounts to autonomy with better shelf stock, which PRO correctly identified as fragmentation risk. PRO's delivery-versus-curriculum-design distinction held well enough across rebuttals to preserve the core argument. Both sides were competent and engaged the live clash; PRO's case simply had a cleaner internal logic and the stronger equity ground throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal at A2.3–A2.4 was the most decisive exchange: against CON's ELL-autonomy argument, PRO conceded the value of vocabulary and culturally relevant texts but argued they need not displace structured phonics—read-alouds can coexist with the decoding sequence, and a child who cannot decode gains nothing from cultural relevance alone. CON never answered why integrating vocabulary requires abandoning the structured sequence rather than layering alongside it, leaving PRO's equity argument intact on the most contested ground.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): B won by keeping the motion focused on prioritizing structured instruction without eliminating teacher judgment. B gave the clearer equity and mechanism case: early decoding requires systematic instruction, and leaving scope and sequence to individual teachers creates uneven access. A’s rigidity concerns were plausible, but often attacked a stricter scripted-publisher mandate than the proposition required, and A never fully solved B’s educational-lottery objection beyond asserting professional expertise and sound modules.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive exchange was distinguishing differentiation from curriculum design in response to A’s pressure questions, while arguing that struggling readers need more systematic support rather than bespoke teacher sequencing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): B wins by maintaining the delivery-vs-design distinction, directly answering pressure questions on adaptation and failure cases, and crystallizing the equity lottery risk for vulnerable students without evasion. A leaves the inconsistency of module selection and vocabulary halting insufficiently resolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2 format) directly neutralized A's autonomy-as-safety-net claim by clarifying escalation stays inside the structured tiers.
