### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A sharply exposed contextual, privacy, and gaming problems, especially through the memorable distinction between noise for workers and a leash on managers. But its decisive compression claim repeatedly assumed that publishing role-level bands makes individual raises or salaries public, which the mandate does not require. Side B therefore retained the stronger comparison: imperfect ranges and rates still provide workers with information and accountability unavailable under secrecy, while discretion remains within the band and privacy can be protected through suppression or aggregation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s distinction between publishing a range and imposing a locked pay grid was decisive. It undercut Side A’s compression mechanism, and Side A never established why role-level disclosure alone would reveal individual premiums or force every raise into a public referendum.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): CON won by expertly defending their core mechanisms against PRO's strongest attacks. PRO attempted a clever strategic trap, arguing CON could not claim the published bands were simultaneously 'worthless noise' and 'expensive enough to cause pay compression.' CON brilliantly dismantled this in Rebuttal 2, explaining the asymmetry: wide, gamed bands are noise to the worker trying to price their labor, but a leash on the manager, for whom top-of-band raises become a public referendum, driving compression. Because CON protected this mechanism, their harms decisively outweighed PRO's benefits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Second Rebuttal untangling PRO's 'worthless vs expensive' trap by establishing the asymmetric impact: 'Noise to the price-taker; leash on the price-setter.'
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A wins by successfully dismantling Side B's central paradox in the final speeches. Side B argued that pay bands are both "noise" to workers and a "leash" on managers, but Side A sharply pointed out that a leash that constrains a manager from lowballing is, by definition, providing a price floor to the worker. Side A also landed a decisive blow on the "outside offer" argument, noting that competing bids only price an exit, not the current role. Side B's arguments on compression and Goodhart's law were strong and realistic, but Side A's weighing and rhetorical precision in the closing secured the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing attack on Side B's "noise vs. leash" paradox, pointing out that if a published band constrains a manager's discretion, it cannot simultaneously be useless noise to the worker.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that most valuable discretion survives within ranges, bonuses, levels, and off-cycle adjustments, while secrecy leaves workers without the information needed to negotiate or investigate bias. Side A effectively distinguished private firms from rigid public pay systems and noted that bands are coarser than actual pay records, but its predicted cage was overstated: it never established that ordinary exceptions would generally become impossible, and its claim that firms would improve promotion-rate optics by delaying promotions was especially weak. B also supplied the cleaner weighing: limited compliance friction for large employers versus a widespread information asymmetry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer that promoting less cannot improve a published promotion rate, followed by the broader point that Side A’s alternatives—complaints, discovery, and market signals—require information that secrecy withholds. Side A’s reply that firms seek a clean rather than high number was inventive but insufficiently explained and did not restore the predicted gaming mechanism.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively exposing the circularity of Side B's alternative ("targeted enforcement"). Side A successfully argued that discovery requires a claim, a claim requires suspicion, and suspicion requires the very facts Side B wants to keep secret. While Side B won the sub-debate on how promotion rates might be gamed for a "clean" number, Side A's overarching structural argument regarding information asymmetry and the impossibility of enforcing rights in the dark remained the most compelling framework for the round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's persistent pressing on the "discovery" circularity in Rebuttal 1, Pressure Questions, and Closing completely dismantled Side B's reliance on existing discrimination law as a viable alternative to the mandate.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A successfully controlled the framing of the debate by redefining "managerial flexibility" as "unaccountability" and forcing Side B to defend hidden exceptions. Side A's logical dismantling of the promotion-rate gaming argument and the sharp pressure questions regarding how targeted enforcement works without initial data created a circularity in Side B's case that Side B never fully escaped. Side B's arguments about corporate risk aversion and wage flattening were strong, but Side A's empirical precedents and rhetorical precision secured the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, specifically the logical trap pointing out that stalling promotions to protect a promotion rate actually lowers the published number, forcing Side B to awkwardly pivot to wanting a "clean" rather than "high" number, which validated Side A's gaming concerns.
