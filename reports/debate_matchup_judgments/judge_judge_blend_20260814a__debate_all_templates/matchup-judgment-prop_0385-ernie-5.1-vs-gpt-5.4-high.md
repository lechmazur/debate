### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): CON built and maintained the debate's decisive structural argument: the motion is absolute ("abolish… even when majorities see them as necessary"), so PRO must show that no narrowly drawn, religion-neutral, court-supervised version could ever be legitimate. CON exploited this burden consistently from Rebuttal 1 onward and PRO never squarely neutralized it. CON's flashpoint-engineering gap—the provocateur who stays just short of explicit incitement—was introduced in B1.4, survived into Rebuttal 2, and PRO's answer (existing public-order law "already covers" it) was asserted rather than demonstrated. CON's pressure questions were sharper: Q2 about consistency with harassment law forced PRO into a response that, while reasonable (blasphemy targets abstract objects, not persons), did not close the gap on staged communal provocations. PRO's case was strongest on concrete examples (Pakistan, Bangladesh, Iran) and the opening was compelling, but the strategy of treating "often abused" as "inherently impossible" was correctly called out by CON in B2.1 and B4.4 and never fully answered. PRO's closing recycled earlier material without crystallizing a clean reason why the impossibility claim defeated the abstract-but-defensible possibility CON was defending. CON's closing was the tightest speech of the debate, clearly identifying the decision rule: if even one defensible narrow retention exists, the absolute motion fails. That framing was live and unanswered going into judgment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 [B2.1]–[B2.3] turning "abuse" into "reform not abolish" and then maintaining it through the closing was the most decisive exchange. PRO's counter that reform has never produced an immune version was plausible but asserted without clinching the impossibility argument the absolute motion requires. CON's consistent use of the burden-of-proof framing against an absolute motion kept this pressure alive throughout.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side A won by directly answering B's pressure questions with existing public-order/incitement coverage, exposing that B's narrow drafting still relies on the same subjective judgments shown to enable abuse, and maintaining the motion's absolute standard without evasion or late evidence dumps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4) directly answering both Qs with concrete coverage argument and distinguishing harassment from abstract sacrilege.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): PRO successfully established that blasphemy laws structurally require the state to make theological judgments that are inherently prone to majoritarian abuse, and that existing public-order and harassment statutes already cover conduct that truly threatens violence. CON never overcame the structural dilemma: if the targeted conduct threatens a community, existing law reaches it; if it does not, criminalizing it is mere viewpoint discrimination. PRO’s distinction between harassment of identifiable persons and blasphemy’s unlimited theological target collapsed CON’s analogies, and CON’s reliance on hypothetical "carefully cabined" laws lacked concrete grounding compared to PRO’s documented pattern of abuse. While CON effectively framed the motion as absolute, PRO showed that abolition is justified even in the edge cases CON described.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.4), where PRO demonstrated that CON’s flashpoint scenario is already covered by public-order and harassment law, and drew the decisive distinction between intent-based statutes targeting identifiable persons versus those requiring judges to weigh theological significance, effectively collapsing CON’s "hard but possible" defense.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by building and maintaining a logically airtight dilemma that Side B never escaped: either a blasphemy law tracks genuine incitement—making it redundant with existing incitement and public-order laws—or it goes beyond incitement, in which case it legalizes the heckler's veto. This framing, crystallized in A's closing, was present from the opening and sharpened in each round. Side B's flagship Pakistan example actually backfired: A correctly turned it into evidence that blasphemy laws empower mob censorship rather than restrain it, and B never effectively answered this turn. B's claim that the laws protect minorities was asserted repeatedly but under-supported, while A consistently showed how they are most easily weaponized against vulnerable groups. B's best move—arguing that blasphemy statutes apply a narrow incitement threshold with judicial safeguards—effectively conceded the first horn of A's dilemma (making the laws redundant), without solving the second. Side A's pressure questions were sharper and more targeted than B's, and A's answers to B's questions were direct and structurally damaging ("one model tells extremists you are criminals, the other tells them you have leverage"). B's emotional closing ("funeral pyres," "body count") had rhetorical force but the underlying logic—blame the provocation because mobs act before courts can—would justify almost any pre-emptive speech restriction, a problem A had already identified as the heckler's veto written into law. Side B's case was competent and raised genuine fragility concerns, but it never offered a principled limiting principle that survived A's scrutiny.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing dilemma is the most decisive exchange: "Either the law tracks genuine incitement, in which case it is redundant, or it goes beyond incitement, in which case it legalizes the heckler's veto. In both cases, it should be abolished." This was the clearest formulation in the debate, and B's prior answers (claiming narrow incitement thresholds and judicial safeguards) had already conceded the first horn, leaving the second unanswered when B had no further speech to respond.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by maintaining a consistent, grounded distinction between incitement and blasphemy, directly neutralizing B’s public-order and minority-protection claims across rebuttals and pressure rounds, and crystallizing the heckler’s-veto incentive in closing. B repeatedly restated early assertions without squarely meeting A’s pressure questions or rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (A4), which opened with labeled A1/A2 answers to B’s pressure questions and then weighed why legalizing the veto worsens order.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing that blasphemy laws criminalize peaceful speech because others might react violently, creating a state-sanctioned heckler’s veto. It turned the Pakistan example against CON and showed that ordinary public-order laws already address incitement and mob violence without punishing dissenters, while CON never solved the redundancy of blasphemy laws alongside ordinary incitement statutes. B’s closing crystallized the decisive dilemma: blasphemy laws are either redundant with incitement law or they punish protected irreverence, and either way abolition is justified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was B’s Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 reframing of CON’s Pakistan example and public-order defense: PRO showed that blasphemy laws predictably lead police to arrest the vulnerable speaker rather than control the violent crowd, and that if speech truly constitutes incitement then ordinary laws already cover it, making blasphemy statutes either redundant or a tool for criminalizing peaceful irreverence. CON’s failure to squarely answer why a distinct blasphemy category is necessary—beyond relabeling provocation as incitement—allowed this rebuttal to stick and define the rest of the debate.
