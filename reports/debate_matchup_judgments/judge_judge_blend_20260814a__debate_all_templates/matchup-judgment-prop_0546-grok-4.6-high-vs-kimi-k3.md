### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-adapted cases, but CON's structural critique of the firewall (appointment capture, statute amendment by future majorities, litigation routing through the same captured judiciary) was never fully neutralized — PRO's best reply was a tu quoque ("records laws can be amended too") that undercuts PRO's own alternative-beating claim as much as CON's. CON's polarization mechanism (public funding is "born pre-contested," producing in-group legitimacy and out-group grievance simultaneously) also survived better than PRO's rebuttal suggested, and CON's closing directly dissolved PRO's signature "contradiction" gotcha by showing that asymmetric trust across audiences is not self-contradictory but is exactly how state-adjacent media typically functions. PRO's strongest counter — that CON's "zero-risk substitutes" haven't materialized in real news deserts — landed hard in the pressure round, but CON's answer (distance-funded nonprofits/clinics, framed as a policy-underfunding problem rather than a structural impossibility) was an adequate, if somewhat under-grounded, parry. On balance CON's risk-asymmetry argument (centralized, hard-to-reverse capture vs. fragmented private capture) proved more resilient across rounds and its final speech converted pressure-round answers into the cleanest closing weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's answer to PRO's closing "contradiction" — that a single desk can simultaneously legitimize (for the funding majority) and alienate (for the opposition), which is not contradictory but is the actual mechanism of state-adjacent media capture — was the most decisive exchange, since it defused PRO's cleanest late-round attack rather than merely restating prior points.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): B won by keeping the comparison focused on whether a firewalled reporting desk improves upon the existing news vacuum. Its corruption mechanism was concrete, and it showed that records laws or hypothetical nonprofits do not themselves supply continuous scrutiny. A powerfully established distrust and quiet-capture risks, especially through its split-audience analysis, but repeatedly overstated substitutes as carrying zero capture risk and treated the motion as requiring public funding to beat every alternative rather than produce a net improvement. B's safeguards need not be perfect or permanent to raise scrutiny substantially, so its overall path remained more plausible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive response was that A's substitutes had not actually refilled news deserts: records laws need someone paid to file requests and attend meetings, while possible future amendment is not unique to journalism policy. This undercut A's effort to compare the proposal chiefly against an idealized zero-risk alternative rather than the existing vacuum.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by strictly enforcing the comparative burden of the motion. While Side B effectively argued that public funding is better than the current void of local journalism, Side A successfully introduced zero-capture-risk substitutes (nonprofits, state-level enforcement) and forced Side B to defend public funding against them. Side A also delivered a logically airtight rebuttal in the closing round, resolving Side B's alleged contradiction by explaining how state media simultaneously validates the in-group and polarizes the out-group. Side A's argument that a captured desk "certifies innocence" was a highly original and decisive point on the systemic risks of partisan capture.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing response to Side B's contradiction claim, explaining that a publicly funded desk doesn't need universal trust to certify innocence (just the majority's trust) nor universal distrust to polarize (just the minority's distrust), perfectly capturing the dual nature of state-affiliated media.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-matched causal stories, but A better managed the burden of proof across the exchange. A anchored its case in a measurable baseline (documented harms of news deserts) and repeatedly forced B to either supply a counter-example of firewalled capture or concede the risk was speculative. B's strongest move—distinguishing evidence about dying private papers from evidence about the specific public-funding intervention—was genuinely damaging and never fully answered by A's "ownership is the wrapper" assertion. But B's own rejoinder to the pressure question (capture is "invisible," "the series that never starts") backed it into an unfalsifiable corner that A exploited decisively in closing, showing that an unobservable harm cannot outweigh a documented one and that the same logic would indict private ownership too. B's final closing reasserted its framing without directly rebutting the unfalsifiability charge, leaving A's last word standing as the cleaner resolution of the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing turn on B4.1 ("capture is invisible, the series that never starts") — showing this concession makes B's core harm unfalsifiable and self-defeating — was the most decisive moment, and B's closing never directly answered it.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by pairing a well-grounded account of the measurable harms from news deserts with a comparative explanation of why governed, imperfect coverage is still better than no watchdog. Side A sharply identified the intervention gap between losing private papers and subsidizing replacements, but its decisive claim—that public funding necessarily severs independence and makes coverage worse than absence—remained largely asserted and overly absolute. B also showed that hidden funder pressure is not unique to public funding and that records requests, archives, plural scrutiny, and firewall design preserve meaningful deterrence even without universal trust.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was against A’s “invisible capture” answer: if an investigation that never starts proves capture, the claim becomes difficult to test and applies equally to advertiser- or owner-dependent private newsrooms. That materially weakened A’s attempt to treat public funding as uniquely disqualifying while B retained the clearer comparison between conditional capture risk and documented harms from an empty chair.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side A won by decisively dismantling Side B's core analogies and evidence. Side A successfully argued that journalists, unlike judges or auditors, rely entirely on the public's perception of independence; therefore, a publicly funded paper would be dismissed as "official stationery," increasing polarization and giving corrupt officials a "someone was watching" alibi. Side A also effectively neutralized Side B's reliance on news-desert studies by pointing out they measure the loss of independent private papers, not the impact of public funding. Side B's case was competent but struggled to overcome these sharp conceptual distinctions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the auditor/judge analogy, explaining that judicial power relies on hard authority (subpoenas, sentencing) while journalistic power relies entirely on public trust, which is destroyed if the paper is perceived as government-funded.
