### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON (Side A) built a more structurally complete and analytically honest case across the full debate. Three key factors tip the balance. First, the false-positive/permanent-harm counter-framing was well-developed and never truly neutralized: CON correctly observed that the PRO "temporary vs. permanent" asymmetry collapses when false positives impose immediate and irremediable job loss, housing instability, and family separation on people who would never have offended. PRO's rebuttal was mostly rhetorical repetition rather than a fresh rejoinder to this structural problem. Second, CON's threshold-creep argument—once statistical forecasts justify detention, the category of "high risk" expands over time with no built-in brake—went substantively unanswered. PRO's "rebuttable presumption" response addressed contestability in individual cases but said nothing about systemic category-widening incentives. Third, when PRO's pressure questions rightly demanded a concrete positive alternative, CON answered directly and specifically: "clear and convincing evidence of imminent dangerousness shown through specific acts, credible threats, or recent violations in an adversarial hearing." By contrast, PRO's answer to CON's threshold-accuracy question was vague ("high probability… that non-custodial supervision cannot safely mitigate"), and PRO never quantified or operationalized that standard. PRO's strongest material was genuine: the GPS-cannot-stop-a-bullet point landed each time it was used, and the irreversible-victim-harm framing carried real moral weight. But these points were not decisive because CON's layered response—enhanced monitoring plus swift revocation plus credible-threat warrants—was adequate, and because PRO's structural reliance on an asymmetry that depends on ignoring false positives was a durable vulnerability. CON's closing crystallized the dispositive issue cleanly: detention severed from demonstrated conduct shifts the burden to statistical group membership, eroding the presumption of innocence without a limiting principle. That was the cleaner, better-grounded reason to vote against the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 response to PRO's pressure question was the most decisive single exchange. PRO's Q2 put CON in the difficult position of having to name a concrete actionable alternative to both algorithmic tools and judicial discretion—a real burden. CON answered directly: adversarial hearings, clear and convincing evidence of imminent dangerousness, specific acts or credible threats. This simultaneously satisfied the demand for a workable standard and sharpened the core distinction between individualized-conduct-based detention and group-statistical detention, which became the organizing frame for CON's close. PRO's parallel A2 (risk score as auditable rebuttable presumption) was weaker because it addressed contestability without addressing category-widening incentives.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): PRO successfully framed the debate around the moral asymmetry between temporary detention and permanent violent harm, a weighing that the motion itself invites by accepting increased detention rates. While CON landed strong blows on algorithmic bias and false positives, PRO neutralized them by showing risk tools are more auditable and contestable than the human discretion CON implicitly relies on, and offered a concrete "rebuttable presumption" framework. Most decisively, PRO undermined CON's primary alternative—electronic monitoring—by demonstrating it cannot physically prevent imminent serious violence, leaving CON without a sufficient shield for high-risk defendants. PRO's closing crystallized this clash cleanly, whereas CON's closing slightly ignored PRO's refinements and overstated the slippery-slope inevitability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's sustained attack on electronic monitoring as an alternative to detention, showing that GPS and supervision record but cannot physically block serious violent reoffending, which blunted CON's main counter-proposal and reinforced the necessity of physical separation for the highest-risk cases.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side B wins by successfully challenging PRO's "temporary harm" frame with concrete collateral consequences and by providing a legally grounded alternative standard (clear and convincing evidence of imminent danger based on specific acts) that directly answered PRO's pressure questions. While PRO landed strong rhetorical blows on the irreversibility of violent crime, its claim that rejecting detention guarantees a "100% certainty" of harm was a logical overreach that weakened its weighing. CON consistently maintained that severing detention from demonstrated conduct erodes the presumption of innocence and scales false-positive errors, offering a cleaner, more disciplined path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 directly answered PRO's pressure questions by articulating a "clear and convincing evidence" standard tied to specific acts, effectively neutralizing PRO's claim that CON had no workable alternative while preserving the presumption of innocence.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Three arguments did the decisive work. First, the rare-event false-positive math: predicting statistically uncommon violent reoffending necessarily generates massive false-positive rates, so broader detention incapacitates many peaceful defendants for each genuine threat averted. PRO never gave a satisfying answer to this; the cash-bail comparison redirects but does not engage the math. Second, "predicting arrests, not behavior": CON correctly noted that tools trained on racially and socioeconomically skewed arrest data encode and amplify existing disparities under a veneer of objectivity. PRO's response—that cash bail embeds the same biases without recalibration—was adequate as a deflection but did not rebut the core point that launder-by-algorithm makes judicial scrutiny harder, not easier. Third, criminogenic destabilization: CON argued that brief detention causes lasting employment, housing, and family damage that elevates long-term recidivism, creating more future victims than the temporary incapacitation prevents. PRO's time-horizon answer (immediate victimizations outweigh downstream instability) was the best PRO offered, but it was asserted rather than weighed; it did not engage the asymmetry between a temporary pause and permanent life disruption. CON's "false choice" reframe—we need not choose between cash bail and algorithmic mass detention; robust supervision is available—was clean and repeatable, and PRO never neutralized it. PRO's victim-centered framing and consistent cash-bail comparison were genuinely strong and kept the debate competitive, but PRO's shorter opening left three CON lines largely uncontested from the start, and PRO's pressure-question answers leaned too heavily on the same comparative rather than squarely engaging CON's statistical and structural critiques. The criminogenic-effects argument grew stronger across rounds rather than weaker, which is the mark of a live unresolved burden. CON answered the A1/A2 format requirement properly and sharpened the comparison in later rounds rather than merely repeating earlier points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's "false choice" reframe in Rebuttal 1 [A2.4] was the most decisive single exchange. PRO's central structural move throughout was "cash bail has the same problems, so tools at least redirect detention more purposefully." CON correctly identified this as a false dichotomy—the proposition does not force a choice between two biased systems; robust pretrial supervision is available—materially damaging PRO's comparative foundation and shifting the burden back onto PRO to defend algorithmic expansion on its own merits, which PRO never fully did.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A wins by establishing that risk-based tools redirect existing detention capacity from low-risk cash-bail defendants toward a narrower high-risk group, making the net trade more purposeful than the status quo. B never neutralizes this comparative frame, instead treating false positives as added to a zero-detention baseline and invoking an underdeveloped pretrial-supervision alternative. A answers B's pressure questions more directly and crystallizes the decisive clash—preventable serious violence versus statistical error—more cleanly in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where A answers B's pressure questions by showing the comparative scale of current cash-bail injustice and weighing immediate victimization against long-term destabilization.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a cleaner comparative baseline and more decisive weighing. A consistently contrasted risk-based tools with the status quo (cash bail and unaided discretion), arguing that the proposition redirects existing detention capacity toward a narrower, high-risk tail rather than blindly expanding incarceration. A effectively neutralized B’s false-positive and criminogenic arguments by prioritizing the prevention of immediate, irreversible harms during the high-risk pretrial window, framing downstream instability as a secondary cost compared to concrete victimization. B made strong statistical and moral arguments about base rates and systemic bias, but struggled to fully answer A’s comparative point that human discretion is equally flawed and less auditable. B’s dismissal of the cash-bail comparison as a “false dichotomy” felt evasive given the practical reality of pretrial decision-making. A’s sharper weighing and consistent focus on the motion’s explicit trade-off provided the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 1 and 2 effectively dismantled the criminogenic and bias objections by introducing a time-horizon comparison (immediate incapacitation vs. long-term destabilization) and an auditability comparison (transparent algorithms vs. opaque cash bail), forcing B to defend a status quo that A successfully framed as more dangerous and less accountable.
