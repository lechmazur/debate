### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A wins narrowly. Its consumer-access and product-longevity benefits remained live, especially because Side B never established that requiring OEM access would necessarily outlaw cheap compatible parts or dismantle informal repair. A also plausibly showed that certified pairing access can preserve security without disclosing source code. B created serious implementation, affordability, and enforcement pressure, while A incurred costs from clipped speeches and some shaky international comparisons, but B did not neutralize the environmental benefit and relied too heavily on an unsupported leap from added access to criminalization of the existing repair market.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pairing-tool dilemma was the sharpest exchange: modern repairs may require security-linked calibration, forcing A to explain whether tools include such access. A answered with tiered certified access and security carve-outs, preventing the dilemma from collapsing the proposition, though B effectively showed that this model could burden small technicians.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): CON built the cleaner India-specific path: the informal reverse-engineering ecosystem already serves price-sensitive consumers, while a FRAND-plus-certification law would reclassify the cheap compatible parts those consumers actually use as counterfeit and replace Nehru Place with mall-scale authorized chains. PRO never neutralized the Make-in-India investment risk or the enforcement gap (Maruti auto-parts analogy), and clipping on later turns left key answers unfinished. Ownership and e-waste claims remained abstract against CON’s concrete tradeoff on factories, safety, and the Rs 600 customer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s [A4] turn turning PRO’s pairing-tool concession into proof that the law either becomes theater or imposes the exact bureaucracy that kills informal techs.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by building a highly contextualized case tailored to India's specific economic and regulatory realities, whereas Side A relied on generic Western right-to-repair analogies. Side B effectively neutralized Side A's EU/US comparisons by highlighting India's role as a manufacturing export hub competing for PLI investments. Furthermore, Side B laid a brilliant tactical trap regarding software calibration tools, forcing Side A to concede the need for tiered certification, which Side B then used to prove that the law would destroy the very informal repair ecosystem Side A claimed to protect. Side A also suffered execution costs from clipped speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it capitalized on Side A's concession about pairing access and tiered certification to prove that the compliance bureaucracy would replace informal Nehru Place technicians with mall-based authorized chains, decisively linking the security scope clash back to the economic harm clash.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won because it showed that mandated repair access need not entail disclosure of signing keys or unrestricted safety-critical work, while turning Side B’s proposed traceable parts, licensing, public oversight, and post-warranty access into substantial concessions to a right-to-repair law. Side B’s statutory-design concerns were meaningful, but it repeatedly treated the broad proposition as forbidding safeguards and never established why a national law could not contain the tiering and carve-outs both sides ultimately accepted. Its clipped turns and some overstated cybersecurity claims also weakened execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was the forced-blindness turn: informal technicians already repair banking-enabled phones, so withholding manuals and genuine traceable parts can increase rather than reduce safety risks. Coupled with the point that repair access does not require private firmware keys, this undercut Side B’s central security narrative and made its targeted alternative look like a regulated version of the proposition.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO built the clearer path by anchoring the case in India's repair culture, price gaps, e-waste, and formalization benefits, then consistently showing that CON's security fears over-read the motion (no forced keys) while CON's own "targeted" alternative still required the access mandate PRO defended. Clipped CON openings/rebuttals and weaker adaptation after pressure hurt execution; PRO's answers and weighing on blindness vs. paper-trail liability kept the decisive comparative live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.8): direct A1/A2 on liability/traceability + carve-outs, then collapsing CON's residual alternative as preserving monopoly discretion.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A built a much stronger and more cohesive case, effectively absorbing Side B's safety and security concerns by pointing out that "forced blindness" creates the very risks B fears, and that standard legislative practice delegates specifics to existing regulators like BIS. Side B's main remaining attack—that the motion's text doesn't explicitly name the regulator or certification tiers—was a weak technicality that failed to overcome A's substantive policy defense. Side A's rhetoric and grounding in the Indian context were also superior, and they successfully trapped Side B by showing that B's proposed "alternative" was functionally just a right-to-repair law.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1, where they dismantled the "cyber-weapon" framing by distinguishing between business locks and security locks, and flipped the "YouTube and tweezers" point to show that the lack of official manuals is the actual safety hazard.
