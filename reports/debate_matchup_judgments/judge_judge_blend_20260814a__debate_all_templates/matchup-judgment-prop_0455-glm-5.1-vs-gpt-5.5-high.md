### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but PRO built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Three live arguments sustain that edge. First, PRO's fee-versus-ban analysis was the sharpest analytical move in the debate: a fee high enough to force compliance functions like a ban, and one low enough to preserve noncompliant supply merely licenses the harm. CON's best answer—timing matters for transition—concedes that the final endpoint should be strict limits, which undermines the case for preferring fees. Second, PRO's point that routine flaring literally destroys usable gas, so banning it pushes toward capture and increases available supply over time, was never squarely neutralized; CON's "infrastructure doesn't appear by fiat" response addressed new capture build-out but not leak repair, reinjection, or connection to existing systems. Third, PRO's grandfathering argument in closing—that allowing existing wells to flare without penalty teaches future operators to underinvest in capture and then negotiate—was original, well-placed, and unanswered. CON's strongest single blow was the production-volume-versus-production-method distinction and the "existing wells can't time-travel" counter to PRO's financing answer; these are genuine analytical advances that PRO only partially answered. However, CON's case carried an unresolved internal tension: if a phased approach reaches the same strict endpoint, the import-substitution concern applies equally to CON's own preferred policy, and CON never explained why transitional leakage costs should fall on the public rather than the operator. The clipping of PRO's speeches is a real execution cost, but the visible arguments were consistent and self-reinforcing, and CON's best attack on the existing-wells point was softened by PRO's list of operational options (reinjection, repair, existing system connection, capital reallocation).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's fee-versus-ban argument in Rebuttal 1: if the fee is high enough to compel compliance it is functionally a ban, and if it is low enough to preserve noncompliant supply it merely licenses the damage. CON's response—that the difference is the transition timeline—was the best available answer but conceded that the eventual endpoint must be strict limits, leaving CON arguing only for more leakage in the interim rather than a genuinely different outcome.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively contrasting the 'strictness' of a ban with its mechanical 'effectiveness.' By pointing out that existing wells cannot retroactively finance capture infrastructure, Side A successfully proved that a strict ban would destroy the capital needed for transition and simply displace emissions to dirtier imports.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 observation that Side B's solution to 'finance before drilling' ignores existing wells, which cannot time-travel and instead face mandated obsolescence.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins because its mechanistic critique that strict bans destroy transition funding for existing wells while risking net global emissions rises via imports held up better after full exchange, with direct pressure question responses exposing the gap; Side B's incentive case is clear but insufficiently addresses practicality and displacement effects.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's direct answers to B's pressure questions on infrastructure and global net emissions (turns 6-8), followed by B's rebuttal 2 and A's closing, crystallize the capital-destruction vs. incentive clash.
