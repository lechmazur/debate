### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): B won by narrowing the motion to whether BNPL should be regulated much more like credit cards, not whether it needs any regulation. A established real BNPL harms but leaned heavily on the label that deferred payment equals credit, plus somewhat asserted empirical harm claims. B more effectively showed that key credit-card-style tools, especially APR-style disclosure and affordability checks, can misfit short fixed installment products and harm the credit-constrained users A centered. B also conceded targeted rules, which neutralized much of A's protection case while preserving opposition to the credit-card frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that annualizing a small flat late fee creates a misleading triple-digit APR, paired with the point that A's affordability checks would deny the very credit-constrained users A invoked, was the decisive exchange.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully separating 'consumer protection' from 'credit card regulation.' B effectively turned both of Side A's primary mechanisms—APR disclosures and affordability checks—into liabilities, arguing they would respectively mislead consumers and push vulnerable users into worse debt traps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal cleverly used Side A's admission that users are credit-constrained to demonstrate that Side A's proposed affordability checks would simply lock vulnerable consumers out of credit entirely.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by exposing a fatal tension in Side A's case: advocating credit-card-style affordability checks for a demographic A admits is credit-constrained, which functionally denies them access rather than protecting them. Side B effectively dismantles the APR disclosure proposal by showing that annualizing a one-time flat fee produces a misleading metric that implies compounding interest where none exists. By conceding targeted fixes (fee caps, plain-dollar disclosures, dispute rights) that address A's cited harms without the collateral damage of the credit-card frame, B neutralizes A's offense while maintaining a coherent, proportionate alternative. A's rebuttals and closing fail to answer the substitution question or justify why the credit label should override product-specific risk matching.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing decisively broke Side A's consumer-protection narrative by pressing the substitution effect. When forced to defend where credit-constrained users go after failing affordability checks, A's denial-is-protection response collapsed under B's framing of the payday-lender pipeline, leaving A with no benign destination for the vulnerable consumers they claimed to champion.
