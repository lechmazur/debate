### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Both sides debated competently with genuine clash, but PRO built a marginally stronger path to victory across the full exchange. PRO's externality framing (private gains, socialized pain) was never squarely neutralized, and the distributional justice argument—that automating firms should bear the cost rather than general taxpayers—went essentially unanswered by CON. PRO's most durable comparative advantage was identifying CON's alternative as an unfunded aspiration: "broader social safety nets" funded by unspecified economic growth. CON never supplied a concrete revenue mechanism, and PRO hammered this gap in every round. CON's strongest attack—the self-cannibalizing paradox (deterrence shrinks revenue; non-deterrence means offshoring)—was genuinely sharp and created real pressure. But PRO's response in A4.3 split the firm landscape effectively: non-relocatable businesses (regional manufacturers, last-mile hubs, grocery chains, utilities) cannot offshore and remain the stable revenue base, and firms that do offshore were already leaving under the status quo. CON never dented this distinction. CON did land a real blow by exploiting PRO's A2.2 expansion to "any capital deployment reducing headcount," framing it as a blanket anti-growth penalty. PRO's clarification in A4.1 (headcount-reduction criterion, not mere productivity growth) was workable but not fully airtight. However, this definitional vulnerability did not outweigh PRO's consistent comparative: CON's rival policy captures zero revenue and offers nothing concrete. CON's retraining-as-mirage argument was also somewhat weakened by never engaging PRO's "underfunded and disconnected" rebuttal. Overall, PRO made the comparative choice clearer and left more of its core case standing at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal exchange was PRO's response to CON's self-cannibalizing paradox in A4.3. CON's paradox (deter → no revenue; don't deter → offshore) was structurally compelling, but PRO split the universe of firms: those that can offshore were already leaving, while non-relocatable businesses—regional plants, last-mile hubs, grocery chains, utilities—cannot avoid the tax regardless. This directly neutralized CON's binary by showing a stable, substantial revenue base that survives either horn of the paradox. CON never adequately addressed the non-relocatable segment in response.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A wins a competitive debate by keeping the central comparison clearer: automation creates displacement costs, and the firms causing those costs should fund transition rather than shifting the burden to general taxpayers. Side A adequately answered that the tax targets measurable labor-substituting deployment, need not be perfect to capture substantial onshore automation, and that Side B's alternative safety nets lacked a concrete funding mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was pressing A2.2 as an overbroad tax on productivity and adding the deterrence-revenue paradox; Side A contained enough of that by reframing the standard as labor-substitution with real social costs and by arguing that imperfect capture still beats unfunded general promises.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B wins a clear but competitive debate by landing the most decisive single attack and sustaining it through every round. The struggling-manufacturer-vs.-tech-giant example at [B2.2] is the debate's turning point: under Side A's own payroll-ratio metric, a desperate firm automating to avoid bankruptcy gets penalized while a cash-rich giant that replaces warehouse workers but grows its engineering headcount escapes entirely. Side A never returns to this example to neutralize it; its A1 answer addresses reclassification evasion but leaves the structural inequity of the metric itself live. Side B's fiscal-trap argument—if the tax succeeds revenue dries up, if it fails displacement outruns retraining—also survived the exchange. Side A's response (revenue "naturally scales" during peak transition decades) is plausible but not fully worked out, and B's counter (the policy is self-defeating by design) remained partially standing. Side A preserves a real live route through the funding gap: the general safety net's revenue base erodes with automation, and Side B's "productivity expands the overall tax base" answer is historically plausible but sidesteps the acute transitional timing problem Side A kept pressing. That keeps the margin competitive rather than dominant. Ultimately, Side B made the debate easier to decide by offering concrete, memorable illustrations of how the PRO's own mechanism would misfire, while Side A's responses, though structurally sound, relied more on "existing law handles this" assurances that didn't fully meet the examples on the table.
