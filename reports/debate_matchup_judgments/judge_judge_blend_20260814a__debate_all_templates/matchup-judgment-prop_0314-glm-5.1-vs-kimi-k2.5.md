### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): PRO won the central clash of this debate by consistently exploiting the relocation examples CON introduced. CON's case rested almost entirely on the claim that enforceable conditions drive teams away, illustrated by St. Louis, San Diego, and Oakland. PRO's decisive counter—those cities gave unconditional subsidies and still lost their franchises—was introduced in Rebuttal 1 and was never squarely neutralized. CON's answer, that those cities "demanded rigid terms while Las Vegas and Los Angeles offered flexible partnerships," is historically dubious and contradicts CON's own narrative that those cities capitulated repeatedly. This made the CON pivot look like a retreat, not a rebuttal. PRO also landed a clean structural point with the "OR" in the proposition: teams could satisfy the mandate via revenue sharing alone, mooting much of CON's objection to unquantifiable civic-goods requirements. CON's most interesting moment was distinguishing league-wide revenue sharing (multilateral) from municipal revenue sharing (bilateral, zero-sum), but PRO minimized this by noting the relocated teams moved for market size and larger unconditional subsidies, not specifically to escape conditions they were never asked to accept. CON's "razor-thin stadium margins cannot absorb CBA costs unlike mixed-use developments" was a narrowly useful distinction in Rebuttal 2, but it came too late and was not weighed against PRO's alternative investments argument. PRO's closing was clipped, which is a real execution cost, but enough of the argument was visible and the crystallization point was already well established. CON's closing was competent but largely restated earlier points without sharpening the comparison. The live route to a PRO victory—conditional subsidies guarantee public returns while unconditional subsidies guarantee neither returns nor retention—remained standing at the end of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 turn on the relocation examples was the most decisive exchange. CON introduced St. Louis, San Diego, and Oakland as proof that enforceable conditions drive teams away. PRO flipped this: those cities offered unconditional subsidies for years and still lost their franchises. This established that the choice is not between conditions-and-departure vs. no-conditions-and-retention, but between conditions-with-possible-retention vs. unconditional-giveaways-with-likely-departure-anyway. CON never recovered from this reversal, and its repeated "rigid terms" counter never made contact with the historical record PRO established.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): PRO systematically dismantled CON's central threat—that enforcing conditions causes teams to relocate—by demonstrating that unconditional subsidies fail to retain teams anyway when larger markets beckon. CON had strong points on stadium economics, but PRO won the overarching comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3 deftly explained that teams relocated to LA and Vegas due to market size and larger subsidies rather than an aversion to revenue sharing, neutralizing CON's primary historical examples.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by establishing that unconditional subsidies fail to retain teams, directly turning CON's St. Louis/Oakland examples into evidence that the status quo yields the very losses CON fears. B effectively answers pressure on Green Bay/non-stadium parallels and revenue-sharing feasibility, while A cycles on the poison-pill framing without neutralizing why conditions remain viable when unconditional deals already collapse. The decisive comparative advantage is B's demonstration that the choice is between conditional returns or repeated giveaways ending in relocation anyway.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 turns relocation history against A by noting receiving markets' leverage still avoided enforceable terms only after bigger subsidies, while exposing that unconditional deals in St. Louis produced departure regardless.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A won by consistently developing and defending a coherent collective-action theory: mandatory floors prevent the race to the bottom by removing the credible escape route owners use to shop for the weakest deal. This frame was introduced in the opening, sharpened in the first rebuttal (cartel structure, limited league slots), and never abandoned. The answer to CON's sharpest pressure question (B3.1 — "if cities can enforce contracts, why mandate anything?") was the debate's clearest piece of reasoning: A4.1 cleanly distinguished technical capacity from political capacity, explaining that current failures stem from asymmetric owner leverage and manufactured urgency, not drafting incompetence. That distinction neutralized CON's dilemma without conceding either horn. CON's best counter — that binding all cities collapses leverage because owners just self-finance or find different jurisdictions — was raised late (B5) and was partly speculative; it also implicitly conceded PRO's framing by engaging on PRO's terms. CON's procedural-reform alternative (referenda, transparency windows) was never made convincing as a complete solution: it fixes process but leaves the substantive asymmetry intact, and a fair-process referendum held under relocation threat still produces a gun-to-the-head choice. CON never closed this gap. Execution costs weighed against both sides roughly equally — A referenced nonexistent reference IDs [C1.5-C1.6] in R1, while B had a clipped R1 (format note), a Chinese-character artifact, and a less crisp answer to A's Q2 (the weak-leverage scenario). On balance, PRO's cleaner framing, stronger pressure-question response, and more durable collective-action logic produced the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1's answer to B3.1 was the most decisive exchange. CON's dilemma—"if cities enforce contracts well, mandatory conditions are redundant; if they don't, mandated terms will be equally unenforceable"—seemed sharp. PRO collapsed it by distinguishing technical capacity (cities can draft enforceable contracts) from political capacity (individual cities cannot resist relocation threats without a binding collective floor). This directly answered the live burden, advanced PRO's core theory, and left CON without a meaningful response in the remaining speeches.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A won by cleanly resolving the core clash over leverage and the collective action problem. Side A successfully argued that the proposition creates a united front that removes the 'gun to the head' relocation threat. Side B's attempt to frame 'self-financing' as a loss for cities backfired, as Side A correctly pointed out that an owner paying for their own stadium without public subsidies is a victory for the taxpayer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 7 perfectly dismantled Side B's enforceability dilemma by distinguishing between cities' technical capacity to write contracts and their political capacity to resist extortion.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): B wins by establishing the cartel-driven race to the bottom and power imbalance that makes procedural reform inadequate, with stronger pressure responses showing mandates create real leverage rather than guaranteed losses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers to A's questions tie enforcement capacity to asymmetric threats, directly neutralizing CON's democracy and flexibility claims.
