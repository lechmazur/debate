### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won on the strength of the decisive logical turn in Rebuttal 2: CON's case required restrictions to simultaneously fail strategically (permeable learning curve via espionage and mobilization) and devastate the allied economy (severe commercial damage). PRO correctly identified that these positions are in tension—if the learning curve is genuinely permeable, adversaries were never meaningfully dependent on allied equipment, making the commercial loss trivial; if the curve is genuinely hard, restrictions work and the strategic delay is real. CON never resolved this contradiction in Rebuttal 2 or the closing. PRO also turned CON's own pressure question (Q2: why not fund R&D directly?) into a concession that the commercial cost is absorbable, and then in the closing showed that CON's "learning loop" escape attempt required adversary fabs to be dependent on allied equipment—contradicting the claim that adversaries can easily route around controls. CON's opening mobilization argument had genuine merit and the tacit-knowledge-erodes-inside-the-coalition angle had real force, but neither was developed enough to neutralize PRO's logical trap, and CON's noticeably shorter closing left the internal contradiction standing. PRO's Q&A formatting was clean, answers were direct, and the "decide for the side that doesn't need it both ways" framing was memorable and substantively earned.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 identifying the internal contradiction at the heart of CON's case: CON simultaneously argued that the learning curve is permeable enough (espionage, joint ventures) that restrictions fail strategically, and severe enough economically that allied innovation suffers. PRO showed these claims are mutually undermining—a permeable barrier means adversaries weren't dependent on the equipment, making commercial loss trivial; a genuine barrier means restrictions work. CON never answered this in Rebuttal 2 or the closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO built a more durable case around the irreplaceability of tacit knowledge in semiconductor manufacturing, consistently pressing the asymmetry between bounded commercial costs and unbounded strategic risks. CON’s backfire thesis relied on a less mechanistically grounded claim that political mobilization could compress decades of engineering iteration, and its commercial-damage argument was materially weakened when PRO turned CON’s own Pressure Question about government offsets into a concession that the cost was absorbable. PRO’s closing crystallized a sharp contradiction—CON needed adversaries to be both dependent enough that losing their sales cripples allied innovation and independent enough to catch up quickly—which CON never resolved cleanly. By the end of the debate, PRO had the clearer live route to victory: the chokepoint is genuine, the delay is real, and the cost is manageable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s answer to CON’s Pressure Question Q2 (A3.2), where PRO demonstrated that if allied governments can offset lost revenue and fund R&D directly, then the commercial cost is by definition absorbable—turning what CON intended as a trap into a concession that undermined CON’s economic-damage narrative and fed directly into PRO’s contradiction framing.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by constructing and sustaining a decisive logical dilemma that Side A (CON) never fully escapes. PRO effectively argued that CON's case relies on a contradiction: claiming restrictions will both fail to stop adversaries (due to rapid indigenous mobilization) and simultaneously cripple allied innovation (due to lost commercial feedback). PRO's rebuttals consistently forced CON into this trap, noting that if the adversary can easily bypass controls, the commercial loss is trivial; if the bottleneck is genuine, restrictions buy vital time. CON's "learning loop" argument was their strongest point, but PRO neutralized it by showing mutual dependency actually proves the chokepoint's leverage. PRO also offered cleaner weighing throughout, framing commercial costs as bounded and recoverable versus strategic risks as unbounded and irreversible. CON was competent and grounded but grew repetitive and failed to resolve the core tension PRO pressed in the late rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which directly answers CON's pressure questions and flips Q2 into a concession that government offsets can absorb commercial costs. B then crystallizes the round's central clash by exposing CON's internal contradiction—needing restrictions to be simultaneously militarily useless and economically devastating—and shows why both cannot be true. This exchange decisively narrowed the debate and gave PRO a clear path to victory.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins on the strength of three arguments that remain live throughout the debate. First, the mature-nodes point [A2.3]: military AI for targeting, logistics, and autonomy runs effectively on older nodes adversaries already produce; restricting sub-5nm delays the apex of capability, not the operational threat. PRO never adequately rebutts this—its only response is that adversaries' documented focus on frontier nodes proves military dependency, but CON immediately turns that by noting states pursue frontier chips for commercial dominance and prestige across the full spectrum, not exclusively for deployed military AI. Second, CON's A2 answer to PRO's Q2 is the single best exchange in the debate: CON concedes that autarky produces an inefficient subsidized industry and then pivots to note this was already the adversary's chosen model, while restrictions force that exact cost onto the West's previously self-reinforcing commercial R&D cycle. PRO's rebuttal that "allied demand funds core R&D while subsidies cover only the narrow security gap" is structurally unconvincing—it does not explain away why needing subsidies signals the loss of the competitive discipline CON identifies. Third, CON's reframe—"you convert a durable asymmetric lead into a symmetric race between two subsidized industries, one already prepared for this contingency"—is sharper than PRO's "both-sides-later" framing and goes unanswered. PRO's strongest moment is the "installed base" closing point, which is a genuine response, but it arrives late and is underexplored. PRO also consistently underused its word budget, leaving key burdens underdeveloped throughout. CON's closing was clipped, an execution cost, but the core arguments were already well established. The decisive comparative work—CON turning PRO's own Q2 into a win—tilts this clearly to Side A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's Q2 [A4.2]: PRO asked what prevents the adversary's state-backed autarkic program from facing the same reinvestment shortfalls. CON conceded the point entirely and then turned it: "restrictions do not impose a new cost on the adversary; they impose a devastating new cost on the West, which had a functioning, globally competitive, self-reinforcing R&D cycle." The adversary was voluntarily running the subsidized model already; restrictions force the West to abandon the commercial model that actually sustained its lead. PRO's answer in the subsequent rebuttal ("allied demand funds core R&D; subsidies are a narrow targeted fix") never resolves this structural asymmetry, leaving CON's framing live into the closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON built the stronger path by disconnecting the proposed restriction from the claimed military benefit: it showed that most adversarial military AI applications run effectively on mature nodes, so blocking sub-5nm tools does not materially slow the threat, while simultaneously demonstrating that the policy would dismantle the Western commercial R&D cycle that sustains the real technological lead. PRO never repaired the mature-node gap or recovered from CON's comparative turn on subsidies, leaving its central causal story unresolved. CON's clipped closing is an execution cost that narrows the margin but does not erase the advantage built across the rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4), where it answered PRO's Pressure Questions by decoupling adversary pursuit of sub-5nm from military necessity (prestige, signaling, commercial AI leadership) and turned PRO's subsidy concession into a structural disadvantage for the West, crystallizing the comparison that restrictions force the West into the same inefficient state-backed model its adversary already tolerates.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON wins by successfully dismantling PRO's core premises: that sub-5nm chips are strictly necessary for military AI, and that export controls preserve a durable technological gap. CON's argument that mature nodes already enable most military applications, combined with the reframing that restrictions force the West to abandon its scale-driven R&D advantage for a subsidized model the adversary already tolerates, created a decisive comparative edge. PRO's "ordering advantage" framing was clean but rested on a static bottleneck assumption CON effectively neutralized. CON's sharper weighing and direct answers to pressure questions secured the win, despite a minor execution cost for a clipped closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 exchange, where it answered PRO's pressure questions by decoupling military AI needs from frontier nodes and turning PRO's subsidy concession into a structural liability. The argument that restrictions force the West to abandon its competitive advantage to drag the adversary into a race it was already running crystallized the industrial base clash and permanently shifted the weighing against PRO's timeline argument.
