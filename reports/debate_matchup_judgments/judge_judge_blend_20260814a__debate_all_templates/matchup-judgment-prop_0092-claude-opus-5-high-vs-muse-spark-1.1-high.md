- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate. Side B compellingly minimized pavement damage and showed that daylighting, tickets, and hourly pricing address particular harms. But it ultimately conceded that larger vehicles require fewer, larger stalls while proposing to spread that lost capacity across all users. Side A better explained why duration pricing and size pricing can coexist, why fees can influence future vehicle choice, and why those imposing the footprint cost should bear more of it. A's clipped first rebuttal was an execution cost, but not enough to erase its stronger comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's decisive response was that repainting for fewer, larger stalls concedes a real footprint cost but sends the bill to compact-car owners. Side B's focus on curb duration remained useful, yet it did not answer why time should be priced while differential space consumption should not.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully maneuvering Side B into a damaging concession on street space. Side A's strategic concessions (dropping pavement damage and the commercial carve-out) allowed them to laser-focus the debate on the indisputable physical facts of footprint and geometry. Side B's argument that larger cars just require repainting stalls to reflect 'reality' was brilliantly weaponized by Side A in the closing to prove that compact owners would subsidize the space larger vehicles consume.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing effectively weaponized Side B's answer on repainting stalls, pointing out that shrinking 200 stalls to 180 means the compact owner pays the physical price for a larger fleet.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively exposing the contradiction in Side B's alternative. When Side B suggested repainting stalls larger and reducing the total number of spaces to accommodate larger vehicles, Side A correctly pointed out that this merely socializes the physical cost of oversized vehicles onto compact car owners, violating Side B's own principle of making the user pay for the cost they impose. Side A also landed a critical, grounded blow by noting that Side B's "price the hour" solution relies on meters, which do not exist in residential permit zones where the bulk of overnight curb storage occurs. Side A's closing crystallized this clash perfectly, securing a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side B's "price the hour" argument by pointing out that most on-street parking is residential permit parking without meters, rendering Side B's primary alternative instrument nonexistent in the exact contexts where the problem is worst.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate because B established that size fees are imperfect proxies for exposure, but not that charging nothing extra is preferable when larger vehicles impose greater curb, sightline, and safety costs. A directly answered the pressure questions with purchase, trip, and fleet-turnover mechanisms, plus compensatory revenue and work-vehicle exemptions. B's speed, daylighting, and trip-pricing alternatives were compatible with rather than decisive against the proposition, while B's own per-foot pricing suggestion substantially conceded A's street-use principle. B was rhetorically sharper, but its categorical claims about buyer behavior and regressivity were insufficiently grounded, and its clipped closing imposed a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to the “parked truck never killed anyone” frame was decisive: registration can price annual access risk, while oversized vehicles also impose costs when parked through curb consumption and blocked sightlines. B's mileage objection exposed imprecision, but did not neutralize those independent costs or show that imperfect corrective pricing is worse than flat pricing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won a highly competitive debate by successfully defending the core principle of internalizing external costs. Side B brilliantly argued that a flat fee won't deter affluent buyers and thus acts as a regressive "license for danger," but Side A effectively countered that if the behavior doesn't change, the fee fairly forces the oversized vehicle owner—rather than the general public—to fund the necessary safety mitigations like daylighting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument in the closing that Side B's alternatives (speed limits and daylighting) "make everyone pay," whereas a size-based fee makes the vehicle that forces those mitigations pay, perfectly neutralized Side B's push for alternative tools.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins solidly by dismantling the causal link between the proposed fees and the safety harms they purport to address. B effectively argued that parking and registration fees tax storage rather than the moving vehicle that causes pedestrian harm, and exposed the perverse incentives (more circling, short-but-tall SUVs). The decisive blow came in the closing, where Side A attempted to spin the affluent buyer's lack of behavioral change as a positive ("funds the daylighting"). Side B brilliantly pounced on this concession, framing it as "licensing danger" and proving that the policy fails its own safety premise while remaining regressive. Side A's case was competently built on city-level tool constraints, but B's logical traps and rhetorical sharpness secured a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing response to Side A's concession that the $70k truck buyer won't change behavior but will "fund daylighting." B devastatingly reframed this as licensing the exact danger PRO spent the debate describing, collapsing PRO's safety justification.
