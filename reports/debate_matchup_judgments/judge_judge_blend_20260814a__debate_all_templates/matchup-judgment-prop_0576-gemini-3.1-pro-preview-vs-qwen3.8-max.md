### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but A more effectively resolved the central clash: whether a workable line exists between moral witness and partisan electioneering. A's proposed test (explicit endorsement, coordination, partisan expenditure) proved more administrable than B credited, and A's closing landed a decisive tu quoque: B's own preferred remedy (auditing diverted funds, staff hours, mailing-list use to police a "nondeductible political fund") requires at least as much granular state scrutiny of religious operations as A's institutional test, undercutting B's core entanglement objection. B never squarely answered this turn in the final speech, instead reasserting that narrower remedies are "less entangling" without explaining why. B's chilling-effect and disproportionality arguments were principled and rhetorically strong, and its walk-back on the food-pantry example under PQ pressure was handled reasonably, but the debate's decisive axis—whether B's alternative actually avoids the entanglement it condemns—tilted to A by the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing turn (A5.2), arguing that B's targeted-accounting alternative (auditing staff hours, mailing-list use, diverted funds) requires the same or greater state entanglement with religious operations that B spent the debate condemning—left largely unanswered in B's final speech.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO wins because it successfully defended the civic bargain framing and demonstrated that CON's targeted-accounting alternative is both more invasive and ineffective due to fungibility. While CON landed real pressure on the subjectivity of "primary purpose" enforcement, PRO's secular-nonprofit parallel and operational-output test provided enough cover to keep its core route alive. PRO's closing turn—showing that CON's own remedy demands deeper state entanglement—crystallized the decisive comparative advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 argument that tax-deductible general funds and religious infrastructure are fungible (A4.2), which materially undermined CON's claim that targeted expenditure taxes could solve the dark-money problem without stripping institutional status.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): PRO leveraged the resolution's own qualifier "rather than as primarily charitable" to blunt CON's isolated-bulletin / moral-witness slippery slope, and kept a live route on subsidy and dark-money fungibility. CON's strong enforcement-discretion and disproportionality points were undercut when PRO showed CON's less-restrictive alternative (allocate overhead, audit staff hours, track mailing lists) demands more invasive entanglement than a primary-purpose financial/behavioral test already used for secular nonprofits. That comparative turn made PRO's bargain argument the cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B5.2 / B4.2 fungibility + entanglement reversal: arguing CON's targeted-accounting cure requires meticulously auditing pastoral hours and mixed expenses, which is more entangling than PRO's primary-activity test.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and sustained real clash on two central questions: (1) whether tax exemption is best understood as a conditional subsidy or a constitutional non-interference shield, and (2) whether a "primarily partisan vs. primarily spiritual/charitable" test is administrable without subjective doctrinal auditing. CON's pressure questions were sharp, correctly isolating a real tension in PRO's framework — PRO's "objective markers" language sits uneasily with the motion's own "rather than primarily" phrasing, and CON exploited this well, reinforced by the Civil Rights Movement example, which PRO never fully defused beyond an assertion that those churches "remained primarily" spiritual. That assertion, repeated rather than proven, was a genuine soft spot CON kept pressing effectively into the final round. However, PRO's case tracked the motion's own language more naturally (tax "advantages" as something that can be "lost" implies a conditional-benefit frame), offered a more concrete limiting principle (endorsements, party transfers, coordinated ads — analogous to real distinctions in tax and campaign law) that gave the judge something administrable to weigh, and exposed a costly concession from CON: biting the bullet that an overt partisan campaign committee should receive identical tax treatment to a food pantry. That concession, while consistent with CON's shield theory, reads as an extreme and less persuasive outcome to a neutral reader, and PRO's closing implicitly leaned on this asymmetry. On balance, PRO's fairness/compelled-subsidy argument and closer alignment with the resolution's own logic edge out CON's strong but ultimately unresolved administrability warning, yielding a competitive but clear win for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Questions (B3.1/B3.2) forcing PRO to reconcile "objective markers" with the motion's "primarily" language and the Civil Rights Movement counterexample was the most damaging exchange; PRO's answer (A4.1-A4.3) narrowed but did not fully close that gap, while CON's concession in A1/B4.1 that partisan committees and food pantries deserve identical treatment was the costliest bitten bullet of the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A (PRO) won by establishing that tax exemption plus deductible donations constitute real fiscal support, not mere government abstention, and by defending a functional standard based on objective public conduct. Side B (CON) mounted strong pressure with the Civil Rights example and viewpoint-discrimination fears, but critically conceded in Rebuttal 2 that even a church functioning as a partisan campaign committee should retain tax protection, undermining its claim that the status quo avoids subsidizing party politics. PRO successfully showed that removing tax advantages preserves speech while ending compelled taxpayer subsidy, and that overt partisan markers (endorsements, party transfers, coordinated ads) can identify partisan function without sermon audits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where PRO answered CON's pressure questions by distinguishing institutional function from theological auditing, clarifying that objective structural markers determine when an organization is primarily partisan, and reframing the Civil Rights churches as spiritually primary despite political engagement.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO kept the core fairness frame live: tax exemption + deductible donations are fiscal advantages, and compelling all taxpayers to underwrite an overt partisan machine is unjustified. CON's shield/entangle argument was strong but forced CON to bite the bullet that a church operating as a party committee deserves the same advantage as a food pantry [B3.1/A4.1], which hurts with a third-party reader. PRO's distinction between moral/issue advocacy and candidate-electioneering with objective markers like endorsements, party transfers, coordinated ads [B2.4/B4.1] sufficiently answered the audit-of-sermons fear, leaving CON's viewpoint-discrimination worry partially mitigated but not decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's R1/R2 move that issue advocacy ≠ candidate electioneering and that objective public conduct (endorsements, transfers, coordinated ads) can prove primary partisan function without theological auditing - directly blunting CON's entanglement and civil-rights-church attack.
