### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO wins on the decisive structural clash: CON's "almost never" answer effectively abolishes the motion's own threshold rather than arguing for a higher one. PRO correctly identified this as dogma rather than economics, and that charge was never adequately neutralized. CON's bounded-vs.-unbounded framework was intellectually interesting but carried a fatal internal problem — it makes the motion's conditional ("when fatigue and disruption outweigh…") structurally unreachable by design, which a neutral reader can see as question-begging rather than persuasive. PRO's counter that harms also compound — degraded product undermines the very global spectacle CON is trying to export — was present from Rebuttal 1 and was never squarely answered by CON, who kept repeating "engineering will fix the friction" without engaging the quality-degradation loop. PRO's pressure question Q1 was the sharpest single move: forcing CON to name what harm would ever satisfy their standard exposed that CON's position admits no threshold at all. CON landed genuine points — modern recovery infrastructure is real, preseason tours underperform commercially, franchise-value-as-compensation has teeth — but these were point-scoring rebuttals rather than a coherent alternative path to the motion's defeat. CON's strongest structural argument required denying that any bounded cost can outweigh an unbounded gain, which is a claim that makes the motion's conditional language meaningless and which PRO exploited effectively across multiple rounds. Both sides had speeches clipped, roughly canceling that execution cost. Margin is clear but competitive because CON's framework was distinctive and its engineering-over-retreat case was coherent within its own logic, even if it ultimately lost the clash on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure question Q1 — asking what observable harm would ever satisfy CON's standard — was the most decisive exchange. CON's answer in Rebuttal 2 essentially confirmed that the threshold is "structurally unreachable" because bounded costs cannot outweigh unbounded gains. PRO then labeled this correctly as dogma rather than judgment, and that characterization stuck because CON never offered a concrete scenario in which it would recommend scaling back. This exchange crystallized the core asymmetry in CON's position and gave PRO a live route to victory that held through the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate tied to the motion’s conditional threshold and exposing Side A’s answer as too absolutist. Side A’s global-compounding and engineering arguments were polished, but its claim that bounded fatigue and disruption can almost never outweigh unbounded brand growth made the threshold effectively meaningless. Side B more convincingly argued that harms to athlete health, product quality, and local fan loyalty can also compound, and that scaling back regular-season travel is a prudent case-by-case response rather than isolationism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s pressure question asking what harm could ever satisfy Side A’s standard was decisive; Side A answered by making the threshold “structurally unreachable,” which let Side B frame CON as treating global expansion as an unweighable absolute.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B won by establishing that the motion's threshold compares bounded, engineerable short-term costs against unbounded, compounding long-term global gains, making retreat structurally favored and economically irrational. A's case remained plausible but never neutralized B's core arithmetic or demonstrated that harms compound faster than the growth they threaten.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 reframing of athlete fatigue as bounded and shrinking while brand expansion is unbounded and compounding, which undermined the entire weighing mechanism A needed to win.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built a tighter, more consistent arc around a single controlling insight: when leagues compensate displaced ticket-holders, they have already adjudged the disruption material enough to warrant restitution—making "the check is the confession" both an effective reframe of CON's best argument and a thread that ran coherently from Rebuttal 1 through the closing. A's answer to the composite-metric pressure question (A1 in Rebuttal 2) was adequate: leagues routinely make incommensurable trade-offs in load management and broadcast decisions without a unified algorithm, so demanding one to operationalize the motion's conditional is a rhetorical trap, not a substantive objection. The NFL's curated international slate as proof-of-concept—already the discipline the motion prescribes—was an effective, concrete anchor. CON's strongest surviving argument was the revealed-preference point (leagues keep doing it, fans don't leave, therefore the outweigh hasn't occurred), which is a real live route to victory that A never fully neutralized. However, CON suffered three clipped speeches (Opening, Rebuttal 1, Closing), leaving incomplete argumentative chains and creating genuine execution costs. CON's mechanistic rebuttal to fatigue—"players know the stakes" and "sports science manages it"—was weaker than A's circadian-disruption story. CON's framing of compensation as evidence of managed success rather than admitted harm was clever but could not fully displace A's version because a refund proves someone was denied what they paid for, regardless of how smoothly the denial was handled. The margin is real but competitive because CON's conditional-burden attack (PRO never demonstrated the outweigh actually occurs at scale) remained partially live at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's "check as confession" reframe in Rebuttal 1 and the Closing. When CON argued that compensation proves the system works, PRO flipped it: leagues pay ticket-holders back precisely because they have judged the disruption material enough to warrant restitution—you don't refund trivial inconvenience. CON's response (compensation keeps the deal net-positive) was competent, but PRO's version left the stronger residue because it grounded the harm concretely in what the buyer was actually sold and denied, making it the most rhetorically and argumentatively decisive exchange in the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): B won by keeping the debate tied to the motion’s conditional tradeoff: when fatigue and local disruption outweigh expansion, scaling back is disciplined scheduling, not retreat. A’s best push was that the threshold is vague and the benefits are large, but B answered that leagues already make incommensurable judgments without a single algorithm. A also suffered from clipped speeches and leaned on late, unsupported claims about renewals and market verdicts. B’s harms and weighing were cleaner and more consistently responsive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to the “phantom threshold” attack was decisive: it showed that the absence of one composite metric does not make weighing impossible, while turning A’s compensation point into evidence that local disruption is real.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by proving the conditional threshold is manageable and already practiced—as shown by the NFL's curated international slate—and by reframing compensation as evidence of material harm rather than efficient management. Side B's threshold critique demanded impossible precision, and multiple clipped speeches left key burdens underdeveloped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's reframing of refunds as "the check is the confession," proving material disruption exists and undermining CON's claim that the trade-off is already net positive.
