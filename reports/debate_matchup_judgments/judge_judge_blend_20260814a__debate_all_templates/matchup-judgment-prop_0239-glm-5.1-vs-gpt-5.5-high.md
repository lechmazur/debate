### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): PRO built and maintained two live routes to victory that CON never fully neutralized. First, the tautology charge: CON's move of packing competence, information, voluntariness, and authority over affected rights into the definition of "consent" means "consent alone" is either tautological or question-begging. CON's response—that those conditions are intrinsic to consent, not additions—is defensible philosophically but does not escape the trap, because the proposition asks whether bare voluntary agreement suffices and CON effectively conceded it does not. Second, the pollution example: PRO correctly identified that if CON explains the harm to downstream residents by saying "A and B lacked authority to consent to C's interests," then C's non-consent is functioning as an independent moral consideration beyond A-B's agreement—which is precisely PRO's thesis. CON's reformulation ("consent absent, not insufficient") relabels the same observation without changing the underlying logic. On insulin, CON's only exit was calling exploitative pricing "ugly but not wrong," which concedes the intuitive gap PRO was pointing at without answering it. CON's strongest material—the anti-paternalism frame and the hardship-versus-coercion distinction—was coherent and sustained throughout, and genuinely narrowed the debate. But CON never produced a principled account of why price gouging during genuine emergencies is merely unattractive rather than wrong, leaving that as a live example for PRO. PRO's closing crystallized the choice cleanly: "pack everything into consent and lose explanatory power, or admit something beyond consent matters." CON's closing answered with structural consistency but did not crack the tautology or the pollution dilemma. PRO wins clearly but not dominantly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 Answer A2 was the most decisive exchange. PRO turned CON's own pollution answer against it: when CON explains the downstream harm by saying A and B "lacked authority to consent away C's rights," CON is acknowledging that an independent moral consideration—third-party non-consent—is required to evaluate whether the transaction is ethical. That is exactly PRO's proposition. CON's reformulation ("consent was absent, not insufficient") is a relabeling, not a refutation, and PRO exposed this clearly, creating a clean comparative advantage on the most contested example in the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that Side B's definition of consent absorbs independent moral conditions—authority, competence, disclosure—making the motion trivial, while still failing to explain hard cases like price-gouging and unconscionable contracts where all those conditions are met. Side A's pollution and payday-loan examples created a live route to victory that Side B could only answer by calling the results merely "ugly" or retreating into definitional stipulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4.1-A4.5), where Side A showed that even granting Side B's expanded definition of consent, fully consensual corporate pollution and price-gouging remain unethical, proving that independent moral criteria beyond consent must enter the calculus.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully defending a robust definition of valid consent that intrinsically includes competence, information, voluntariness, and authority over affected rights. This neutralized Side B’s core semantic attack that CON was smuggling in “extra” conditions. Side A also effectively turned the third-party harm example by showing it proves absent consent rather than insufficient consent, and consistently exposed Side B’s alternative standard (“gross disproportion,” “structural exploitation”) as unprincipled paternalism that overrides the chooser’s own valuation. Side B relied heavily on moral intuitions about price gouging and payday loans but failed to provide a clear, non-arbitrary threshold to separate exploitation from hard bargains, leaving Side A’s autonomy-based framework as the cleaner, more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to the pollution and insulin examples, clarifying that disposing of a third party’s rights voids consent due to lack of authority (not insufficiency), and distinguishing tragic scarcity from transactional wrongdoing, which dismantled Side B’s hardest cases and exposed the paternalism in PRO’s standard.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): PRO built and sustained the cleaner path to victory across the full exchange. The debate's decisive clash was whether CON's "constitutive conditions" move was genuine conceptual analysis or relabeling. PRO exposed the circularity repeatedly and effectively: every time CON needed to exclude a morally uncomfortable case (exploitation, self-enslavement, death-for-amusement), CON retroactively redefined "genuine consent" to exclude it. PRO named this problem as early as R1 ("smuggled in") and kept the pressure on. CON never escaped it. The death-for-amusement example in R2 was well-constructed: a competent adult, fully informed, no third parties harmed — and CON's only response was that you "cannot competently will the elimination of your will," which PRO correctly identified as defining competence by the substantive impermissibility of the deal. That is precisely PRO's thesis. The exploitation/voluntariness distinction in A4.3 was also live and unanswered: a worker can voluntarily accept a dangerous underpaid job without any deception or coercion, yet the employer may still be acting exploitatively. CON's B4.3 response addressed price-gouging but not this cleaner case. The democracy analogy, which CON used as a shield, was correctly turned in A5.4: if "consent" by definition includes all the contested ethical conditions, then "consent alone" isn't doing the work — the package label doesn't change what is inside. CON's position had internal consistency but relied on an ever-expanding definition of consent that, as PRO showed, reduces to "an agreement that already passes every ethical test," which is not a defense of consent's sufficiency but a concession by vocabulary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.3 in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange. CON had persistently argued that exploitation reduces to a voluntariness failure. PRO drew a clean conceptual line: voluntariness asks whether the choice is authored by the person rather than produced by force, fraud, or incapacity; exploitation asks whether the other party takes unfair advantage of vulnerability or unequal leverage. The worker who voluntarily accepts a dangerous, underpaid job because starvation is the alternative is making a genuinely authored choice — and CON's own admission that serious need doesn't void voluntariness (to preserve medical and employment consent) meant CON could not then claim exploitation always collapses into non-voluntariness. This gap was never closed and left PRO's core claim intact.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A won by exposing that Side B's defense relies on redefining "consent" to include every independent moral condition—fairness, non-exploitation, third-party rights, and substantive limits on commodification—making CON's sufficiency claim tautological. A's death-for-amusement counterexample and the clear conceptual distinction between voluntariness and exploitation provided concrete pressure that B could only absorb by importing substantive ethical judgments into the definition of competence. B's framework remained internally coherent but never overcame the charge that, on their account, "consent" functions as a package name for ethics rather than a standalone sufficient condition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal that CON's "genuine consent" smuggles in independent ethical criteria, turning a debate about sufficiency into a trivial definitional victory—crystallized by the "concession by vocabulary" line and reinforced by the death-for-amusement counterexample that forced B to define competence by the substantive morality of the deal.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully identifying and dismantling Side A's core strategy: inflating the definition of "consent" to absorb all independent moral conditions (fairness, non-exploitation, dignity, third-party rights). Side B consistently argued that this is semantic smuggling rather than a substantive defense, noting that if "consent" is defined as "already ethical agreement," the sufficiency claim becomes a tautology that quietly concedes the proposition. Side B's distinction between voluntariness and exploitation was particularly strong, showing that constrained choices can remain voluntary while still being ethically problematic due to unfair leverage. Side B also effectively turned Side A's democracy analogy in the closing to demonstrate that packing external requirements into a concept proves those external requirements are doing the real moral work. Side A executed a coherent philosophical defense but ultimately could not escape the charge that it was merely relabeling external ethical judgments as internal consent conditions, leaving Side B with the cleaner, more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 sharply distinguished voluntariness from exploitation, explaining that a choice can be fully voluntary (authored by the agent under pressure) yet still be exploitative because the counterparty takes unfair advantage of vulnerability. This directly broke Side A's attempt to collapse all ethical failures into "absent consent," preserving a clear independent ethical criterion beyond consent and forcing Side A into post-hoc definitional defenses.
