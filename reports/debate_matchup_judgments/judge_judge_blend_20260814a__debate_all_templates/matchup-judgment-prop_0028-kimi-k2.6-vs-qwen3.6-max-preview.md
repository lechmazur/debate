### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built and sustained a cleaner path to victory across the full debate. Its core examples—receptionists, warehouse supervisors, police recruits facing degree requirements for duties that did not become meaningfully more complex—were concrete, sticky, and repeatedly tested without being neutralized. CON's counter that work genuinely became more complex was plausible in broad strokes, but PRO's single most decisive argument arrived in Rebuttal 2: if the skill floor truly rose to bachelor's level, wages in those roles should have risen proportionally to reflect that investment—yet they did not. CON never answered this directly or satisfactorily. PRO also landed the contaminated-baseline argument effectively: CON's absolute-gains metric compared graduates to a population that policy itself excluded from previously accessible roles, making the comparison circular rather than probative. CON's strongest material—persistent graduate lifetime earnings, first-generation access, workforce resilience—was genuine but was hampered by repeatedly relying on the same framing without meaningfully dismantling PRO's best responses. CON admitted credential inflation exists and called it "marginal," which PRO correctly flagged as a pivotal concession given the scope of the examples on the table. The tollgate metaphor and "distributing access to a tollgate is not building new roads" framing in PRO's closing crystallized the key clash more memorably than CON's closing, which recycled the absolute-gains argument without sharpening it. The debate was genuinely competitive—CON was competent and never collapsed—but PRO's argumentative progression was tighter, its pressure questions more pointed, and its answers in Rebuttal 2 more directly on the live burdens.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A1 answer): if degree requirements in reception, warehouse, and policing roles reflected a genuine rise in the skill floor, wages in those occupations would have risen to reflect degree-level training. They did not. This logical gap—credential barrier appearing without corresponding wage recognition—was the sharpest single argument in the debate and CON never provided a clean answer to it.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by making the comparative test clearer: university expansion often raised the price of access to existing jobs rather than creating enough genuinely new opportunity. Side A had a plausible human-capital story and pressed absolute graduate gains, but B’s response that those gains are measured against a non-graduate baseline degraded by degree barriers directly undercut A’s main metric. B also more effectively explained why basic digitalization does not justify four-year degree requirements for static work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it answered A’s pressure by distinguishing basic trainable digital skills from degree-level work, then reframed A’s graduate-premium evidence as partly manufactured by excluding non-graduates from jobs they previously could access.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A built the stronger overall case by grounding credential inflation in concrete unchanged-job examples (receptionists, police) and directly answering pressure questions on transitional underemployment and artificial baseline erosion; B's absolute-gains and economic-complexity claims remained more general and were undercut by the distinction between on-the-job basics and degree-level work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 directly neutralized B's digital-skill and absolute-mobility pressure responses by noting quick learnability and policy-degraded non-graduate floor.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but CON (Side A) held three live advantages that PRO never fully neutralized. First, the tautology charge landed and stuck: CON correctly identified that PRO's entire inflation diagnosis rests on treating any new degree requirement as evidence of inflation, with no independent metric for actual task complexity. PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer ("actual task content and training necessity") was too vague to constitute a real methodology and left the circularity unresolved. Second, the persistent college wage premium is CON's strongest empirical anchor, and PRO's reframe—that it is merely a sorting penalty imposed on non-graduates—was handled cleanly by CON: if the premium were purely relative, real graduate wages would stagnate while non-graduate pay simply collapsed; instead, graduates migrated into genuinely higher-productivity sectors, and employers actively pay more for them, which is hard to reconcile with pure screening theory. Third, CON's absolute-vs.-relative mobility distinction held up: first-generation entrants into licensed professions their parents were structurally excluded from represents absolute advancement, even if the relative ceiling has risen. PRO's "tollbooth" framing and debt-compression argument are rhetorically effective and identify real costs, but the magnitude comparison the motion requires—inflation more than opportunity—was never established through anything other than assertion. PRO's static job-market assumption (firms don't create new roles just because graduates arrive) was also repeatedly pressured by CON's human-capital complement argument and never cleanly answered. The decisive margin belongs to CON because PRO's core causal story rests on a methodological gap that CON exposed early and PRO couldn't close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's tautology charge in A3.1—that PRO identifies credential inflation by pointing to new degree requirements and then cites those same requirements as evidence of inflation—was the sharpest moment in the debate. PRO's response in B4.1 claimed to use "actual task content and training necessity" as an independent standard, but offered no specification of how to apply that standard without already presupposing the answer. CON kept this pressure alive through the closing, and it functioned as a genuine methodological indictment of PRO's entire case rather than a narrow point of dispute.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side A won by making the cleaner comparative case that mass university expansion generated real human capital and expanded high-skill opportunity, not just positional sorting. Side B had a plausible credential-inflation story, but leaned heavily on asserted claims about “most” degrees, unchanged work, and debt overwhelming returns without sufficiently grounding the net comparison. A’s persistent wage-premium argument, dynamic economy framing, and first-generation mobility point gave a stronger reason to reject the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange was pressing B for an independent way to distinguish credential inflation from genuine skill upgrading, then tying that to persistent graduate wage premiums and employers’ willingness to pay more for graduates. B’s “premium as penalty on non-graduates” answer was relevant but did not fully neutralize A’s productivity explanation.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): CON wins by more convincingly linking expansion to skill upgrading and broken barriers, showing employer clustering and persistent premiums as evidence of genuine productivity gains rather than pure sorting. PRO's case on devaluation and debt was coherent but undermined by unaddressed pressure on independent complexity metrics and relative framing, leaving inflation less dominant than claimed after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's direct A1/A2 answers in rebuttal 2 on productivity premiums and human-capital complements, which neutralized PRO's task-content metric and tautology charge while sharpening absolute mobility contrast.
