### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B (CON) wins this debate on three interlocking grounds. First, the indirect-channels argument — buses, kerosene, diesel-fertilized food, generator electricity — materially damages Side A's foundational premise that the poor gain "near-zero" from fuel subsidies. Side A never fully absorbed this blow; its closing still treats the "poorest can't afford cars" framing as decisive even after it was shown to be incomplete. Second, Side B's pressure question demanding a real country where indexed transfers survived a fiscal crunch exposed a genuine gap in Side A's case: the A1 answer retreated to design principles rather than evidence, which is a clear execution loss on a central burden. Third, Side B's political-economy argument is better grounded throughout — fuel riots in Nigeria and Ecuador versus no comparable constituency defending cash transfers is specific and unrebutted. Side A's case is theoretically coherent (targeted > universal in principle, 60% beats near-zero), and it lands one genuine hit with the budget-deficit crowding-out point that Side B only partially answers. But Side A never escapes the "working system now versus promised system later" framing, and its closing is notably thin (well under word limit), leaving key burdens undefended. Side B repeats "that is not reform, it is exposure" to the point of diminishing returns, which costs some rhetorical credit, but its overall grounding and adaptation across rounds are stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure question Q1 — "name one low-income country where a statutory indexed transfer survived a fiscal crunch without nominal value collapsing" — is the most decisive exchange. Side A's A1 response conceded it could not name one and fell back on abstract design principles, effectively validating Side B's core claim that the replacement mechanism does not yet exist in the countries where the motion matters most.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the stronger path to victory by grounding the debate in empirical reality and sustaining that pressure through all rounds. PRO’s theoretical case—that targeted cash transfers are more equitable and can be indexed to inflation—was consistently undermined by CON’s concrete examples of transfer programs failing to reach the poor in Nigeria, Indonesia, and India, alongside evidence that cash transfers are politically fragile and often the first cuts in fiscal crunches (Ghana, Kenya, Zambia). PRO relied on policy-design hypotheticals (“statutory indexed entitlements”) but could not name a single low-income country where such a transfer survived a fiscal crunch, evading CON’s direct pressure question. CON also effectively rebutted PRO’s leakage comparison by showing that universal subsidies reach the poor through indirect channels (transport, food, kerosene, electricity), whereas high leakage in targeted programs means the state cannot even locate the households it claims to prioritize. The decisive asymmetry was that CON demonstrated the motion asks governments to dismantle an automatic, working stabilizer in exchange for administrative capacity that has repeatedly failed to materialize on time.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s reframing of the leakage debate in [A4.3], arguing that 40% transfer leakage represents a targeting machine failing to find the poor, while universal subsidies automatically cushion the poor through indirect price effects (buses, food transport, generator fuel) that PRO’s case ignores. This reversed the moral intuition of PRO’s opening and survived the exchange unanswered with concrete examples.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B won by consistently grounding the debate in administrative feasibility, political economy, and indirect cost channels, while Side A relied on theoretical policy designs that never adequately answered the reality gap. B successfully flipped A's "poor don't drive" claim by detailing how fuel subsidies cushion transport, food, and electricity costs, and effectively painted A's "statutory indexed transfers" as institutionally unrealistic for the target countries. B's historical examples of transfer erosion and political fragility created a sustained burden that A could not clear, making B's path to victory cleaner and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2 exchange on indirect costs and political durability. B dismantled A's claim that the poor don't benefit from fuel subsidies by showing how diesel and kerosene price floors protect bus fares, food transport, and generator electricity. B then tied this to the political economy, demonstrating that cash transfers lack the visible, riot-inducing constituency that keeps subsidies intact during fiscal crunches, effectively neutralizing A's theoretical indexing solution.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A (PRO) built and held the decisive path to victory: the distributional arithmetic. The central argument—that subsidy value flows disproportionately to high-income households while targeted transfers invert that logic—was never squarely refuted. CON's cascading-benefits rebuttal concedes the basic mechanism but cannot rescue the arithmetic; at each rung of the cascade (operator margins, fare stickiness, competitive pressure), value is lost, and the bottom quintile receives residue. PRO crystallized this with the "cascade loss" framing that persisted throughout. CON's most damaging line was the exclusion of informal workers from targeting systems, but PRO answered that the comparison is not "perfect targeting vs. universal coverage" but "partial coverage reaching the poor" vs. "universal coverage delivering most value to the rich"—an asymmetry CON never overcame. PRO's Q1 pressure question was the most decisive exchange: it forced CON to admit it could not quantify the pass-through rate, then CON pivoted to a "40% of income on fuel-transported goods" figure that appeared unsupported and reframed rather than answered the question. CON's answer to Q2 was weaker than PRO's answer to CON's matching Q2: PRO pointed to Indonesia 2015 and Nigeria as examples of reform surviving with paired transfers, while CON acknowledged those same cases faced unrest without undermining the core comparison. PRO did absorb execution costs—two speeches clipped for exceeding the word limit—which modestly reduces the margin and explains why this is not a dominant win. Still, the fundamental distributional logic, the cascade analysis, and the superior handling of pressure questions leave PRO with a clear and earned advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to CON's "cascading benefits" argument was the most decisive exchange. PRO accepted the premise of pass-through while showing the arithmetic still favors targeted transfers at each rung: operators capture margins, fares are sticky, and competitive pressure absorbs additional value before reaching the poor. The "residue" framing—that the bottom quintile receives whatever survives each stage of the cascade—was memorable and never refuted. This was sharpened further when PRO's Q1 forced CON to admit it had no pass-through rate to offer, at which point CON reframed to a different metric rather than defending the cascade on its own terms.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner path to victory on the core distributional clash. The residue framing—that universal subsidies deliver far more absolute value to affluent households than to the poor, regardless of cascading pass-through—remained analytically dominant throughout the exchange. CON landed credible blows on administrative exclusion and political sustainability, but PRO successfully absorbed the cost-of-living concern by showing a calibrated transfer could replicate that protection at a fraction of the fiscal cost. PRO’s clipped speeches were a real execution cost, yet the central regressivity argument was never squarely neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s reframing of cascading benefits as trickle-down economics for fuel and the subsequent residue imagery, which recurred across speeches and decisively undermined CON’s claim that indirect subsidy capture protects the poor comparably to direct transfers.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing the core clash around distributional efficiency and comparative leakage. A’s “cascade vs. residue” metaphor crystallized why universal subsidies inherently bleed value before reaching the poor, directly neutralizing B’s “cascading benefits” argument. A also effectively turned B’s political feasibility claim by highlighting the procyclical fiscal danger of subsidies during price spikes, making them unsustainable precisely when needed. While B raised valid concerns about informal worker exclusion and real-time price protection, B relied on asserted pass-through percentages that felt brittle and failed to fully resolve the fiscal sustainability clash. A’s comparative weighing was sharper, more progressive across rounds, and provided a cleaner reason to vote PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2, where A directly answers the pressure questions and dismantles B’s “automatic stabilizer” claim by explaining the procyclical nature of fuel subsidies, then zooms out to the “leakage vs. leakage” comparison. This turn successfully reframed the political and fiscal clash while sharpening the distributional weighing.
