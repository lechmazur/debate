### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (Side A) built a stronger analytical path to victory on the central clash: whether mandated portability produces more training or less. The prisoner's dilemma argument was the spine of CON's case—individual employers facing independent budget decisions have a dominant strategy to underinvest and poach from others—and PRO never supplied a binding enforcement mechanism to dissolve it. PRO's best answer to this pressure was that employers might substitute cash wages, which inadvertently conceded that the training accounts themselves could disappear, undermining the very benefit the proposition is meant to protect. CON's rebuttal of the union apprenticeship analogy [A2.3] was the debate's most analytically precise moment: correctly distinguishing jointly governed, collectively bargained contribution pools from unilateral employer commitments repurposed by regulation. CON also dismantled PRO's "earned compensation" reframe with the "forward-looking conditional commitment" distinction, reinforced by the health insurance analogy in Rebuttal 2. PRO had real strengths—the job lock argument resonates, the non-compete comparison was fresh, and the "corporate leash vs. ladder" framing was memorable—but PRO's rhetorical polish never translated into a genuine resolution of the incentive problem. CON's closings sharpened the winning question ("does this produce more training or less?") clearly and without overreach, while PRO's closings leaned heavily on the same emotional frame without advancing the argument. The margin reflects that PRO was competitive and not without substance, but CON controlled the decisive clash from Round 1 onward.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal of the union apprenticeship example [A2.3]: PRO cited multi-employer union programs as proof that sector-wide portable systems work without collapsing into defection. CON sharply distinguished these as collectively bargained, jointly governed pools with negotiated contribution rates binding all parties—structurally unlike a mandate forcing individual employers to make their unilateral commitments portable. This directly neutralized PRO's strongest empirical anchor and was never adequately answered.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): A won by keeping the debate centered on the incentive problem: if employer-funded accounts must follow workers to competitors, firms have weaker reasons to fund robust training in the first place. B had a persuasive job-lock frame and strong rhetoric, but never fully solved A's free-rider argument or explained what binding mechanism makes the sector-wide reciprocity work. A also effectively distinguished union apprenticeship pools from unilateral employer accounts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal to the apprenticeship example was decisive: collectively bargained multi-employer pools do not prove that mandatory portability of individual employer commitments will avoid defection.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side B won by exposing the fatal incentive gap in Side A's model. B consistently showed that without a binding collective mechanism, individual firms face a prisoner's dilemma where defection (underinvesting and poaching) is rational. B decisively distinguished Side A's union apprenticeship analogy—showing those are jointly governed pools, not unilateral mandates—neutralizing A's main empirical support. B also forced A into a partial concession: if firms substitute cash for training accounts to avoid portability, the specific training benefit A claims to protect disappears. A's "earned compensation" reframe was effectively countered by B's observation that unused future training funds are conditional commitments, not wages. A never resolved who bears the cost when an account transfers or why sector-wide reciprocity would spontaneously emerge from independent firm decisions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's destruction of the union apprenticeship analogy (B2.3/B4.4) and the pressure question exposing cash substitution (B3.1) were decisive. B showed that multi-employer union pools operate through negotiated, binding collective agreements—nothing like mandating portability on individual employment contracts. When combined with B3.1, which revealed that A's policy could simply cause employers to convert training accounts into ordinary wages (defeating the purpose), A's empirical and economic case collapsed.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO wins on the decisive clash. CON's entire load-bearing argument—that portability will cause rational employers to abolish flexible training accounts entirely—is a theoretical prediction that PRO challenges with robust empirical counterexamples: 401k matching has not disappeared despite full portability, and professional licensing funding persists despite credential portability. CON's response throughout is to insist that training accounts are categorically different because they fund "future" rather than "past" productivity, but this distinction is weaker than it sounds. A 401k match deposited today also functions as a forward-looking retention and productivity investment; CON never explains why the line falls exactly where they draw it. PRO's vesting-schedule argument in Rebuttal 2 is particularly effective: it shows that training accounts do convert from operational budget to earned entitlement over time, in the same structural way retirement matching does. CON's "unspent financial capital vs. acquired skills" distinction is the genuinely strongest CON move, and it lands some damage—there is a real difference between carrying skills in your head and carrying an unspent fund to a competitor. But PRO answers it adequately with the vesting logic and the same-sector qualifier. CON's GI Bill and HSA rebuttals are decent but don't dislodge the core comparison. PRO's pressure questions were sharper and more targeted than CON's, and PRO answered CON's questions (especially the cash-out trap) by correctly noting that purpose restrictions appear in GI Bill and HSA frameworks without negating earned status. The clipped PRO closing is a minor execution cost but PRO had already established its key arguments. CON's closing is mostly recap. On balance, PRO built the stronger and more empirically grounded path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.7 move—pointing out that CON's logic applied consistently would justify non-portable retirement accounts, since employers fund 401k matches as forward-looking investments too—was the most decisive exchange. CON never escaped this consistency trap, and PRO's Q2 in the pressure round sharpened it further by asking why 401k portability has not destroyed employer matching if the mechanism is the same. CON's "past vs. future labor" distinction is real but too thin to carry the weight CON needed it to bear.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by making the central distinction sharper: acquired skills already move with workers, but the motion also lets workers carry unspent employer-funded training money into future education while employed by a direct competitor. Side B had a plausible compensation and portability analogy, especially with 401(k)s and licensing, but it leaned heavily on assuming training accounts are vested earned pay and never fully answered why future training funds are not a forward-looking employer investment. Side A also turned the same-sector limit into a direct-competitor problem and gave the cleaner net-impact story that broad flexible accounts would be reduced or eliminated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest rebuttal was the human-capital versus unspent-financial-capital distinction, paired with the argument that same-sector portability worsens the free-rider problem because the old employer funds a rival's future productivity.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by defending the intuitive earned-compensation framing and empirically falsifying Side B's central prediction that portability would destroy employer-funded training. B's distinction between acquired human capital and unspent financial capital was sharp, but B never squarely answered why 401(k) portability has not eliminated employer matching, nor overcame A's point that vesting schedules turn training budgets into worker-owned benefits. A's clipped closing was an execution cost, yet the visible portion already collapsed B's past-vs-future distinction and showed that existing portable-benefit regimes contradict B's doomsday scenario. B's free-rider argument remained theoretically coherent but insufficiently grounded to outweigh A's live counterexamples and the proposition's built-in same-sector qualifier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and Pressure Questions exposing that 401(k) matches are fully portable to competitors yet persist, which directly undermined Side B's predictive claim that portability would cause flexible training accounts to vanish.
