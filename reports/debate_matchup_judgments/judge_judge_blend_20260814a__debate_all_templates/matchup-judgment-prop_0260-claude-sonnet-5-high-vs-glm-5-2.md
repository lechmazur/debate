### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that the mandate could worsen gatekeeper concentration while remaining least effective against first-party tracking. A established substantial privacy and consent harms, but its claim that the mandate was strictly additive did not answer B's marginal-harm argument. A also first acknowledged consolidation following ATT, then tried to retreat from that concession and relied on separate antitrust action outside the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was its response to A's claim that concentration already exists. B correctly argued that existing harm does not mean a mandate cannot intensify it, while pressing A on the lack of a workable system-level distinction between necessary first-party processing and behavioral profiling.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's proposed mechanism. While Side A argued effectively on the moral necessity of consent, Side B successfully proved that the mandate would fail technically (adversarial tracking vs stable seatbelts) and economically (killing independent publishers while first-party giants exploit "purpose" loopholes). Side B's final crystallization that the mandate actively redistributes power rather than sitting inertly on the status quo was decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1 to B4.3, where Side B explains why Side A's "purpose" test fails technically by contrasting invisible first-party tracking with visible third-party signals, effectively closing Side A's attempt to solve the moat argument.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): CON demonstrated that the mandate would likely be captured by dominant OS vendors, turning the promised exit door into a vendor-controlled gate. PRO never adequately resolved the first-party loophole: when pressed, PRO offered a purpose-based distinction that CON showed would grant Apple and Google even broader discretion to define compliance in their own favor. PROs reliance on future antitrust to fix concentration remained speculative and outside the proposition, while CONs ATT example and regulatory-capture argument showed the mandate would redistribute surveillance power rather than reduce it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CONs Rebuttal 2 dismantling of PROs purpose not identity test for first-party tracking, showing that widening the mandates scope to close the moat would actually move the loophole deeper into the vendors own stack and grant them more discretion over the definition of tracker.
