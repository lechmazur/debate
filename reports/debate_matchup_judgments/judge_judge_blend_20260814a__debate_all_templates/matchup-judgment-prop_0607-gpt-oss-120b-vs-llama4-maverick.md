### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Llama 4 Maverick (PRO); B = GPT-OSS-120B (CON)): Side B won by consistently identifying and pressing the structural defect in Side A's case: trust is a prerequisite for formal intelligence-sharing to function, not a byproduct it can generate. Side A never adequately answered this chicken-and-egg problem. Three exchanges stand out. First, Side B's rebuttal correctly identified the NATO analogy as a category error—NATO has U.S. hierarchical leadership and no colonizer-colonized history, while Japan-Korea would be a peer-to-peer command with no external arbiter—and Side A's response ("gradual approach") never addressed who would arbitrate peer-level disputes. Second, in Rebuttal 2, Side B turned Side A's own pressure question against it: the reason U.S.-mediated sharing works is precisely because the U.S. acts as a neutral filter that insulates each side from the other's political fallout; removing that buffer to create a direct bilateral conduit eliminates the only mechanism that makes exchange workable. Third, Side B's China-escalation risk was raised early and effectively ignored throughout by Side A, leaving a live unanswered route to CON victory. Side A's core arguments—urgency of the North Korean threat and the value of incremental cooperation—have genuine weight but were always asserted rather than defended against the structural criticisms. Side A was also repetitive across rounds, restating "tangible benefits outweigh grievances" without deepening the comparative weighing. Side B's multiple clipping penalties reduce what might otherwise be a larger margin, but the visible content consistently outperformed Side A on direct engagement, sharpness of rebuttal, and crystallization in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 answer to Side A's Q1 pressure question was the most decisive exchange. Side A asked how historical grievances could make bilateral sharing "inherently unreliable" when both nations already share intel with the U.S. despite grievances. Side B's response dismantled the analogy by explaining that the U.S. trilateral architecture functions precisely because the U.S. acts as a neutral filter—data never passes directly between Tokyo and Seoul, so the political fallout of a leak or selective disclosure is neutralized by a third-party buffer. The direct bilateral conduit that PRO proposes would remove that buffer, making the channel uniquely vulnerable to the very grievances PRO claimed were already being managed. This turned PRO's own question into evidence for CON and was never adequately answered.

PRO never overcame the burden of explaining how bilateral intelligence-sharing would avoid the politicization risks that B identified, nor how a joint command between two nations with colonial history and no higher arbiter could function. B's closing crystallized this trust deficit as the decisive structural flaw.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's explanation that the US-centered trilateral system works precisely because Washington acts as a neutral filter, and that direct Japan-Korea bilateral intelligence would remove this protective buffer and weaponize historical grievances (B4.1-B4.2).
