### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the stronger cumulative case across five rounds. Its most decisive move was the regressivity reversal: rather than simply denying that pricing is regressive, PRO showed that the unpriced status quo is already a regressive time-and-money tax on transit-dependent low-income riders whose buses crawl through gridlock, and that ring-fenced revenue plus targeted discounts can flip that dynamic. Side A never adequately answered this baseline comparison; it kept reasserting that pricing harms low-income drivers without engaging the counterpoint that those same workers gain through faster buses. PRO's induced-demand argument against CON's alternatives was sharp and only partially answered: CON's Rebuttal 2 correctly noted that dedicated bus lanes reallocate physical space impervious to induced demand, which was a real point, but it still left general-lane gridlock unresolved and never showed how alternatives scale to full congestion management. PRO's democratic-legitimacy reframe—distinguishing lane occupants from total city population bearing externality costs—materially weakened CON's "majority will" argument, which CON had presented as a trump card. CON's London western extension example was shaky throughout; PRO correctly identified it as a political retreat from one boundary extension, not a failure of the core mechanism, and CON never recovered that ground. CON's closing was largely repetitive, restating the democratic and regressivity points without narrowing the live clash. PRO's closing crystallized the two-illusions framework and linked the fee to funding the very alternatives CON demanded, giving a cleaner comparative reason to vote PRO. CON's strongest moment was the dedicated-bus-lane distinction in Rebuttal 2, but it came too late and too narrowly to shift the overall balance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The regressivity reversal in PRO's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2: PRO turned CON's strongest card by showing that unpriced congestion is itself a regressive burden on bus-riding low-income workers, and that ring-fenced revenue plus discounts flips the distributional math. CON had framed regressivity as its decisive argument but never answered the baseline comparison, leaving PRO's version of fairness standing at the close.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B won by successfully reframing Side A's two main voting issues: democratic legitimacy and regressivity. Side B effectively argued that drivers represent a 'spatial monopoly' rather than a popular mandate by expanding the denominator to include residents, pedestrians, and bus riders. Furthermore, Side B neutralized the regressivity claim by pointing out the status quo time-tax on low-income bus riders and introducing targeted discounts, which Side A dropped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's A1 response, which dismantled the 'majority' claim by distinguishing between who takes up the most road space and the broader population affected by gridlock.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): PRO won by reframing the debate away from a simplistic majority-will framing and toward the management of a finite public commons. CON’s democratic legitimacy argument had intuitive force, but PRO successfully showed that streets belong to all users and residents, not just current occupants, and that deferring to driver opposition preserves a hidden subsidy paid for by bus riders, emergency delays, and pollution. PRO also effectively turned CON’s regressivity argument by demonstrating that unpriced congestion is itself regressive—harming low-income transit riders—and that pricing revenue can fund fare subsidies and targeted discounts. CON never adequately answered this comparative regressivity point, instead repeating that pricing burdens low-income drivers without engaging with the status quo’s burdens on low-income non-drivers. CON’s alternative package of bus lanes, signal priority, and land-use planning was plausible but underdeveloped; PRO’s induced-demand critique showed that physical reallocation alone does not ration peak-hour demand on general roadways, and CON did not convincingly demonstrate that these alternatives eliminate central-district scarcity without any price signal. PRO’s crystallization was cleaner, framing the choice as managed scarcity versus permanent gridlock disguised as deference. CON’s closing was largely a recap that restated democratic objections without sharpening the comparative tradeoff or resolving PRO’s strongest turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s rebuttal to CON’s regressivity charge (B2.2, B4.2): PRO argued that unpriced congestion is already regressive because it slows buses that low-income workers rely on, and that pricing flips the dynamic by generating revenue for fare subsidies and higher bus frequency while targeting discretionary car trips. This turn forced CON into a defensive posture for the rest of the debate, and CON never fully recovered by showing why the status quo’s regressive impacts are less severe than pricing’s package of costs and reinvestment.
