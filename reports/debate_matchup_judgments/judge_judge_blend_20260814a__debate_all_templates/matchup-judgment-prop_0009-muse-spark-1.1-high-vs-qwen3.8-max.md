### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): The decisive exchange was PRO's pressure questions forcing CON to define exactly what practice it defends. CON's answer in [A4.1] essentially conceded that the only remaining defended practice is 22+ hour isolation past day 15 for the "no safe contact possible" case—which PRO immediately identified as the exact box described in the opening. PRO's key move throughout was denying CON's trilemma (release/restraint/sedation vs. torture) by offering monitored individual separation with real human contact as a middle path that satisfies both safety and the abolition standard; CON never convincingly explained why this middle path is unavailable except by gesturing at "staffing shortages" and "any interaction is a weapon" cases, which PRO effectively reframed as a necessity defense for torture ("scarcity justifying torture... we never accept that for waterboarding"). CON's case relied heavily on a hypothetical extreme prisoner for whom literally all contact is dangerous, but never established this is common enough to justify keeping the practice legal rather than as a rare edge case handled through emergency exception rather than the norm the motion targets. PRO's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly, while CON's closing largely restated the trilemma without fully rebutting PRO's answer that separation-with-contact satisfies safety without meeting the tortuous-conditions threshold. Both debaters were skilled and adapted well, but PRO's Q&A extracted a clearer concession and PRO used it more decisively in the following rebuttal and closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question in Turn 5, forcing CON to concede in [A4.1] that the only defended practice is 22+ hour isolation past day 15 when "any contact is dangerous"—which PRO then reframed as identical to the opening's torture box and as a necessity defense analogous to excusing waterboarding under resource scarcity.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by strategically defining 'solitary confinement' using the 22-hour Mandela Rules standard, which allowed them to co-opt Side A's 'humane separation' alternatives as fully compliant with the ban. This forced Side A into the difficult position of defending 22+ hours of pure isolation for extreme cases based on resource scarcity, which Side B effectively countered by comparing it to unacceptable justifications for torture.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal cleanly neutralized Side A's 'humane separation' argument by agreeing with it and pointing out that such separation breaks the 22-hour threshold and is therefore not solitary confinement.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A won by locking the motion to an objective conditions-plus-duration test (22+ hours without meaningful contact past 15 days) and showing B's "humane separation" is already outside that ban. B raised real edge cases but kept collapsing into a scarcity/necessity exception for residual boxes it had earlier conceded should be banned; A repeatedly dissolved the release/restraint/sedation trilemma with non-peer contact that still ends solitary. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly within the constraints, but PRO built a more internally consistent case. PRO's torture definition (foreseeable severe suffering imposed by a controlling authority, regardless of sadistic intent) survived CON's intent-based attack better than CON's rebuttal survived PRO's bright-line defense (the speed-limit analogy answered the "arbitrary line" charge adequately). The pivotal exchange was the Pressure Questions round: CON's Q1 forced PRO to clarify that "humane" restrictive housing with real contact isn't the "solitary" the motion targets, and CON tried to spin this in closing as a full concession that guts PRO's case. But PRO's position remains coherent — the motion targets near-total isolation, not restrictive housing per se — so CON's "you just conceded" framing in the final speech is rhetorically strong but substantively overclaimed rather than fully dismantling PRO's structure. Meanwhile, CON's own alternative (regulated segregation with contact and mental-health checks) relies on the same resources CON criticized PRO's alternatives for lacking, undercutting CON's practicality objection. PRO's answers to the pressure questions were direct, properly labeled, and reinforced its framework, while CON's closing leaned on assertion of concession rather than fresh resolution of the conditions-vs-duration tension it raised. This gives PRO a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Pressure Questions exchange (A3.1/A3.2 and PRO's A1/A2 answers in Rebuttal 2) was decisive: CON's question about whether "humane" long-term separation is still banned exposed a real definitional pressure point, but PRO's direct answer — that genuine contact and programming means it isn't the "solitary confinement" at issue — held together better than CON's later attempt to claim this as an outright concession.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully separating the harms of sensory deprivation (conditions) from the act of prolonged separation (duration). When Side B concedes that humane single-cell separation past 15 days is acceptable, Side A brilliantly weaponizes this to show that the duration of separation is not inherently torture, meaning the proposition bans the wrong mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which crystallize the definitional trap by arguing: 'If harm tracks conditions, you ban degrading conditions, you don't abolish duration and mislabel distance as torture.'
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): PRO built the clearer path by fixing the motion on social/sensory starvation itself and repeatedly forcing CON to show why safety requires that specific tool rather than intensive secure separation. CON landed real pressure on intent, bright-line arbitrariness, and PRO’s late definitional narrowing, but never neutralized the alternatives clash or proved necessity of the banned practice; PRO’s answers to the pressure questions kept the distinction live and decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s A4.1–A4.4 answers: distinguishing true deprivation from regulated separation with contact/programming, which collapsed CON’s “humane unit” defense into PRO’s own alternative.
