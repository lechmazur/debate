### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides executed the format cleanly (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 structure) and avoided fabricated sourcing, relying on the same stock historical analogies (player pianos, radio). The debate hinged on whether compulsory payment can substitute for consent when AI causes market substitution. PRO's strongest move was arguing that individual refusal cannot stop adjacent-catalog training from producing substitutes, so a veto yields "zero compensation" while displacement happens anyway. CON's sharpest response—both in Rebuttal 1 and especially in the pressure-question exchange—drew a consistent distinction between ordinary competition from consented works and forced appropriation of a specific creator's own material, and repeatedly pressed the point that a statutory fee "monetizes the trespass" rather than restoring lost scarcity or bargaining power. CON also better answered the transaction-cost claim by offering concrete alternatives (collectives, opt-in pools, antitrust checks on enclosure) rather than merely asserting impossibility, and effectively turned PRO's "transparent tribunal" claim back on itself by noting political rate-setting is itself capture-prone. PRO's closing reasserted its framing without fully neutralizing CON's core objection that payment does not equal protection, leaving CON's throughline about control versus consolation the more resilient position by the end. It was a close, well-matched debate, but CON's rebuttals more directly damaged PRO's central "displacement is inevitable, so take the money" logic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Pressure Questions exchange: PRO's Q1 asking how a veto helps an artist when adjacent catalogs can produce substitutes anyway, and CON's answer distinguishing "ordinary competition" from "forced appropriation" of the specific artist's own work, which became the fulcrum both closings returned to.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by distinguishing competition from compelled appropriation and showing that compensation cannot necessarily restore lost control or scarcity. Side A made a strong transaction-cost and aggregate-substitution case, but repeatedly overclaimed that a compulsory pool would guarantee equitable remuneration or creator survival without adequately explaining rates and allocation. B preserved a viable consent-based alternative through collectives and licensed corpora while directly answering A’s strongest pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1 most decisively answered A’s claim that refusal is useless: models trained on consenting catalogs may still compete, but that does not make forced ingestion of a holdout’s own portfolio equivalent. This preserved the value of the veto and exposed the limits of treating a statutory payment as full repair.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): A wins by exposing that B’s prized "right to refuse" is economically illusory in generative AI markets: models can approximate a holdout’s style from adjacent consenting catalogs, giving the artist identical displacement but zero compensation. A’s compulsory licensing framework guarantees remuneration and breaks anti-competitive bottlenecks, while B’s voluntarism offers only abstract hopes about opt-in pools and antitrust that fail to protect independents. B’s historical distinctions and principled defense of copyright are sharp, but they cannot overcome the decisive pragmatic reality that control without compensation is empty when technological substitution is inevitable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 pressure answers ([A4.1]–[A4.4]), where A demonstrates that an individual veto cannot prevent stylistic substitution from neighboring datasets, turning B’s "right to refuse" into a hollow guarantee of poverty rather than market protection.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B more successfully sustained the practical burden of the motion: it kept returning to the scale problem (millions of works, no feasible one-by-one clearance) and turned A's "corporate capture" objection back on A by noting voluntary markets already favor the same large players, leaving freelancers with nothing. B's pressure questions exposed a real tension in A's case—A had conceded that ordinary infringement/output doctrine cannot stop style-level substitution, yet still leaned on "consent" as the mechanism that supposedly protects and pays creators. A's answer (distinguishing ingestion-stage infringement from output-stage infringement) was a legitimate move but was not clearly integrated into the rest of A's case, and A never squarely answered why voluntary markets would actually deliver freelancers better outcomes than a statutory pool, especially given A's own admission that existing voluntary deals are dominated by large intermediaries. A's rhetoric ("train their own replacement," "subsidize their own obsolescence") was vivid and emotionally resonant, and its opening drew a sharp, valid distinction between broadcasting and AI substitution, but its rebuttals repeated the same consent/leverage assertion without closing the scale and capture gaps B kept pressing. B's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly: paid training under enforceable rules versus unpaid or offshored training under a "fiction of consent."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Questions (Q2) forcing A to reconcile its concession that output-level infringement doctrine cannot stop style substitution with its claim that an absolute right to refuse delivers real payment—A's answer partially addressed this but left the freelancer-leverage problem under voluntary markets largely unresolved, which B then exploited decisively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won the decisive leverage comparison. It showed that compulsory permission removes creators’ ability to withhold works before potentially substitutive training, while voluntary collective licensing and the need for legally clean commercial datasets offer a plausible alternative to A’s asserted choice between gridlock and unpaid offshore training. A made a strong scalability and compensation case, but repeatedly assumed training was inevitable and did not adequately answer B’s point that domestic liability, market access, and collective negotiation can make consent meaningful. A also never established that statutory payments would outweigh lost bargaining power or displacement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answers in Rebuttal 2 were decisive: actionable infringement, collective bargaining, and commercial demand for clean chain-of-title directly answered A’s questions and weakened its central claim that rejecting compulsory licensing necessarily means unpaid overseas ingestion. A’s closing largely repeated inevitability rather than resolving that response.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that the proposition explicitly empowers the corporate intermediaries it names—publishers, labels, and studios—stripping individual creators of consent while substituting inadequate statutory micropayments for real bargaining leverage. B successfully collapsed A's radio analogy by showing AI training is market substitution rather than distribution, and that ingestion itself builds the replacement engine. While A made a strong practical case about volume and gridlock, B's frame that consent is the creator's only structural leverage proved the cleaner route to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal was B's collapse of the ingestion/output wall by arguing that generative AI extracts style and technique to create limitless competing substitutes, making ingestion itself the economic substitution and rendering post-hoc output policing useless. This neutralized A's "non-consumptive analysis" frame and forced A into a concession that displacement is inevitable, leaving only a choice between obsolescence with a small check or without one.
