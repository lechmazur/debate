- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO built the cleaner causal story and held the most important ground across the exchange. The core distinction—between access to recorded information and the acquisition of tacit judgment—was introduced early, pressed through both rebuttals, and never fully neutralized by CON. PRO's explanation of why the harm would be specifically under-acknowledged (invisible to short-term output metrics, surfacing only as a ceiling effect years later) was a direct answer to the proposition's unusual burden, and it held up under pressure. CON's equity reframing—that office osmosis was never equitable or universal—was the strongest counter-move and genuinely illuminating, but it argued the old system was bad rather than demonstrating the new system is better for tacit development. CON's B4.3 claim that "remote-first firms have promoted junior cohorts with no evidence of worse strategic judgment" was overconfident in a closed-book setting and deserved more skepticism. CON's pressure question on the burden ("what specific gap have you demonstrated?") was sharp, and PRO's A1 answer—that dashboards are designed to miss precisely this loss—was adequate without being airtight, which is why the margin stays competitive rather than decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 (A2.1–A2.4) naming the substitution error at the heart of CON's case: conflating access to finished products with the acquisition of judgment. The distinction between "error-tagging after the fact" and "intervening in the moment with tone, context, and a live 'here's why'" sharpened the core mechanism of harm and framed why async tools, however consistent in logging, do not replicate the learning loop that once shaped mental models in real time.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s information-versus-judgment rebuttal was most decisive: searchable archives and async comments may improve access to outputs, but they do not fully replace live observation, room-reading, and informal trust formation for junior employees.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A wins by consistently meeting the motion's burden of proving harm exceeds what is admitted, directly answering pressure questions with mechanisms like replayable debriefs and output-based trust while exposing PRO's equity blind spot and unproven long-term speculation. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won on three compounding factors. First, Side B had three speeches clipped (Opening, Rebuttal 2, Closing), a real execution cost that left its arguments visibly incomplete across half the rounds. Second, and more substantively, Side A consistently held the correct threshold for the motion: the proposition requires only that the harm is underacknowledged relative to what employers and workers "usually admit" in ordinary public discourse, not that silence is total. Side A's A4.1 cleanly distinguished between niche HR pilots and public-facing messaging, and that distinction was never squarely neutralized. CON kept pointing to internal surveys and mentorship programs as proof of open acknowledgment, but Side A correctly noted these are siloed materials that do not reach most juniors or the broader public WFH conversation. Third, Side A's causal account of why the silence persists—misaligned incentives pushing employers toward retention framing and juniors toward self-censorship—was more persuasive than CON's claim that "the conversation is already happening at the right volume." CON's equity argument (introverts and minorities fared poorly in office cultures) was genuinely strong and partially answered A4.2, but it ultimately addressed a different question: whether the old office was fair, not whether the harm from WFH is adequately acknowledged now. The reach-does-not-equal-career-capital rebuttal (A2.4, A4.2) was never cleanly dismantled: personal endorsement for stretch assignments still relies on relational trust that async portfolios cannot fully replicate. The debate was competitive on the substantive merits, but Side A's cleaner threshold definition, consistent maintenance of its core causal story, and Side B's repeated clipping gave a clear but not dominant edge to PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.1 response to Side B's Q1 was the most decisive exchange. CON's question demanded what admission would satisfy PRO; Side A answered by distinguishing between niche internal HR documents and the public-facing WFH narrative, then specifying a concrete benchmark (public acknowledgment that junior promotion rates lag due to remote work). This directly answered the burden-testing question without conceding ground, sharpened the motion's threshold, and exposed that CON's cited evidence—pilots, surveys, town hall mentions—lives in channels most juniors never access and does not constitute the "usually admit" standard the proposition targets.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the motion focused on disproportionate junior harm being downplayed, not totally hidden. Side A made strong points about structured remote mentorship, async visibility, and the inequities of the old office, but B more convincingly argued that these substitutes do not replace tacit learning, personal trust, and senior endorsement for juniors. B also handled the acknowledgment clash better by distinguishing generic mentoring talk from candid admission that WFH structurally worsens junior promotion trajectories. A was competitive, but repeated the open-discussion answer without fully matching B's magnitude claim, and several clipped turns were an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was the key exchange: it answered A's pressure by saying surveys and HR pilots acknowledge generic gaps but not the specific disproportionate junior career damage, while also conceding office inequity without surrendering the baseline tacit-learning advantage.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) more convincingly demonstrated the proposition's core on underacknowledgment, highlighting public messaging that frames WFH as uniformly positive while incentives drive self-censorship and burial of junior-specific harms in niche reports. B directly answered pressure questions on sufficient admissions and real-time endorsements in rebuttals. Side A (CON) offered coherent counters on async transparency and adaptations but suffered execution costs from multiple clipped turns, leaving responses incomplete on visibility claims and failing to fully neutralize the public-private gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 answered the pressure questions by defining sufficient admission as a public statement on lagging promotions and explaining why async misses real-time endorsements, sharpening the clash on what "admit" requires.
