### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON won primarily through two moves that PRO never fully answered. First, the decoding-vs-attention reframe: CON argued that the real bottleneck for children most at risk of reading failure is phonological decoding, not sustained attention, and that structured phonics apps directly address that bottleneck in ways a worksheet the child cannot yet decode does not. PRO's entire sequencing case rests on attention being the scarce resource worth protecting; CON challenged that premise directly and PRO never dislodged it. Second, CON drew a clean distinction between unrestricted consumer apps and teacher-vetted structured phonics sequences with completion gates and linear progressions. PRO's "fragmented attention" and "task-switching" claims only hold for the former, but PRO never acknowledged the difference, leaving its core mechanism underdeveloped. PRO's equity flip—that tablets train task-switching in disadvantaged children—was the weakest moment: it relied on the same undifferentiated app claim CON had already undermined. PRO's "device-light, not absolute" concession in Rebuttal 2 also softened its own bright-line policy argument by quietly accommodating the motor-disability case. CON's pressure questions were sharper and PRO's answers more evasive. The clipping of CON's opening is a real execution cost but was offset by stronger rebuttal work across the remaining rounds. PRO had genuine intuitive appeal with the sequencing metaphor and proprioception argument, which is why the margin is competitive rather than dominant.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 pivot: identifying decoding rather than attention as the actual bottleneck for at-risk children, and distinguishing structured phonics apps with linear completion gates from open-ended consumer apps. This move simultaneously neutralized PRO's core mechanism (fragmented attention), exposed the speculative nature of PRO's equity flip, and reframed the deciding question as which deprivation—of paper's uninterrupted surface or of a proven decoding route—costs at-risk children more. PRO's closing never closed this gap.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B wins by successfully isolating decoding, rather than attention, as the primary bottleneck for early literacy. By introducing the specific mechanics of instructional apps (linear sequences, completion gates), Side B neutralized Side A's core premise that all screen time trains task-switching.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to the 'uninterrupted surface' argument was decisive, effectively pointing out that a worksheet a struggling reader cannot decode is not an equal surface, but a 'stalled one.'
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): CON won by exposing that PRO's sequencing argument assumes attention stamina is the binding constraint for all children, while the equity debate actually turns on decoding. CON showed that structured phonics apps with completion gates are not the task-switching hazard PRO claimed, and that withholding devices removes a concrete decoding intervention from the children who most need it, trading a working route into reading for an unproven paper-first hypothesis. PRO never established that paper alone closes decoding gaps faster, and failed to answer CON's reframing of the equity clash from "same surface" to "effective intervention."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal distinguishing teacher-vetted structured phonics apps from unrestricted consumer apps, which defanged PRO's core task-switching mechanism, and the closing crystallization that a worksheet a struggling reader cannot decode is a "stalled" surface—not an equal one.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO (B) ran the stronger debate across the full exchange for three cumulative reasons. First, PRO identified a genuine logical contradiction in CON's case early and held it to the end: CON simultaneously argued devices are the developmentally appropriate default medium for five-year-olds and that they require teacher-managed locks, usage tracking, and feature restrictions to function safely. CON's response—"managed locks turn devices into reliable tools"—restated that locks work without resolving why a medium that needs constant containment should be the default medium during the developmental window in question. The contradiction stood live throughout. Second, PRO's risk-asymmetry frame in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive comparative argument in the debate: the recoverable cost of learning to type a year later vs. the potentially irreversible cost of a six-year-old's attention architecture being shaped by engagement-optimized software. CON's Rebuttal 2 (only 188 of 340 words) briefly inverted this framing—claiming the compounding deficit lands on lower-income students—but left the asymmetry argument largely unaddressed in depth. Third, PRO effectively partially turned CON's strongest point, the equity argument, by noting that affluent families are actively paying to remove early device exposure while CON's policy would impose it on lower-income students. CON never adequately answered this inversion. CON's best live route—that adaptive, personalized digital feedback cannot be scaled on paper—was a real point, but PRO's A1 answer (leveled readers, small groups, peer tutors scaling for generations) was adequate and CON never returned to press on scaling in the closing. PRO's two clipped speeches are real execution costs but the core framing survived both clips intact, and CON's underuse of the Closing and Rebuttal 2 word caps left more burden unanswered than PRO's truncations did.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.5 point—"Con cannot simultaneously claim devices are safe enough for a five-year-old and dangerous enough to need a teacher's software leash every minute"—was the sharpest blow of the debate. It identified a real internal contradiction in CON's core position and was reinforced by Q1 in the Pressure Questions round. CON's answer restated that locks make devices into reliable tools without explaining why a medium that requires active containment to be safe should nonetheless be the developmentally appropriate default, leaving the contradiction standing through the closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by establishing a clear, decisive asymmetry: delaying interface skills is a recoverable risk because children adapt quickly, whereas damaging early attention architecture is irreversible. Side B failed to adequately answer this weighing and ultimately conceded it in their closing speech by stating that tool competence remains recoverable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized Side B's equity argument by pointing out that wealthy families are paying premiums for device-light classrooms, turning Side B's push for early tech access into an imposition of an inferior learning environment on lower-income students.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by establishing a decisive asymmetry of risk: foundational attention architecture, decoding habits, and fine-motor memory shaped in early childhood are difficult to reverse, whereas digital interface fluency is rapidly acquired later and recovered within months. Side A turned Side B's equity argument by showing it would force lower-income students into the screen-heavy environments affluent families are already rejecting, and exposed that Side B's own defense—requiring software locks, tracking, and restrictions—concedes tablets are not developmentally appropriate as a default medium for young children. Side B never fully answered the "locks prove hazard" contradiction or provided a convincing causal mechanism showing the digital fluency gap is irreversible, leaving Side A's developmental sequencing logic as the clearer live route to victory despite execution costs from clipped turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal that teacher-managed locks and restrictions prove tablets are hazards requiring containment, not developmentally appropriate defaults—paired with the turn that Side B's equity argument would impose on lower-income students the very exposure affluent families are fleeing.
