### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): The decisive clash was whether decriminalization is the causally indispensable mechanism for the claimed benefits, or whether targeted policies (broad Good Samaritan immunity, diversion, expungement, drug courts) can produce the same benefits without surrendering enforcement leverage. CON's "separation argument"—that every benefit PRO names can be authorized without decriminalizing possession itself, while only decriminalization sacrifices the earliest enforceable intervention point—was the strongest move in the debate. PRO's best counter, the "indispensable prerequisite" claim, was asserted rather than demonstrated: PRO never showed why harm-reduction tools cannot function without full possession decriminalization, only that they function better in its absence. CON's "highest-risk user" pressure—that the people most likely to die are precisely those who cannot use voluntary pathways—was introduced cleanly and never squarely answered; PRO's "peer navigation and civil referrals" response was correctly identified as inadequate for the deepest cases. CON's closing crystallized the burden well: PRO needed to prove decriminalization itself causes better outcomes compared to a system with targeted immunity plus supervised treatment without mass incarceration, and never separated that proof from bundled public-health tools. Execution costs matter in both directions: CON had two speeches clipped for exceeding the word limit, which is a real penalty, and PRO suffered a meaningful grounding penalty for citing non-existent reference IDs [C1.4, C2.1] in Rebuttal 2. The clip penalty narrows the margin but does not reverse it, because the CON arguments visible in each round were sharper on the live clash, and PRO's strongest rhetorical move—the "cage vs. care" binary—was repeatedly and accurately exposed as a caricature of the actual alternative on offer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's bootstrapping rebuttal in Rebuttal 2 [B4.3]: PRO credits decriminalization for every benefit of naloxone, drug-checking, outreach, and treatment, then blames every bad outcome on underfunding or "punitive residue." CON correctly identified this as the core evasion—services can be authorized, immunized, and funded without removing possession prohibition, so decriminalization is not the indispensable cause but the part that surrenders leverage. PRO never cleanly answered this in any subsequent speech.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won narrowly by sustaining concrete, grounded harms of punitive enforcement—bystander fear delaying 911 calls, post-release overdose spikes from jail-induced tolerance loss, and criminal records manufacturing survival crime—that Side B never fully neutralized. While B’s bootstrapping critique was incisive, it relied on an idealized enforcement alternative that partially evaded the "continuing punitive enforcement" frame of the motion and suffered real execution costs from clipped speeches. A’s pressure question on the historical failure of punitive enforcement remained inadequately answered, leaving A’s live route to victory slightly more durable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal exposed that PRO conflated decriminalization with adjacent public health investments, forcing the central clash over whether decriminalization itself does unique causal work that targeted immunity, diversion, and treatment-backed supervision cannot replicate.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A won by successfully isolating the specific effects of decriminalization from broader harm-reduction and treatment policies. A demonstrated that targeted Good Samaritan immunity could address the 911-fear mechanism without sacrificing the enforceable leverage (diversion, supervised treatment) needed for high-risk, non-voluntary users. Side B’s claim that decriminalization is an "indispensable prerequisite" for public health tools was historically shaky and allowed A to frame B’s case as bootstrapping unrelated services to the motion. A’s nuanced defense of civil and therapeutic leverage over mass incarceration neutralized B’s "cage vs. care" binary, giving A the cleaner comparative path on both overdose prevention and downstream crime.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 effectively dismantled Side B’s conflation of policies by exposing the "bootstrapping" fallacy: crediting decriminalization for benefits that actually stem from funded treatment and naloxone, which can exist alongside targeted immunity. A’s direct answer to the pressure questions clarified that broad, operational Good Samaritan laws solve the specific 911 fear without abandoning early intervention points for chaotic users, decisively shifting the weighing to CON’s favor.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A (PRO) won the decisive causal exchanges. Its core mechanism—fear of arrest pushes users into isolation, delays 911 calls, interrupts tolerance through short detention, and releases people back to a toxic supply—was specific, concrete, and never squarely defeated by CON. CON's central argument (legal leverage through drug courts) was asserted but never defended against A's sharp challenge: why must coercion come specifically through criminal possession arrests rather than enforcement of downstream crimes, civil commitment, or diversion without arrest? CON's A1 in Rebuttal 2 claimed that "waiting for downstream crimes requires a victim to be harmed," but this never engaged with civil or administrative alternatives and tacitly conceded the mechanism question. On Oregon, CON invoked it repeatedly but never isolated the causal mechanism from the confounders PRO named (fentanyl spread, pandemic disruption, homelessness, weak treatment rollout). The "microscopic fraction sought help" claim was used as if causally decisive, but without separating those confounders it was illustrative at most. PRO's A1/A2 answers in Rebuttal 2 were direct and properly labeled; the format was followed. The repeated clipping of Side A's turns is a real execution cost but did not erase the argumentative advantage: what remained visible was structurally superior—more concrete moments, cleaner comparison, better answered pressure. CON's rhetorical framing ("compassionate policy requires intervention, not abandonment") was memorable but substituted emotional assertion for mechanistic argument in the closing rather than sharpening the live clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answers to the pressure questions were the most decisive exchange. A's A2 directly dismantled CON's open-air market argument by specifying that police retain authority to enforce trafficking, weapons, assault, theft, coercion, nuisance, and commercial dealing—that decriminalization removes only the blunt shortcut of arresting users as a proxy for dismantling supply. A's A1 reframed the treatment-entry question entirely: overdose prevention cannot wait on compelling recovery because the lethal moment may be tonight, and the specific mechanisms (naloxone, outreach, low-barrier access, crisis referral) operate without requiring a criminal record to trigger them. CON's responses to these same answers in Rebuttal 2 were largely restatements of the opening rather than genuine engagement with the narrowed points.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by establishing concrete, unrebutted mechanisms linking criminal possession enforcement to higher overdose fatalities—secrecy-driven solitary use, delayed 911 calls, and tolerance disruption from jail cycling—while Side B repeatedly asserted that decriminalization increases use and crime without isolating Oregon from confounding factors or explaining why legal leverage requires possession arrests rather than enforcement against actual theft and violence. A’s reframe that “leverage is not a synonym for safety” and that overdose is decided in immediate survival moments rather than theoretical treatment mandates crystallized a clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 reframe that overdose prevention cannot wait for compelled recovery because “the next use may happen tonight,” paired with its direct dismantling of the Oregon example by highlighting unisolated confounders (fentanyl spread, homelessness, weak services), which Side B never successfully answered.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by establishing a cleaner causal pathway on the motion's core metrics. PRO's mechanism linking punitive enforcement to higher overdose risk (pushing use underground, tolerance interruption post-detention, delayed 911 calls) directly addressed the prompt and remained largely unanswered by CON, who relied on abstract "legal leverage" claims. PRO effectively mitigated CON's Oregon example by isolating confounders (fentanyl, pandemic, weak rollout) and correctly noting it doesn't prove re-criminalization improves emergency response behaviors. PRO also answered pressure questions directly with the required A1/A2 format, clarifying that non-criminal pathways and targeted enforcement still exist. CON's case was coherent but repetitive, failing to weigh the harms of coercion against PRO's concrete risk model. PRO incurred an execution cost for repeated clipping, but the visible argumentation was sharper, more responsive, and easier to vote on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 directly answered CON's pressure questions using the required format, dismantling the false binary between arrest and abandonment. By contrasting CON's abstract "leverage" with the concrete, lethal moments created by criminalization (hiding, tolerance loss, warrant cycles), PRO crystallized the decisive comparative advantage on both overdose deaths and downstream crime.
