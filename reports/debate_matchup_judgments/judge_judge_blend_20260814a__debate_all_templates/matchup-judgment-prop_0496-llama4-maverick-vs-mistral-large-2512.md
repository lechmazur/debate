### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Llama 4 Maverick (PRO); B = Mistral Large 3 (CON)): Side B won by consistently framing the debate's decisive comparison correctly: not PMCs versus a perfect accountability ideal, but PMCs versus fallible state militaries. B's opening established this frame with the Abu Ghraib counterexample and the Montreux Document. Through the rebuttals, B sharpened it into a concrete comparative claim—that when contractors commit abuses (Nisour Square leading to convictions) they face more tangible consequences than uniformed soldiers committing comparable acts in Iraq and Afghanistan, where prosecutions were rare. A never squarely answered this inversion. A's core argument—that state militaries are subject to "established parliamentary processes"—was repeatedly asserted but never demonstrated to work better in practice. B's pressure questions were sharper: Q1 exposed the classified-contract problem (undermining A's transparency narrative) and Q2 distinguished procurement debates from battlefield oversight, a distinction A conceded in Rebuttal 2 without recovering from it. A's own Q2 in the pressure round was somewhat self-defeating: if state forces are fallible, A needed to explain why that strengthens a ban rather than just compounding the problem, and it never did. A's closing recycled earlier points without sharpening the clash or addressing B's most damaging arguments, whereas B's closing—despite being clipped at the end—crystallized the "myth of state virtue" frame effectively. A's consistent under-use of word limits and failure to develop the sovereign-function and chain-of-command arguments into real comparative pressure left B's live route to victory—that a ban removes accountability rather than restoring it—unrebutted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 comparison between Blackwater's Nisour Square convictions and the rarity of uniformed-soldier prosecutions for comparable abuses in Iraq and Afghanistan. This directly inverted A's central premise that state militaries are more accountable, offered a concrete real-world example grounded in public knowledge, and A's closing never addressed it, leaving it as the clearest live claim in the debate.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's best exchange was against B's contract-transparency theory: procurement scrutiny and contract termination do not equal real-time democratic control over combat decisions, especially where contracts may be classified or redacted.

A's car-brakes analogy crystallized why "eventually accountable" is not democratic accountability. B never escaped the contradiction A identified: if regulation already existed through the same oversight mechanisms that failed in every cited scandal, why would the same mechanisms succeed next time? Both format clippings on A's opening and closing are mild execution costs but don't disturb the argumentative superiority across the full exchange.
