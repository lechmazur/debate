### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins on the strength of a cleaner, consistently complete case across all rounds, while Side A (CON) suffered a meaningful execution cost from being clipped in four separate speeches—Opening, Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2, and Closing—leaving several arguments incomplete at the point of evaluation. The central clash was whether an explicit target adds anything beyond what macroprudential tools already provide. PRO's answer—that tools exist but are deployed timidly and late because no mandate removes the excuse for inaction, and that "mandate-driven political cover converts discretion into obligation"—was coherent and never squarely neutralized. CON's best analytical contribution, the leverage/price distinction (leverage is the disease, price the symptom; targeting price forces tightening against scarcity-driven markets), is genuinely sharp and partially lands. However, PRO pre-empted it by specifying that credit-growth and debt-service metrics would filter scarcity-driven appreciation from leverage-driven appreciation, and CON's follow-up—that if tracking leverage separately the price target is redundant—was made in a clipped speech and could not be fully developed. PRO's crystallizing line, "accountability that has not been tried beats discretion that has already failed," went unanswered as a comparative claim. CON's Ireland counter (ECB-wide target would have misfitted Germany vs. Ireland) was effective but PRO's response—Ireland lacked national tools precisely because no explicit mandate required them—held up. Overall PRO maintained the decisive comparative claim about mandate-driven inaction while CON's incomplete speeches left real pressure unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q1: the institutional mechanism is mandate-driven political cover that converts "we probably should tighten" into "we are obligated to tighten," giving regulators statutory cover against concentrated homeowner and lender opposition. This directly answered the sharpest question CON posed, and CON's response—that a target is "a hope, not a design"—while catchy, was itself underdeveloped (and clipped) and did not explain why regulators with existing authority have consistently waited until leverage became systemic.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. A made a plausible case that CPI targeting misses housing leverage cycles and that an explicit mandate could overcome political inaction. But B more cleanly separated asset prices from the actual systemic danger: leverage. That distinction let B answer A's crisis examples, blunt-tool concerns, and mandate argument by saying central banks should target credit risk directly, not housing prices as such. A's "mandate-driven political cover" remained plausible but underdeveloped, while B's redundancy/misdiagnosis point against a price target was the sharper decision rule. B's repeated clipping is an execution cost, but the visible argument was still stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive rebuttal was that if credit-growth and debt-service metrics are needed to distinguish dangerous housing booms from scarcity-driven price increases, then leverage is the real target; the asset-price target is either redundant when it follows leverage or misleading when price and leverage diverge.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO wins on the core mandate/accountability clash; CON's leverage-vs-price distinction was strong but weakened by repeated clips and incomplete answers to pressure questions on mechanisms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO Rebuttal 2 (B4) directly answered pressure Qs on political cover and symmetry, exposing CON's repeated reliance on unproven "better legislation" without a trigger.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but CON (Side A) held the decisive edge on the most important sustained clash: accountability without authority produces scapegoating, not governance. PRO's best counter — the FSOC coordination precedent — was plausible but never closed the enforcement gap. If the rate-setter misses an inflation target, it adjusts the one instrument it controls; if it misses a housing target, it publishes a benchmark and waits for agencies it cannot compel, which hands political cover to the coalition that defeated every prior restraint. CON pressed this asymmetry — floor hardened into institutional obligation, ceiling staying aspirational — through rebuttals and closing without meaningful neutralization. PRO's strongest genuine move was distinguishing Ireland/Spain's vague "financial stability" mandates from an explicit, metric-anchored housing target, which partly answered CON's Q1; but CON's response (explicit benchmarks still don't dissolve the political coalition that benefits from booms) remained live. PRO also made a real comparative-regime argument (the status quo produced 2008), and PRO's turn on the tools concession was clever. However, PRO had three rounds clipped for exceeding the word limit — a real execution cost — and its FSOC answer, while adequate, was the weakest link in an otherwise solid case. CON's "fire alarm ringing for twenty years is not governance" and the scapegoating framing were the most memorable and best-sustained arguments in the debate, and they were never squarely overturned.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's floor/ceiling asymmetry argument developed across Rebuttal 1 and sustained through the closing: an explicit housing-price target regularizes floor intervention (making it an institutional obligation rather than a discretionary choice) while the ceiling stays aspirational because hiking against homeowners, builders, banks, and local governments faces the same political coalition that defeated every prior restraint. PRO's response — that the target creates a ceiling that currently has no advocate — was the right framing but never adequately addressed CON's enforcement mechanism: without command over the instruments that would impose the ceiling, the central bank can only name and shame, which CON correctly called scapegoating dressed as coordination.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): B won by making the central issue accountability without authority. A built a plausible case that housing bubbles are dangerous and that explicit targets create pressure to act, but B more convincingly showed that the relevant tools often sit outside the rate-setting central bank, that a target does not overcome political coalitions, and that it may harden the floor while leaving the ceiling aspirational. A’s clipped turns and some overreach on coordination weakened an otherwise strong case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer that an explicit housing target would blame the central bank for outcomes it cannot enforce, while regularizing support for falling prices, was the most decisive exchange against A’s mandate-solves-politics claim.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): B wins because its mandate argument directly explained why vague stability rules failed in Ireland/Spain and why an explicit housing target would create visible accountability the status quo lacks; A’s coordination claim offered no alternative mechanism and left the floor-bias asymmetry live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2’s A1/A2 exchange on concrete benchmarks vs vague mandates and coordinating without control.
