### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won on three compounding advantages. First, the framing discipline held throughout: A consistently compared AI to the actual status quo—high turnover, rigid scripts, inconsistent answers—rather than an idealized human service. B never fully dislodged that comparison, instead appealing to "empowered first-line staff" that A correctly labelled aspirational. Second, A identified a genuine logical contradiction in B's case (scale makes errors catastrophic AND companies are too cost-optimized to fix them) and pressed it cleanly in Rebuttal 2 and the closing. B's attempted resolution—"silent errors" that don't trigger escalation—was valid but narrow, conceding the broader detection argument and leaving the contradiction largely intact. Third, grounding discipline favoured A: B introduced suspiciously precise numbers in Rebuttal 2 ("30-50% implementation overhead," "15-20% churn reduction") that looked like brittle fake precision in a closed-book debate, and those figures did rhetorical work A did not have to match because A avoided the same move. B's pressure questions were sharp, and A's A1/A2 answers were adequate rather than excellent, but B's own A1/A2 answers smuggled in the unwarranted statistics noted above, partially neutralising the pressure B had created. A's closing crystallised the clash without new material; B's closing was more repetitive. On the motion as written—net cost reduction versus net trust/accountability/quality degradation—A's case remained the more internally consistent route to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's identification, in Rebuttal 2 and the closing, of the internal contradiction in B's case: B needed AI errors to be simultaneously massive in scale (hitting thousands) and chronically undetected in neglected static systems, but an error reaching thousands generates the complaint spikes and escalation data that create the financial incentive to patch it fast. B's response—distinguishing silent errors from escalation-triggering ones—was logically fair but conceded the broader detection advantage and never resolved why cost-optimized companies would ignore the obvious revenue signal that scaling itself provides.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins because it kept the comparison anchored to most first-line service: routine, repetitive, high-volume work where labor savings, speed, availability, and consistency plausibly dominate. Side B raised real concerns about loops, discretion, and hidden churn, but relied more on worst-case deployment assumptions and late unsupported precision about implementation costs and repeat purchase losses. A gave the cleaner weighing mechanism: recurring labor savings and scalable correction versus speculative downstream harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's most decisive exchange was in Rebuttal 2, where it argued that B needed AI failures to be both massively scaled and chronically unnoticed or unmaintained. B's silent-error answer blunted this somewhat, but its added numerical cost claims were insufficiently grounded.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): B won by exposing A's core contradiction (AI errors must be both massively scaled and chronically undetected/neglected) and directly answering pressure questions in Rebuttal 2; A relied on idealized humans and introduced ungrounded specifics without neutralizing the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers plus contradiction point on scale vs neglect.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side B wins on the decisive accountability clash and the measurement-asymmetry framing. The sharpest turn in the debate is B4.3: PRO's own Rebuttal 2 conceded that AI cannot provide accountability at first contact and requires humans with pre-authorized correction authority behind it—which CON correctly identifies as retreating from the original "strictly better" accountability claim and confirming that the structural absence of authority at first contact is real, not a fringe edge case. Side A never squarely recovered from this concession. B's pressure questions were also superior—Q1 exposed a genuine tension between the 70% routine claim and the need for dynamic routing, and Q2 forced PRO to specify who actually holds corrective authority. A's pressure questions were partially disguised mini-arguments and the Q1 assertion about "mandatory human ticket ownership" was an undefended factual claim rather than a genuine question. B's closing crystallized the decisive weighing insight: the motion asks whether costs fall more than quality degrades, not whether costs fall more than quality is easy to measure on a dashboard. This reframe did real work. Side A's execution had several costs: incorrect reference IDs in Rebuttal 1 ([C1.4] instead of [B1.4]), a clipped Rebuttal 2, and a thin closing (169 words, under cap) that simply restated earlier claims without answering live pressure. Side A's core cost-savings argument is real and was never fully destroyed—the motion's burden is comparative, and PRO's efficiency numbers are plausible—but Side B's analytical precision, memorable framing ("forensic, not restorative," "wrong ruler," "crack in a foundation"), and consistent pressure on the structural accountability gap carried the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3 is the most decisive exchange: Side B catches that PRO's own Rebuttal 2 implicitly abandoned the "strictly better accountability" claim by conceding AI requires humans with pre-authorized correction authority behind it—confirming that the structural absence of authority at first contact is exactly the degradation the motion asks us to weigh. PRO never answered this turn.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): B won by making the degradation side of the motion more structurally important rather than merely anecdotal. A had a plausible cost-saving story, but leaned on unsupported precise numbers, idealized implementation, and late claims about mandatory ownership, instant flags, and reinvestment. B cleanly reframed logs as documentation rather than accountability, showed that routing failures matter most when trust is tested, and exposed A's reinvestment and seamless escalation claims as assumptions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was the response that timestamped logs are forensic, not restorative: they may document an AI failure but do not provide judgment or authority at first contact. That directly undercut A's claim that AI is strictly better for accountability.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): A wins by exposing structural accountability gaps and back-loaded trust erosion that B never neutralized, while forcing B to retreat from universal claims to narrow mitigations. B's cost savings were concrete but failed to outweigh the measurement-bias clash A crystallized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's [A4.3] forcing B to concede AI needs human authority behind it, directly damaging B's "strictly better" accountability route.
