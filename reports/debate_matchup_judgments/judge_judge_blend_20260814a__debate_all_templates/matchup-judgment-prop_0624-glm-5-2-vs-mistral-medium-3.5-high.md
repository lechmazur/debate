### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) ran a structurally and substantively superior debate across every round. PRO's opening was nearly twice as long as CON's and established the core framing—floor not ceiling, reimbursement as distinct from regulatory approval, and public vs. private accountability—with clarity and concrete stakes. CON's opening was underweight and left several arguments underdeveloped. In Rebuttal 1, PRO systematically turned CON's key examples: the diabetic retinopathy case was reframed as evidence for benchmarks (patients became the test population), the COVID-19 drift concern was answered by noting public reporting enables ongoing scrutiny, and the Goodhart gaming objection was inverted—gaming a public metric is visible and contestable, gaming an invisible one is not. CON's Rebuttal 1 largely repeated the opening with minor rewording and failed to engage PRO's specific counters. PRO's pressure question Q1—"name one minimum threshold CON would accept"—was devastating. CON's Rebuttal 2 answered with context-dependence, which is theoretically interesting but collapsed as a rebuttal because PRO had already implied context-specific benchmarks satisfy the proposition. PRO exploited this non-answer effectively in both Rebuttal 2 and the closing. The rural ER argument, CON's strongest rhetorical card, was consistently turned by PRO: overwhelmed rural settings are precisely where silent bias causes undetectable harm, and a model meeting even a minimum bar is still available under the proposition. CON never answered this turn. PRO's closing crystallized the live clash cleanly without introducing new material. CON's closing remained emotional but did not resolve the core unmet burden. The margin reflects a clear win for PRO rather than a dominant one because CON's context-dependence and Goodhart arguments had real theoretical weight even if they were not developed into decisive rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question Q1 and its exploitation in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange. By asking CON to name a single acceptable accountability gate, PRO exposed that the objection was not to unrealistic benchmarks but to any benchmark at all. CON's answer—that clinical performance is context-dependent—was then cleanly reframed by PRO: context-dependence argues for context-specific benchmarks, not for no benchmarks. This exchange shifted the entire framing of the debate and CON never recovered from it.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the motion centered on minimum publicly reported accountability, not perfection. Side B had plausible concerns about gaming, delay, and context-specific deployment, but repeatedly overstated the proposition as requiring rigid, universal, perfect benchmarks and never showed why context-specific or ongoing public benchmarks could not satisfy the motion. A directly answered redundancy and gaming, separated FDA approval from reimbursement, and turned the rural ER example by arguing vulnerable settings most need visible bias checks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response that benchmarks are a floor rather than perfection, combined with the pressure that Side B could not name any acceptable accountability gate, most damaged CON’s route to victory.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO successfully framed the debate around whether any accountability gate is acceptable, and CON’s inability to name even one threshold—retreating instead to context-dependence that undermines all pre-reimbursement standards—left CON without a coherent limiting principle. PRO cleanly distinguished FDA clearance from reimbursement, turned CON’s diabetic retinopathy example into evidence for early transparency, and showed that invisible bias in vulnerable settings is worse than delayed deployment. CON’s rural ER urgency and gaming concerns had force, but they were progressively neutralized by PRO’s argument that public, independently verifiable benchmarks are detectable, adaptable, and already standard for other reimbursed technologies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s pressure question demanding CON name a single acceptable threshold, followed by PRO’s Rebuttal 2 exposure that CON deflected to context-dependence rather than offer any floor, crystallized the clash and revealed CON’s position opposed any pre-reimbursement gate—not merely an unrealistic one.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the cleaner path to victory across the full exchange. Its most durable move was identifying early and pressing consistently that PRO kept rewriting the proposition: the "appropriate benchmarks" qualifier, the "continuous evaluation pipelines," and the "staffing reforms" alternative all import language and mechanisms absent from the motion's actual "only if" clause. PRO never adequately answered this, instead defending a more flexible version of the proposition that does not exist in the text. CON's second decisive move was turning PRO's principal evidence—the triage algorithm bias—by noting the harm was caught through post-deployment analysis of real clinical data, not a pre-deployment public benchmark gate, which is precisely the mechanism PRO dismisses as inadequate. PRO's A1 response to the versioning lag question ("continuous evaluation pipelines") was creative but invented policy not in the motion and actually reinforced CON's medical-device analogy point. PRO's A2 response to the burnout question ("addressable via staffing reforms") was correctly labeled utopian deflection and never recovered. PRO's closing failed to engage these live charges, relying instead on asserting that benchmarks "would have" caught the bias before deployment—a speculative claim CON had already refuted. PRO had a genuinely grounded opening and sharp pressure questions (especially Q2 on pre-deployment harm), but the pattern of defending an amended rather than actual proposition was the decisive execution cost throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 was the pivotal exchange: CON caught PRO quietly inserting a proportionality qualifier ("appropriate") absent from the motion, fatally undermined the clinical trials analogy by noting drugs do not retrain weekly while AI does, showed the "stronger benchmarks" fix worsens the lag problem rather than solving it, and turned PRO's signature bias example by pointing out it was surfaced by post-deployment analysis—the very mechanism PRO calls inadequate—rather than any pre-deployment benchmark gate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B won by making the proposition’s “only if” condition look overbroad and operationally brittle. Side A’s safety and bias case was plausible and grounded in a real type of healthcare failure, but it often assumed that public benchmarks would be appropriately tiered, continuously updated, and pre-deployment effective without those mechanisms being in the motion. Side B more consistently weighed the tradeoff: delay of useful documentation tools, benchmark gaming, and model-iteration lag versus uncertain added protection beyond existing validation and monitoring.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s decisive exchange was its answer to Side A’s fixes: tiered benchmarks and continuous evaluation were framed as unwritten amendments, while stronger public benchmarks were shown to worsen the lag problem Side A needed to solve.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by holding PRO to the actual text of the motion and turning PRO's central example against them. CON repeatedly demonstrated that the proposition's "only if" creates a rigid, uniform gate with no risk-tiering—textual flexibility that PRO kept trying to import through invented mechanisms like "appropriate" benchmarks and continuous evaluation pipelines. CON's most decisive blow was showing that the triage algorithm PRO cited as a horror story was actually caught by post-deployment analysis, undermining the claim that a pre-deployment benchmark gate would have uniquely prevented harm. Meanwhile, CON's structural argument—that benchmark validation lag cannot keep pace with weekly model iteration—was never resolved by PRO without unsupported policy invention. CON also effectively established that the proposition creates measurable ongoing harms (blocked documentation tools, continued burnout errors) in exchange for theoretical protection. PRO's closing merely repeated the triage example and dismissed CON's harms as speculative without answering the post-deployment turn, cementing a solid CON victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal turn [A2.1-A2.6] was the decisive exchange: it exposed that PRO was rewriting the motion by adding "appropriate" qualifiers the text lacks, demonstrated that the clinical trials analogy backfires because AI iterates while drugs are static, and most damagingly turned PRO's triage algorithm example by showing it was identified through post-deployment correction—proving the mechanism PRO dismissed actually works.
