### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but Side B held a persistent edge on the decisive clash. PRO's sequencing strategy depended on manufacturing revenue funding a later clean transition, but CON repeatedly demonstrated that Scope 2 mandates from private buyers are already active procurement gates—not merely future CBAM threats—meaning the export revenue that funds PRO's transition may never materialize. PRO's answer (CBAM exemptions for developing nations) did not address private brand audits, and CON's counter-example of Vietnam losing orders to Indonesia's renewable industrial zones was never adequately neutralized. CON's pressure question about the absence of a statutory retirement deadline was genuinely damaging: PRO's response offered a "dual binding framework" of CBAM deadlines and a domestic revenue threshold, but neither was concretely defined, leaving CON's path-dependency argument live. PRO's strongest unanswered point—what sector absorbs 10 million low-skilled annual workforce entrants if not manufacturing?—kept real pressure on CON throughout, and CON's "renewables create more jobs" counter was partially deflated by PRO's distinction between temporary construction work and stable middle-income positions. But this advantage was insufficient to offset PRO's failure to resolve the live market-access problem. CON's closing line—"you cannot fund a transition with goods no one will buy"—crystallized the core issue cleanly. PRO's Rebuttal 2 was also clipped, a minor execution cost. On balance, CON's case left more of its routes to victory intact after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.2 and B4.2 sequence—turning PRO's Vietnam example against itself (Vietnam now losing orders to Indonesia's renewable zones) and pressing that global Scope 2 mandates operate regardless of CBAM phase-ins—was the most decisive exchange. PRO introduced Vietnam as proof that coal-supported manufacturing works, and CON converted it into evidence that coal-linked supply chains are already losing contracts. PRO never squarely answered the private-procurement dimension, which left CON's market-access argument live through the closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively severing Side B's core link between delayed coal retirement and manufacturing export revenue. By proving that private procurement (Scope 2 mandates) and energy-intensive finishing processes bypass CBAM phase-ins, Side A showed that coal-linked goods will lose their markets immediately, collapsing Side B's sequencing strategy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal successfully dismantled Side B's "low-embodied-carbon" defense by pointing out that global brands audit Scope 2 emissions now and that textile dyeing/finishing are energy-intensive, proving contract losses are an immediate threat rather than a future one.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): Side B wins by showing coal ties risk export lockout via CBAM and Scope 2 buyer rules, making promised manufacturing growth illusory before revenues materialize; this undercuts A's sequencing premise more than A's rebuttals neutralize it. A's demographic urgency is solid but weakened by path-dependency and ongoing-cost attacks. A's clipped R2 adds execution cost. B's responses to pressure questions were direct and damaging.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1/A2 answers on path dependency turning "limited delay" into lock-in and immediate buyer audits canceling contracts, exposing how A's triggers fail to protect market access.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A (PRO) built the stronger cumulative case across the exchange. Its most decisive moves came in Rebuttal 2: the distinction between sunk, already-financed state coal assets and greenfield coal construction cleanly neutralized CON's capital-flight argument, which had been framed as if it applied equally to both. The domestic-market reframe (1.4 billion consumers providing absorption capacity for textiles and assembly regardless of export tariff timing) was a genuine new angle that partially deflated CON's best structural argument—the factory-build-cycle timing point—without abandoning the original thesis. PRO also answered the pressure questions more directly than CON did: CON's A2 ("parallel scaling") never addressed the decade-long gap concretely and functioned as reassertion rather than answer, a real execution cost. CON's strongest moments were the factory-build-cycle timing argument (genuinely clever and underrebutted) and the health-cost-hits-the-same-youth point, but CON never pinned down how green supply-chain roles absorb millions of semi-skilled monthly entrants at scale, leaving PRO's Q1 pressure largely live. CON's closing was only 150 words and largely recapped rather than crystallized, while PRO's closing sharpened the comparative cleanly. The sodium-ion storage claim by CON was also somewhat speculative for India's required gigawatt-scale industrial loads. On balance PRO held a clear but competitive edge in rebuttal quality, directness under pressure, and resolution of the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 in Rebuttal 2, distinguishing existing depreciated state-owned coal infrastructure (which requires only operational budgets already on government balance sheets) from greenfield coal construction (to which global lender exit actually applies). This was a sharp, clean move that directly answered CON's financing pressure question and materially damaged one of CON's strongest structural arguments, which had been conflating the two categories throughout the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): Side B won by decisively neutralizing Side A's main offensive routes regarding carbon border tariffs and capital flight. B successfully distinguished between greenfield CAPEX and sunk asset OPEX, and leveraged India's domestic market to blunt the export tariff threat. Side A failed to adapt to these responses, repeating their initial claims in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answers in Rebuttal 2 expertly dismantled the threats of capital flight and carbon tariffs by differentiating between new project finance and domestic OPEX, and introducing the 1.4 billion domestic consumer market.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by maintaining focus on immediate demographic labor absorption and the immaturity of renewables for industrial baseload, directly addressing pressure questions with domestic markets and sunk-asset financing while B's tariff and green-chain arguments were countered without evasion. B offered solid long-term risks but left scalability gaps unclosed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A1/A2 answers to B's tariff and capital questions, using transition exemptions, domestic demand, and existing finance for sunk plants to neutralize B's stranding claims.
