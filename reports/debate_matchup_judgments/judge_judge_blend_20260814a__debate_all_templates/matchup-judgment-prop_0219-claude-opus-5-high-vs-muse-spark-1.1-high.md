### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by turning Side B's own examples into concessions that transparency raises low-information workers' asks and can expose incumbent underpayment. A also showed that transparency may improve matching by filtering out pay-incompatible applications, while B asserted large economy-wide matching losses without adequately establishing their net magnitude or inevitability. B's within-range discretion objection remained meaningful, but it limited rather than eliminated A's equity mechanism. A's clipped opening and occasional overstatement narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to B's 85-to-95 and later 85-to-90 examples was decisive: even B's attempted defense conceded upward movement for the disadvantaged candidate. B showed that gaps could persist within ranges, but not that this partial gap reduction was outweighed by its more speculative matching harms.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by brilliantly reframing Side A's efficiency harms as either the equity mechanism working exactly as intended (underpaid workers realizing their market value and leaving) or avoidable choices by firms trying to protect secrecy. Side B effectively used Side A's own concessions to prove an arithmetically guaranteed reduction in the pay gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal (B4.3) effectively neutralized A's argument about destroyed firm-specific capital by pointing out that if the capital was truly valuable, the old employer would match the new offer; if they don't, the worker was simply mispriced.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): A won by making the comparative trade clearer and cleaner: information automatically raises low asks and corrects underpayment, while B's efficiency harms are distributed, firm-chosen, and transitional. B's "range is not a wage" line was consistent and limited the equity claim, but A repeatedly converted B's own numbers and turnover examples into gap-reduction rather than pure matching loss, and exposed B's ledger asymmetry (employer replacement costs counted; decade of underpay not). The path to victory stayed live for A after pressure answers on bargaining power and metrics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's conversion of B's 85-to-95/90 concession and turnover examples into floor-binding equity gains rather than compliance theater or matching damage (A2.1–A2.4 / A4.1–A4.4).

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that the equity mechanism covers only posted external hiring while the mandate can immediately shift jobs toward closed networks and constrain exceptional offers. A persuasively defended anchoring and screening benefits, but largely characterized channel migration as discriminatory rather than proving its matching harm would be smaller than the pay-gap benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was that quiet referral searches have no mandated range, so A could not claim transparency neutralizes insider advantage there. B then showed that employers may use referrals for legitimate matching reasons, undermining A's attempt to dismiss the resulting exclusion as merely a discrimination rent.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully turning Side B's own causal mechanism against them and dominating the comparative weighing. Side A argued that mandating ranges on public postings drives employers into the very exclusionary informal networks Side B warned about. Side A's disciplined focus on the 'more than' comparative burden and their dismantling of Side B's intent-based defenses made their case highly persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's claim that channel migration is just employers paying "discrimination rent." Side A cleanly pointed out that regardless of the employer's motive, the result is identical: hiring migrates to closed networks, harming the exact candidates Side B wants to protect.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): B won the comparative “more than” ledger. PRO had a clean anchor-and-compounding story and sharp pressure, but never established magnitude for the equity side and never fully neutralized channel migration or the broken-for-harm/efficient-for-remedy dilemma. CON made timing (slow cohort flow vs immediate distortion), thin-slice coverage, and referral flight decisively easier to weigh, so the efficiency cost outranked the unmeasured gap reduction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s competition dilemma (market can’t be both already disciplining pay and systematically lowballing under opacity) plus channel-migration flip of A’s own insider-network mechanism.
