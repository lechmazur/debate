### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built a coherent principal-agent framework (state designed and legislated assimilation; churches were contracted, supervised operators) and paired it with a concrete institutional-structure argument (church assets are purpose-restricted charitable trusts, and the orders that actually ran the schools are often small and now defunct). This gave CON two live, mutually reinforcing routes: a moral/causal claim about who bears ultimate responsibility, and a practical claim about who can actually be made to pay without collateral harm. PRO's opening and first rebuttal were strong on moral urgency and "continuity" logic, but under pressure PRO never supplied a binding mechanism to stop states from offsetting their own liability against church contributions (raised sharply in A3.1, never actually rebutted beyond asserting funds would be "additive"). More damagingly, when pressed on whose assets would be seized (A3.2), PRO pivoted from "the orders that administered the abuse" to "central diocesan reserves and national portfolios" — entities CON had already noted didn't run the schools. CON explicitly caught this inconsistency in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, and PRO's own closing did not resolve it, instead restating the continuity theme. That unanswered shift is the decisive break in the debate: PRO's liability theory (direct operational agency) and PRO's payment mechanism (central wealthy institutions) target different entities, undermining the core justification. CON's cleaner, more internally consistent case and sharper exploitation of that gap earns a clear, solid win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4), pointing out that PRO's answer to the asset-seizure question (B4.2) quietly shifted the target from the small operating orders (whose direct agency justified liability) to central dioceses that did not run the schools — an unresolved contradiction PRO never repaired in its closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won by making the decisive clash clearer: principal authority and fiscal reality beat PRO's co-perpetrator continuity frame. CON showed joint liability is zero-sum in practice (Canada-style offsets), that purpose-restricted charitable assets are not liquid corporate windfalls, and that PRO's "additive trusts" offer aspiration without a binding stop to state free-riding. PRO retained moral force on operational administration and selective continuity, but never neutralized the mechanism gap or the vicarious reach past elderly operating congregations to present dioceses and Indigenous parishes. CON's closings crystallized the cleaner vote reason: accountability that matches authority, with the state holding tax base and land.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 answers (A1/A2) plus [A4.3]-[A4.4]: individual-cruelty liability ≠ collective historical levy, and "additive/fixed" is unanswered aspiration contradicted by real offset patterns, while central-portfolio targeting is vicarious liability past actual operators.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won the practical weighing of the debate. While Side A presented a morally compelling case for institutional accountability based on continuity and direct operational harm, Side B successfully demonstrated that forcing churches to pay structurally lets the state off the hook (the offset problem) and requires liquidating purpose-restricted charitable trusts or imposing vicarious liability on dioceses that did not directly operate the schools. Side A's assertion that funds could be "strictly additive" lacked a binding mechanism against political reality, leaving Side B with the cleaner, more realistic path to actual reparative justice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's "additive funds" claim in Rebuttal 2, pointing out that fiscal history (like Canada's settlement) proves governments will simply cap their own exposure when churches contribute, effectively neutralizing Side A's defense against the zero-sum offset critique.

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a coherent joint-liability framework (state owes 100% immediately, churches settle their share, courts can ring-fence essential charity from historic capital) and used it consistently across rounds to answer CON's core "sovereign non-delegable duty" claim. CON's case remained principled and disciplined but grew repetitive and left the land-return contradiction unresolved, costing it the decisive exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure question in [B3.2]/[B4.5]—forcing CON to admit that returning land worth millions is itself a "financial contribution," undercutting CON's claim that only non-monetary redress is appropriate—was the most damaging unanswered pressure point in the debate.

- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built a much stronger comparative case on liability and practical implementation. They effectively dismantled Side B's sovereign immunity argument by highlighting the churches' daily central agency and historical enrichment. Side A also brilliantly trapped Side B in the pressure questions regarding land repatriation, exposing the contradiction in Side B's refusal of financial contributions while supporting massive material asset transfers. Side A's closing crystallized this clash perfectly, making their route to victory decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's pressure questions, specifically A1 and A2, where they clarified that joint liability doesn't delay state payment and exposed the material equivalence of Side B's proposed land returns.
