### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by separating access from remedy and showing that a fast human answer does not itself guarantee cancellation, charge suspension, or safety escalation. Its customer-declaration trigger and legally enforceable acceptance duty directly answered Side B's strongest recognition argument. Side B persuasively defended humans as an adaptive backup, but never fully resolved A's concerns about limited authority, scripted agents, queue gaming, and the mandate merely accelerating referral or refusal. A's repeated clipping and a few overconfident claims narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B argued that every remedy needs a human to recognize and trigger it; Side A answered that the customer's written declaration can itself trigger a legal duty, producing an auditable record without classification by either a chatbot or frontline agent. That response substantially undercut the claimed independent function of the human fast path.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON wins by showing the fast path is either redundant or counterproductive. The decisive turn is CON's alternative framework: regulate what must be accepted rather than who answers. When PRO argues that human recognition is the unique value of a fast path, CON effectively answers that customer declarations can trigger legal duties directly, like card chargebacks, bypassing the need for bot or human classification. CON also lands the comparative point that a speed mandate manufactures cheap, scripted agents who lack the discretion PRO claims is necessary, while outcome regulation binds every channel simultaneously. PRO's closing about the trigger is sharp, but CON's closing neutralizes it with the duty-to-accept distinction and the concrete example of app-based dispute submission where no human is needed to start the clock.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8), where CON answers PRO's pressure questions by showing that customer declarations—not human recognition—can trigger remedies via the chargeback model, reducing PRO's claimed independent work to mere routing that forms perform better, and demonstrating that a speed mandate would mass-produce the exact low-discretion agents PRO criticizes.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's reliance on human agents. B brilliantly reframed the debate from channel regulation to outcome regulation, pointing out that human agents are often the retention trap themselves and that speed mandates incentivize companies to hire cheaper, heavily scripted workers with less discretion. When A retreated to arguing that a human is necessary as a "trigger" for outcome rules, B neutralized this with the chargeback analogy, showing that a customer's legal declaration in any channel starts the clock without needing bot classification or human recognition. B's logical progression, sharp rebuttals, and memorable rhetoric made the comparative case decisively clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it used the credit card chargeback model to prove that a customer's own declaration can trigger an automatic suspension and legal clock across any channel, rendering Side A's "human as trigger" argument obsolete and exposing the flaws in mandating answer speed.
