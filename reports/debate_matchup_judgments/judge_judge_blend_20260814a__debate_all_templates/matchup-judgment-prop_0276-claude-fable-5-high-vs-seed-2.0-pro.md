### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won clearly. A kept the debate centered on the motion: moving controllable-delay compensation from complaint friction to default accountability. B’s main objection depended on a narrow assumption that automatic compensation must lock instantly and cannot scale with the passenger’s actual delay after rebooking. A repeatedly and persuasively showed that this is not required by the motion and that an automatic meter stopping when the passenger is delivered preserves automaticity while improving incentives. B’s threshold and fare claims were less grounded, sometimes overly specific, and the late “bargaining chip” framing mostly turned into an indictment of the status quo rather than a defense of it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s decisive exchange was the answer that automatic compensation can scale with actual delay and include cancellations, which undercut both B’s threshold-gaming story and the claim that airlines would lose all incentive to rebook passengers.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won decisively by trapping Side B in a contradiction regarding airline rebooking incentives. A successfully framed B's defense of the status quo—that passengers can waive their rights in exchange for a flight—as airlines holding journeys hostage for ransom.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing perfectly weaponized Side B's 'bargaining chip' concession, pointing out that defending the status quo means defending a system where airlines ransom a passenger's legal rights for a rebooking.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by showing that automatic compensation can take the form of a running meter that preserves airline incentives to rebook quickly, rather than the rigid irrevocable trigger B assumed. A exposed a damaging contradiction in B's case—admitting airlines already refuse to rebook today while claiming automaticity would destroy a helpful culture—and reframed B's bargaining chip as a ransom of passengers' own rights. B's arithmetic objection never recovered from A's demonstration that the motion does not require an instant locked liability, and A's European example survived as plausible real-world support while B's predictions remained speculative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (A4) was decisive: it deployed the speed-camera analogy to neutralize threshold-gaming, used B's own admission that airlines already refuse rebooking to undermine B's horror story, and introduced the running-meter model that answered B's core incentive objection without widening the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won by making the central problem automatic classification: if airlines are incentivized to avoid payouts, letting them code controllability upstream makes abuse harder to see. A had a strong fairness/default-burden case, but its answer required notices and appeals, allowing B to argue A had rebuilt a claims process while keeping the weakest part of automaticity. B also answered the politics and dishonesty questions more directly and gave the cleaner final comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that A’s distrust of airline claim handling applies even more strongly to private delay-coding was the decisive exchange, especially after A conceded passengers would need classification notices and appeals.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing a structural flaw in Side A's mechanism and trapping Side A into conceding a claims process. Side B persuasively argued that making payouts automatic for 'controllable' delays incentivizes airlines to code delays as 'uncontrollable' upstream. When Side A attempted to solve this by adding an appeal process, Side B effectively pointed out that Side A had abandoned the motion's core premise of avoiding complaint processes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which pointed out that Side A's proposed 'notification and 14-day appeal window' simply reconstructed a complaint process but gave the airline the advantage of a default 'uncontrollable' judgment.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing that PRO's "automatic" compensation collapses into a claims process with worse properties. When pressed, PRO conceded passengers need classification notices and a 14-day appeal window, which CON seized to show the burden was never flipped—it was buried in an invisible upstream coding step. CON's argument that airlines will game self-interested delay classifications in the dark, while at least CON's visible denials carry statutory penalties and audit trails, remained unanswered. PRO's acceptance that marginal routes to small cities would disappear also functioned as a damaging concession. CON did better comparative weighing, sharper pressure, and cleaner progression across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (Turn 8), where CON demonstrated that PRO's answer to the pressure questions effectively rebuilt a claims process, abandoned the core "automaticity" of the motion, and left passengers appealing against invisible airline self-classifications rather than a visible denial system.
