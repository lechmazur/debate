### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON built and sustained a genuine dilemma: either the required "complete" history is only useful as broad, actionable patterns (in which case sampled audits/compelled discovery already achieve accountability and completeness is surplus), or it truly must include withheld-block sizes, queue-segment access, and full sale paths (in which case it functions as a harvest map for professional resellers/bots). CON pressed this fork sharply in Rebuttal 1 and via a pointed pressure question asking PRO to name any listed operational fact sellers could still withhold. PRO's answer (R2, A2) conceded that withheld seats, price changes, and queue-segment access must all be disclosed, only exempting future plans and security internals — which is precisely the granular historical data CON identified as the harvest layer, not a resolution of the tension. CON exploited this concession cleanly in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, landing the most memorable line of the debate ("If only broad patterns matter, completeness is surplus. If completeness is required, it is because that harvest layer matters."). PRO's closing reasserted structural asymmetry and accountability values but did not directly dismantle this trap, largely repackaging earlier claims. PRO's opening was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost. Both sides argued with discipline and without fake precision, but CON's tighter logical throughline, sharper cross-examination, and cleaner final framing make it the stronger case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 fork ("PRO cannot keep both halves of its story...") combined with the pressure question demanding a specific undisclosable fact, which forced PRO's Rebuttal 2 concession that granular historical data (withheld blocks, queue-segment access) must be disclosed — validating rather than escaping CON's harvest-layer objection.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully weaponizing the word 'complete' in the resolution, arguing that while accountability is necessary, a 'complete' public dump provides the exact granular data scalpers need for multi-date tours. Side B's argument that confidential audits solve the accountability problem without handing over the 'harvest layer' to bots was decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 expertly deployed a dilemma: if consumers only need broad patterns from the data, 'completeness' is surplus; if 'completeness' is required, it forces the release of the exact granular data scalpers monetize.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): PRO wins because its accountability and deterrence framework survives CON’s sharpest attacks. While CON’s “fork” about predictive value created real pressure, PRO adequately distinguished between broad historical patterns useful for consumer choice and verification versus granular future tactics that sellers can vary. CON’s alternative of confidential audits and targeted enforcement was undermined by PRO’s showing that invisible conduct cannot generate complaints or reliable targeting, leaving secrecy as a shield for unaccountable discretion. CON’s reseller harm remained speculative and was mitigated by PRO’s arguments for delayed release and operational variation. PRO crystallized the decisive clash better: yesterday’s market conduct should be verifiable even if tomorrow’s defenses remain secret.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s initiation of the predictive-value fork in Rebuttal 1 (B2.1-B2.2) and PRO’s subsequent defense via the broad-pattern versus exact-tactic distinction in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1, A4.3), which became the central axis of the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): The decisive clash was whether a "complete" history that excludes account-level/decision data can actually verify the claims PRO relies on (queue-jumping, personalization, manufactured scarcity). PRO's move to redefine "complete" as an aggregate, de-identified tape of prices/inventory/queue movement was a clever dodge of CON's privacy/security attack, but it left PRO exposed to CON's sharpest point: such a tape cannot distinguish manipulation from innocuous causes (bot removals, expired sessions, released holds, failed payments), so it "shows up" claims (B4.1-4.2) remain assertions, not proof. CON pressed this exact dilemma in the pressure round and again in Rebuttal 2/Closing ("the same chart supports incompatible stories"), and PRO's closing never squarely answered it, instead re-asserting that the tape "answers" the question. CON's confidential-audit alternative also has unaddressed weaknesses (regulatory capture, no guarantee of proactive review), but PRO never pressed that gap decisively. CON's case suffered a real execution cost from two clipped speeches (opening and Rebuttal 1) for exceeding word limits, which is a genuine discipline problem, but the core dilemma CON built survived cleanly into the final exchange and was never neutralized, giving CON the cleaner path to victory on the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.5-A4.6), which showed that PRO's "shows up" evidence (queue-jumping, personalized prices, pulsed inventory) is equally consistent with innocent explanations, and that PRO's fallback to "coded labels" either collapses into CON's targeted-disclosure model or fails to verify anything—an attack PRO's closing did not directly rebut.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully proving that the public disclosure Side A proposed would either be dangerously detailed or uselessly ambiguous. Side B's argument that "seeing a pattern is not verifying its cause" effectively neutralized Side A's claim that a stripped tape would provide public accountability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal pointed out that Side A's "coded labels" solution would require the public to blindly trust the platform's narration, perfectly turning Side A's own opening premise against them.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A won the central definitional fight by showing that "complete queueing and pricing histories" means exhaustive market-level records (inventory releases, prices, queue movement), not the operational dossiers Side B used to stoke privacy and security fears. By framing B's case as a bait-and-switch, PRO dissolved the false dilemma between dangerous completeness and useless incompleteness. CON's confidential-audit alternative remained plausible but never fully answered PRO's argument that the party controlling the sale cannot also control the only record of what happened, leaving captive buyers with an unfalsifiable narrative. CON's clipped speeches added a small execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal (A2), which exposed CON's inflation of "complete" to include personal account paths, device signals, and fraud internals—demonstrating that the motion's actual requirements (de-identified queue and price data) avoid privacy harms while still enabling public accountability.
