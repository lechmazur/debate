### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO built a more durable case across the full exchange. The opening addressed CON's objections proactively, turning the background-check and work-requirement language of the proposition itself into a rebuttal. The decisive comparative pressure came from PRO's repeated, unanswered challenge: the status quo deterrence-only approach has already produced the shadow population under debate. CON's answer—that "mixed signals," not deterrence itself, created the crisis—is plausible but speculative and self-serving; it asserts that truly consistent enforcement would eventually work without explaining how that transition occurs or why decades of the current regime haven't produced it. PRO's reframing in Rebuttal 2 was effective: the real choice is "pathway + whatever enforcement exists" versus "no pathway + whatever enforcement exists," which neutralized CON's core claim that the pathway worsens deterrence. PRO also won the rule-of-law clash in substance: maintaining millions permanently beyond the reach of labor, tax, and criminal law is not preserving a legal boundary—it is abandoning jurisdiction. CON's "law is a boundary, not a pressure valve" line is rhetorically strong but practically hollow when the breach already encompasses millions and CON offers no achievable route to remedy it. CON held its own on verification (mass adjudication pressure is real) and fiscal liabilities (chain migration, benefits eligibility), but these were partially answered and never framed as decisive compared to the status quo failure. PRO's closing crystallized the governed-vs.-ungoverned choice cleanly. CON's closing was crisp but amounted to restating earlier positions without closing the gap on the track-record problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 turning the rule-of-law argument: pointing out that a population permanently beyond the reach of labor law, tax law, and criminal accountability is itself a rule-of-law failure, and that CON's alternative of tolerating non-compliance indefinitely is institutionalized lawlessness rather than principled enforcement. CON never fully answered why an unenforceable line that millions have already crossed constitutes meaningful legal order.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A wins by systematically dismantling Side B's claim that legalization restores the rule of law. A successfully frames the pathway as "capitulation to scale" and effectively uses the "mixed signals" argument to prove that amnesties cause the very shadow populations Side B decries. Furthermore, Side B drops the structural fiscal costs (welfare and chain migration) after Side A's sharp first rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralizes Side B's strongest pressure question by arguing that the current shadow population isn't proof deterrence failed, but proof that mixed signals and promises of future regularization succeeded.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A wins by clearly showing the status quo already creates ungoverned populations and that the pathway embeds enforcement via criteria while CON's deterrence-only stance has produced the existing problem with no credible fix offered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers: pathway includes built-in enforcement mechanisms and cross-referencing prevents pooled fraud, reframing the choice as status quo failure vs. manageable compliance.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): PRO won primarily because its "zero data vs. some data" framework held throughout the exchange and was never fully neutralized. PRO's central claim—that the status quo deliberately forfeits any security intelligence while the pathway at least subjects the compliant population to biometric and criminal cross-referencing, freeing enforcement resources to focus on those who remain hidden—is more compelling than CON's alternative. CON's "vetting theater" point (high-risk individuals self-select out) is structurally sound and PRO never fully answered why someone with a disqualifying record would voluntarily apply. But PRO's counter—that willful blindness guarantees zero security whereas even partial screening is an improvement, and that resolving the legacy population is the precondition for enforceable forward-looking enforcement—is adequate and was maintained consistently. CON's magnet effect argument persisted but rested on asserted correlation rather than a demonstrated causal mechanism; PRO's "migration tracks immediate labor demand, not speculative future amnesties" was not squarely refuted. CON's attrition model was challenged effectively by PRO's Q1 (rooted families with citizen children will not self-deport), and CON's Great Recession example actually undermined their point—it was an economic contraction, not enforcement-driven, and says little about what consistent E-Verify enforcement alone would achieve. PRO's practical governance frame ("regulated reality vs. unenforceable fantasy") held the cleaner comparative case across all five rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's Q2 combined with CON's A2 response. PRO's question forced CON to concede the pathway generates at least some vetting data. CON's answer—that only the compliant subset applies while the genuinely dangerous self-select out—was the sharpest point CON landed all debate. However, PRO's closing counter (leverage compounds: as the cash economy contracts and legal employment requires verification, staying hidden becomes unsustainable) was sufficient to keep the framework intact, preventing CON's self-selection critique from becoming a decisive turn.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B won by successfully subsuming Side A's core enforcement mechanism. When Side A argued for "attrition through enforcement," Side B effectively countered that a pathway is a necessary political and logistical prerequisite to actually implementing those forward-looking policies (like mandatory E-Verify). Side A failed to engage with this sequencing. Furthermore, Side A's defense against family separation—relying on "judicial discretion in removal proceedings"—undercut its own strict adherence to the rule of law.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal, which argued that clearing the backlog removes the political paralysis preventing mandatory employment verification, directly answering Side A's pressure about stopping the magnet effect.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side B wins by establishing that voluntary vetting leaves high-risk applicants unaddressed and that no mechanism blocks the magnet precedent from past programs, while attrition offers consistent reduction without rewarding violations. Side A's leverage claim is neutralized by self-selection and missing enforcement trigger. Clash progression favors B's consistent weighing of security and fairness over A's practical-governance framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 directly answering pressure questions by exposing vetting self-selection and unrefuted expectation of future pathways.
