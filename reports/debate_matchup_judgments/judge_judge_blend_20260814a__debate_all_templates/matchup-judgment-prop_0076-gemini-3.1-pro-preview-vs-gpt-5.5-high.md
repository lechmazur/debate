### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory by clearly establishing long-term care as an uninsurable social risk that imposes hidden family costs and means-testing cliffs, then directly answering pressure questions on financing and rationing while maintaining a coherent comparison between pre-crisis pooling and post-harm exhaustion. Side B raised serious demographic and induced-demand concerns that landed effectively but left its alternative model exposed on equity and middle-income burdens without fully neutralizing A's equity framing or progressive design option.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2, which begins by labeling A1 and A2 answers to B's pressure questions on waitlists and taxes, then reframes scarcity as better managed under public assessment than private spend-down or family conscription.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO won by establishing that the decisive clash was not whether care is scarce, but who bears the burden of that scarcity. PRO showed that CON's means-tested model forces middle-income families into asset spend-down and unpaid caregiving before the state steps in, effectively making destitution the doorway to help. While CON landed strong points on demographics and induced demand, PRO answered them by noting that workforce scarcity exists under both systems and that a near-universal floor with progressive financing, assessed need, and permitted private top-ups handles scarcity more honestly than hiding it inside family burnout. CON never escaped PRO's pressure question about who carries the load for the not-yet-poor middle-income elder, and its closing image of a fully staffed, instantly accessible safety net contradicted its own warnings about severe workforce limits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 reframing of CON's means-testing as "rationing by exhaustion" rather than avoiding rationing, and its direct answers to CON's pressure questions showing that scarcity is managed, not created, by universal design.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by leveraging the "near-universal" clause to defang CON's regressivity attacks and by decisively reframing the scarcity clash. PRO shows that workforce shortages exist under any model, but CON's means-testing rations by poverty, paperwork, and family exhaustion, while PRO's system rations by assessed clinical need without forcing bankruptcy. CON's "waitlist spend-down" flip was rhetorically sharp, but PRO's causal story about catastrophic, unpredictable LTC risk and the failure of private/family backups remained more robust. PRO also handled pressure questions directly. Repeated clipping of PRO's speeches incurs a minor execution cost, but the comparative advantage remains clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's turn of CON's labor shortage attack in Rebuttal 2 and Closing: noting that means-testing does not create caregivers either, it simply hides rationing inside unpaid family labor and financial collapse ("rationing by exhaustion"). This neutralized CON's main offense and crystallized why PRO's model manages the same real-world constraints more effectively.
