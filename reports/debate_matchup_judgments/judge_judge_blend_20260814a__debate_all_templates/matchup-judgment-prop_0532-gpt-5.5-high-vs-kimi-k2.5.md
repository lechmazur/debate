### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): Side A built the cleaner path to victory across the full exchange. The most decisive structural win came from A's Q1—what is your allocation rule when the legal space is already full?—which forced B to answer with "temporal rotation." But A correctly identified that rotation only helps if turnover happens; during peak demand, when the problem is worst, multiple vehicles compete for the same scarce curb simultaneously and rotation produces no additional supply. B never resolved this. A's reframe of "managed vs. unmanaged scarcity" was accurate and consistently applied: calling free access democratic does not explain how it allocates when demand exceeds supply, and A correctly charged that the "lottery" is actually rationed by circling time, rule-breaking tolerance, and dwell-by-default—costs that fall disproportionately on bus riders, cyclists, and small operators who cannot afford to waste twenty minutes. B's "plutocratic auction" framing was rhetorically effective but never engaged with A's platform-billing mechanism, which shifts the charge to the platform rather than the individual worker. B's equity argument assumed pricing must fall on drivers when A explicitly proposed charging platform accounts. B's infrastructure cost argument landed real pressure, and A's answer—that cities already absorb enforcement, delay, and crash costs without revenue to fix them—was adequate but not fully sharp. The execution cost that hurt B most was Rebuttal 2 running only 166 words against a 340-word cap, leaving several of A's live responses unanswered. B's closing recycled the plutocratic framing without narrowing the dispute further. A's closing crystallized the genuine clash—free curb is not neutral, it is congestion rationed by chaos—with genuine comparative force.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1 pressure question ("when the legal space is full, what is your actual allocation rule besides circling, double-parking, or blocking buses?") and A's subsequent follow-up in Rebuttal 2 identifying that temporal rotation only solves the problem after a vehicle leaves, not while multiple vehicles compete for the same space at peak demand. B's answer defended rotation in principle but could not show it produces turnover precisely when and where demand is highest, leaving A's core congestion argument standing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): Side A won by effectively demonstrating that Side B's alternative—"rotation" and "waiting for a slot"—is practically synonymous with the circling, idling, and congestion that the proposition seeks to solve. Side A successfully framed zero-price curbs as a hidden tax paid in chaos, which neutralized Side B's equity arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing correctly identified that Side B's "rotation" only helps after a vehicle reaches a legal space, leaving the overflow to be paid in circling and blocked buses.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): Side A won by decisively framing the status quo as rationing by chaos that externalizes costs onto vulnerable road users and small operators alike. While Side B raised strong equity and administrative concerns, A effectively mitigated them through platform billing, targeted discounts, and the observation that enforcement costs already exist. Crucially, A's pressure questions exposed a fatal gap in B's alternative: time limits and rotation fail to allocate space when peak demand physically exceeds supply, leaving circling and double-parking as the de facto rationing mechanism. B's reliance on waiting for turnover conceded the very delay costs A highlighted. A provided a clearer, more workable comparative path for managing fixed geometry under dynamic demand.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 directly answering B's pressure questions while dismantling the democratic lottery frame. A showed that zero-price allocation favors rule-breakers and those with time to waste, not the working class, and that posted dynamic rates can be predictable while still managing demand, effectively neutralizing B's core equity and uncertainty offense.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): PRO built and sustained the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. The decisive contribution was the "fatal asymmetry" charge in [A4.3]: CON demands perfect enforcement for physical management while implicitly assuming pricing will fail under those same enforcement conditions. CON's response (B4.1) — that automated systems enforce time limits as readily as fees — is fair but circular. It concedes that the city needs robust automated infrastructure either way, which PRO then converts into the revenue argument: only pricing generates the fund to build the loading zones CON itself admitted are lacking in [B2.2]. That concession was never escaped, and CON's alternative funding mechanisms (permits, development requirements) were offered without any comparative force. PRO's compliance-math argument — that a transparent, certain modest fee makes legal occupancy cheaper than the gamble of a large but uncertain ticket — also partially answered CON's painless/powerful dilemma, though that dilemma remains the strongest unresolved pressure CON landed. PRO's equity reframe was also effective: the status quo already lets large fleets treat circling and fines as overhead, so pricing at minimum levels the field rather than tilting it further toward the well-capitalized. CON's "manage not monetize" framing is rhetorically clean but never closed the enforcement gap or the revenue gap that PRO kept reopening. The two format violations (clipped openings and first rebuttal) cost CON real argumentative completeness, compounding the substantive deficit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's "fatal asymmetry" move in [A4.3], combined with the compliance-math breakdown in [A4.3–A4.4]: if enforcement is too weak to stop camping under current rules, pricing changes the incentive calculus by making legal stopping cheaper than the gamble of an uncertain fine, while CON's alternative still relies on the same enforcement capacity it conceded is currently absent. CON's response that automated cameras can enforce rules equally well concedes the infrastructure point without escaping the behavioral mechanism PRO identified.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by neutralizing Side A's primary mechanism. B successfully argued that the automated technology required for dynamic pricing could be used to enforce direct spatial rules (time limits, designated zones) without regressively taxing access. B also introduced a fatal dilemma in Rebuttal 2—that a fee low enough to be painless will not deter congestion, while a high fee harms small businesses—which severely damaged A's case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange. By attacking A's claim that pricing "distinguishes duration" by pointing out that "duration is not the same as harm," and by presenting the "pennies" dilemma, B cleanly dismantled A's pricing mechanism.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully neutralizing PRO's core mechanism. CON's enforcement symmetry argument—that any sensor or camera network capable of billing dynamic prices can equally enforce time limits, loading zones, and lane protections—undercuts PRO's claim that pricing is uniquely self-enforcing. CON also lands a decisive analytical blow with the duration-versus-harm distinction and the fee-level dilemma: if the price is low enough to be equitable, it will not meaningfully change peak behavior; if high enough to deter congestion, it crushes urgent trips and small-scale users. PRO's compliance math and revenue arguments are coherent but never fully resolve why blunt price signals outperform targeted, automated regulation of obstruction. CON's alternative remains functionally complete, directly addresses the harms PRO cites, and avoids the equity and volatility downsides. B's clipped turns incur a minor execution cost, but the argumentative depth and cleaner resolution of the central clash secure the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 dismantles PRO's pricing mechanism by exposing the fee-level dilemma (painless pricing fails to deter; powerful pricing harms essential users) and clarifying that duration-based pricing cannot distinguish between a short stop that blocks a bus and a longer stop that is functionally necessary, proving targeted rules manage harm more precisely than blunt price signals.
