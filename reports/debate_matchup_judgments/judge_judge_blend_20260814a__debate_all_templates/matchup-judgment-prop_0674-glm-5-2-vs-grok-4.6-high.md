  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the "cheaper deterrence" and "unused stocks" arguments in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, specifically the line that a freeze "does not park Western strength in a neutral garage" but instead trades active deterrence for a failed bet.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing an immediate settlement as a ratification of limited conquest, proving that any 'tripwire' attached to a profitable freeze lacks deterrent credibility. While Side A made a brilliant structural argument comparing the unsustainable daily drain of war to a sustainable, NATO-style conditional commitment, Side B decisively countered that setting this tripwire only after cashing out the aggressor's gains provides a playbook for future aggression rather than a bright line against it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing sequence dismantling Side A's 'settlement with teeth', arguing that automatic responses attached to a locked-in, profitable occupation just create 'a procedure for how to cash out the last war and start the next one.'
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by exposing that sustained aid lacks a defined exit criterion and structurally erodes the Western resolve it depends on, whereas a conditional settlement framework creates sustainable deterrence. While Side B powerfully framed the danger of letting Russia "cash out" limited conquest, Side A's rebuttal that occupation persists under aid either way—and that only a defined legal order with tripwires converts the status quo into credible deterrence—proved more durable after the full exchange. Side B never squarely answered why an undefined "cost threshold" is more sustainable than a conditional commitment, leaving their path to victory dependent on an indefinite war that CON persuasively argued undermines itself.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.8), where CON argued that Moscow keeps territory under both scenarios, so the decisive question is whether the surrounding framework creates a sustainable tripwire or an exhausting open-ended drain, using NATO's conditional-commitment model to show why settlement enforcement is more credible than indefinite aid.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A wins by systematically dismantling Side B's core mechanism of a "settlement with teeth." Side A effectively argues that if the West lacks the resolve to sustain current aid, a paper tripwire over already-conceded territory lacks credibility. Side A's framing of immediate settlement as "cashing out" and converting seizure into a file makes the comparative deterrence calculus clearer and more grounded in adversary psychology. While Side B's distinction between continuous and discrete costs was theoretically sound, it failed to overcome Side A's decisive point that conceding the prize validates the aggression and makes the next map cheaper to draw.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, specifically A2, where they answer pressure by arguing that settling "while resolve still exists" spends remaining credibility on ratifying the seizure, making a future paper threat cheaper for Moscow to ignore than the live signal of current aid.
