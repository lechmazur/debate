### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): This was a genuinely close debate, but CON (A) edges it out. PRO (B) built a strong initial case — the control/dependence framing, the UK Uber ruling and Spain's Riders Law as evidence against "mass exodus" predictions, and a clean multi-employer/barista analogy that neutralized the multi-app objection reasonably well. PRO's closing move — that CON's transaction-floor alternative still relies on the same regulatory enforcement apparatus, just untested — was a sharp and nearly decisive collapse of CON's distinguishing claim. However, CON consistently pre-empted and answered this: attaching obligations to payment-processor records is more auditable than forcing worker-by-worker status litigation, and CON's final closing directly countered "proven vs. untested" by arguing employment law is proven for stable jobs, not fragmented multi-app gig work — a fair rejoinder that kept CON's case alive. CON also handled deactivation, exemption-timing, and informal-economy displacement concerns with concrete, if imperfect, alternative mechanisms (due process rules, per-task levies), while never losing content to the word cap. PRO's opening, rebuttal 1, rebuttal 2, and closing were all clipped for exceeding limits — a real, repeated execution cost that cut off PRO's momentum right where its strongest closing argument was building, denying it a full landing. That asymmetry in execution, combined with CON's slightly more adaptive engagement with the enforcement-mechanism clash, gives CON a modest but real edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The clash over "who actually enforces compliance" — PRO's closing charge that CON's transaction-floor model is just the same enforcement apparatus wearing new vocabulary, versus CON's counter that payment-ledger attachment is more auditable than status litigation and that "proven" employment law doesn't fit fragmented multi-app work — was the most decisive exchange, and CON's answer, though squeezed into a short closing, held up better under scrutiny.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): B won a clear but competitive debate. B showed that platform control, pricing, allocation, and deactivation resemble employment and that a default shifts the evidentiary burden appropriately. A offered a thoughtful transaction-based alternative, but did not establish that its own pay floors and levies would avoid the costs, enforcement demands, or reduced access attributed to employment. B also answered the multi-app and flexibility objections with proportional obligations and flexible employment, though repeated clipping and limited treatment of Asian informality narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was its answer to the multi-app objection: multiple employers with proportional duties are already legally familiar. This weakened A’s claim that fragmented work makes employee status inherently incoherent, while A’s ledger-based alternative could equally help calculate proportional obligations.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): B won a competitive debate by making the cleaner comparative case: employee-default is the wrong instrument because it turns multi-app micro-work into overlapping-liability friction, pressures exclusivity, and risks pushing marginal (especially Asian informal) work off-platform, while a transaction floor can attach pay, appeal, and benefits to visible ledgers. A’s control/burden-shift case and enforcement pressure were strong, and multi-employer analogies were coherent, but A never fully neutralized access-shrinkage or classification-gateway costs; repeated clipping also cost A some late sharpness. B answered pressure more operationally and crystallized enforceability-without-exclusion more decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s multi-app answer: barista-style dual employment fails for fragmented micro-shifts, so proportional employee duties create overlapping liability and exclusivity pressure rather than a clean baseline.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON built the sharper, more damaging line of attack: it pressed PRO on exactly what "employee by default" means under existing law, and PRO's answers progressively retreated into vague promises (open scheduling within employee status, "aligning the law," "phased implementation," locally adjusted benefits) that concede the motion's literal mechanism doesn't match what PRO actually wants to defend. CON's pressure question ("which jurisdiction lets employers pick obligations") was never answered with a real example; PRO instead pivoted to "the correct fix is to align the law," effectively admitting new legislative redesign is needed rather than defending the motion as written. CON's geography point similarly landed: PRO's own language ("phased," "adjusted benefits") undercut its claim that a single default works across radically different labor markets, and CON's closing effectively crystallized this progression of concessions. PRO's opening framework (control creates responsibility) was strong and consistently rhetorically clean, and its closing reasserted the core normative claim well, but it never resolved the mechanism problem CON kept exposing. CON's case suffered an execution cost from being clipped twice for exceeding word limits, which is weighed against it, but the substantive rebuttal work and exploitation of PRO's drifting position was still the more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question asking which jurisdiction allows selective employee obligations, followed by PRO's answer pivoting to "align the law" rather than naming a real mechanism — this exchange best exposed the gap between the motion's literal legal command and PRO's evolving, softened defense of it.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B won by grounding the default in the clearest principle: employer-like control should trigger employer responsibility, while genuinely independent workers remain exempt. Side A raised a serious cost and rigidity objection, but repeatedly assumed the motion freezes every jurisdiction’s existing statutory bundle and overstated that employee status necessarily requires fixed rosters. Its late targeted-benefits alternative was underdeveloped and partly introduced only after pressure. Side B directly answered both questions, distinguished a common classification default from identical national implementation, and better explained why flexibility does not justify externalizing injury and income risk. Side A’s clipped opening and closing were additional execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered that the motion concerns employment status rather than selectively applying duties, noted that variable-hour employment can preserve scheduling flexibility, and showed that locally adjusted benefits do not negate a shared default. This weakened Side A’s central claim that variation across countries amounted to abandoning the proposition.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): B won by keeping the clash on the motion as written: default employee status under real European/Asian law triggers a full costly bundle, not the pick-and-choose minimums-plus-open-scheduling PRO kept promising. A’s control-creates-duty principle landed cleanly and exposed that B’s model still leaves core risk optional, but B forced successive A concessions on redesign, phasing, local adjustment, and legacy categories that made the specific vehicle look mismatched; geography and marginal-access harms stayed live. B answered pressure more sharply and crystallized the sledgehammer vs. scalpel comparison better across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R2 A1/A2 and follow-on: motion delivers the full statutory bundle, not PRO’s idealized protections, turning A’s own “phase/adjust/redesign” answers into admissions the vehicle fails.
