### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won on three live routes that CON never squarely neutralized. First, the historical precedent argument—telecom portability, email federation, banking switching—was sustained across every speech and never effectively answered. CON's response was always structural ("social graphs are different from phone numbers") without ever explaining why the underlying economic logic of network lock-in doesn't apply; the argument that platforms are "curated environments" distinguishes the service layer but not the connectivity layer at stake. Second, PRO's framing of the fundamental asymmetry—"manageable design challenges vs. permanent unaccountable monopoly"—was clean and directly keyed to the motion's "qualified" language, which CON largely accepted as given but treated as a fatal weakness rather than an addressable design feature. Third, PRO's A1/A2 answers to the pressure questions were substantively stronger: the revocation/conditions answer on moderation (platforms retain their own filters; qualified status requires equivalent standards) is more concrete than CON's safety objection allows, and the MVNO/graduated-compliance answer on startup costs directly inverted the cartelization worry. CON's strongest live argument—relational consent, the idea that a social graph maps other people's choices made under specific terms—was genuinely interesting and underresponded to by PRO; PRO's email-contact analogy is imperfect because following is indeed more unilateral than mutual address-sharing. But CON never converted this into a decisive reason to reject the motion entirely rather than to refine its implementation, and PRO correctly pointed out the symmetry flaw: permanent platform hoarding of that same relational data is an even larger consent violation. CON's opening being clipped is a minor execution cost. Overall, PRO made the debate easier to decide by consistently answering the clash and framing the choice as tractable problems versus permanent monopoly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's "qualified mirage" / startup-lockout argument: pointing out that the status quo is already the worst licensure wall of all (incumbents own the graph permanently, zero on-ramps exist), and that the MVNO precedent shows graduated compliance can create entry points for smaller actors who could never build their own networks. This directly inverted CON's cartelization claim and left CON defending a regime that already produces the outcome CON claimed to fear.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B wins by successfully breaking Side A's core telecom/email analogies and exposing a contradiction in Side A's case. When Side A defends against safety concerns by suggesting incumbents can mandate "equivalent moderation standards" for third parties, Side B expertly leverages this to show that such homogenization destroys the very differentiation and competitive "exit" that Side A promised.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing effectively flip Side A's safety safeguards, arguing that enforcing moderation baselines across platforms creates a homogenized environment that defeats the purpose of competitive differentiation.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B wins by maintaining a clear distinction between social platforms as curated communities (with moderation and relational context) versus neutral pipes, showing how forced interoperability risks homogenization and weakened accountability; Side A's historical analogies and qualified-requirements rebuttals addressed some points but did not fully neutralize the differentiation and consent pressures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 directly answered the pressure questions by contrasting transient pipes with persistent behavioral maps and unilateral follower consent, sharpening why the telecom precedent fails to fit social graphs.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Both sides ran substantive, well-grounded cases with proper format compliance throughout. CON's most powerful contribution—the bilateral consent argument (social graphs are co-owned; exporting them gives one party unilateral authority over shared data)—is genuinely original and never fully neutralized. PRO's primary counter was "platforms already exploit this data," which is more of a comparative framing than a direct rebuttal; CON's "two violators are worse than one" point stands largely unanswered. However, PRO maintains a cleaner overall path to victory for three reasons. First, the network-effect lock-in argument is strong and CON never proposes a workable alternative—its case amounts to "stay captive because portability is complicated," which is a real burden. Second, PRO's comparative framing (ungovernable shadow exploitation today vs. auditable, user-initiated exit tomorrow) is more persuasive than CON's static comparison (one bad actor vs. two). Third, CON's qualification-moat argument, while insightful, was partially blunted by PRO's point that today's lock-in is total and absolute, whereas a compliance barrier is at least partially surmountable. PRO's rhetorical throughline—"captivity vs. freedom with guardrails"—is more memorable and directs the reader back to the motion as written. CON's closing crystallization is sharp but leaves the consent concern as an implementation caveat to a problem PRO has more honestly engaged with. The win for PRO is real but competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's "there is no 'my copy' distinct from 'your copy'" reframe [A2.1] was the sharpest single move in the debate—it correctly identified that PRO's property analogy collapses under relational data. PRO's response (platforms already exploit this, the mandate just gives the user an auditable exit) is comparative rather than direct, and CON's follow-up "doubles the attack surface" point was never squarely answered. This exchange represents the closest the debate came to a decisive clash, and while CON lands the cleaner individual blow, PRO's broader "no escape at all" frame carries more weight in the overall comparison.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side B won by successfully reframing the core nature of social data, arguing that it is inherently relational and bilateral. Side A never adequately resolved the problem that exporting a shared conversation violates the consent of the non-exporting party, allowing Side B to decisively characterize the policy as a multiplier of privacy violations rather than user liberation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 response (A1) brilliantly neutralized Side A's defense of relational privacy by stating: "You gain a choice; I lose one. Two violators are worse than one."
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A wins because it more effectively neutralized B's relational consent challenge by repeatedly highlighting that platforms already unilaterally exploit shared data without user consent, then directly answered B's pressure questions in Rebuttal 2 with cross-border regulatory precedents and existing moderation mechanisms to show added accountability over the status quo. B's qualification moat and ossification arguments were addressed via comparisons to licensed industries, while A's case for dissolving network-effect lock-in remained the clearer path to victory after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's direct A1/A2 answers in Rebuttal 2 to B's jurisdiction and abuse-moderation questions, reframing both as solvable via reciprocity and user-initiated controls rather than new structural failures.
