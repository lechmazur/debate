### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A (PRO) built a cleaner, more durable path to victory across the full exchange. Its core framework—irreversible, life-threatening harm to a verifiable subset vs. marginal, easily bypassed reduction in low-effort toxicity—remained intact through every round. The decisive clash was over the honeypot/subpoena risk and whether platform exceptions could protect dissidents. PRO's answer in A4.2–A4.3 was sharp: deleting a raw ID scan does not erase the persistent name-to-account mapping that a real-name policy must maintain to be enforceable, and authoritarian regimes compel local platforms to hand over exactly that mapping. CON's cryptographic third-party verification response was technically interesting but introduced late, never developed into a full mechanism, and never answered the core enforcement question—if the platform does not link a legal name to the account, it cannot enforce the policy. PRO also handled CON's clever Q1 contradiction ("bypassed but still a reliable directory") convincingly: sophisticated actors bypass; good-faith local dissidents comply under threat of domestic law, giving regimes precisely what they need. On moral weighing, CON argued scale (millions vs. a subset) and raised genuine harms (PTSD, suicide, career destruction), but never dislodged the asymmetry that the policy's admitted failure against sophisticated abusers leaves the worst harassment intact while removing the only protection for those facing state violence. CON's late reframe—that anonymity itself enables dissident targeting through impersonation—was conceptually interesting but arrived in the second rebuttal and closing without prior development, leaving it unable to do decisive work. CON's opening was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost at the most evidence-intensive stage. PRO answered both pressure questions with the required A1/A2 format and sharpened rather than widened the clash in closing. Overall, PRO maintained argumentative discipline while CON's best moves came too late and too thinly grounded to overcome the established core harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.1 answer to CON's Q1 contradiction ("if verification is easily bypassed, how can it create a reliable directory?"): PRO cleanly distinguished that sophisticated abusers bypass verification while vulnerable, law-abiding dissidents comply in good faith, and that authoritarian regimes need only compel local platform compliance to extract verified identities of domestic critics—resolving the apparent paradox without retreating from either premise.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively dismantling Side B's primary defense—that platforms can use privacy-preserving verification to protect vulnerable users. Side A's explanation that retaining a verified legal name still creates a subpoenaable database, regardless of whether the raw ID scan is deleted, was highly persuasive and left Side B's case critically vulnerable to the core proposition. Side A also provided logically tight answers to Side B's pressure questions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response that "Deleting a passport JPEG does not erase the database mapping the dissident to their account" completely neutralized Side B's cryptographic defense.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): PRO (Side A) won by showing that real-name policies structurally require platforms to retain linkable legal identities, creating subpoena-able honeypots that expose dissidents to state violence, while CON’s idealized "targeted exceptions" and privacy-preserving verification never resolved this core vulnerability. A also sustained the decisive moral asymmetry: CON conceded that sophisticated abusers bypass verification, leaving only a marginal reduction in casual toxicity that cannot outweigh the existential physical danger to whistleblowers and vulnerable users. B’s clipped opening and failure to directly outweigh psychological distress against imprisonment further weakened its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s dismantling of CON’s "targeted exceptions" defense by proving that platforms must persistently link legal names to accounts to enforce the policy, making that data vulnerable to authoritarian subpoenas and hacks regardless of whether raw ID scans are deleted (A2.1, A4.1–A4.3).
