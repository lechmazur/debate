### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A won by giving the clearer moral and practical case for material reparations: state continuity, inherited public benefits, ongoing wealth gaps, and workable community-level programs. B was right to press administrability and the possibility of needs-based alternatives, but too often narrowed reparations into ancestry-based individual payouts or blood quantum, which A plausibly answered with sovereign and community-based mechanisms. A's repeated claim that B conceded the whole motion was overstated, but A still better defended why direct, named repair matters more than generic redistribution or apologies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was using Britain's compensation to slaveholders to challenge B's objection to intergenerational taxpayer responsibility. B's contractual-debt answer was relevant, but A effectively framed it as proof that states can choose to make slavery-related obligations binding when the beneficiaries are perpetrators, weakening B's moral asymmetry.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by forcing the material-repair concession, exposing the one-way intergenerational-payment inconsistency, and demonstrating that universalism is the status-quo failure; Side B retreats to unworkable audits while evading the asymmetry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's direct answers to pressure questions crystallizing the "direction of payment" asymmetry.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO won by showing that the strongest objections to direct reparations—administrative impossibility and intergenerational unfairness—were inconsistent with historical practice and precedent. CON could not explain why intergenerational payment was acceptable when compensating slaveholders but became “collective punishment” when directed at victims, and offered no concrete evidence that the proposed universalist alternative would close racialized wealth gaps where similar policies have failed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s rebuttal exposing the asymmetry of Britain’s centuries-long, intergenerational payments to slaveholders—demonstrating that CON’s “collective punishment” objection disappears when the creditor class changes—forced CON into a weak technical distinction between sovereign bonds and moral debts that retained little persuasive force.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won by narrowing the debate to the difference between material targeted investment and reparations as debt settlement. A made a strong moral case for state liability and material restitution, but B persuasively argued that trusts, endowments, and targeted capital can exist without the reparations label, while the debt-settlement framing adds eligibility conflict, taxes victims' descendants, and grants the state closure. A did not fully resolve why ownership is unique to reparations or answer B's eligibility and causation pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive: it answered A's questions directly, argued irrevocable targeted capital is not unique to reparations, and turned A's acceptance of closure and general taxation into liabilities.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won by forcing the decisive concession that mechanisms (trusts/endowments) and funding (including descendants of the colonized) are identical, leaving only the "debt settled" label—which B showed grants the state closure while A explicitly embraced it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1/B4.1 exposed PRO's retreat to the exact tools CON already endorsed, then crystallized the remaining dispute as one that benefits only the perpetrator.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): CON successfully narrowed the debate to whether the "debt settled" framing uniquely serves perpetrators by providing closure, while showing the actual policy tools (trusts, endowments, targeted investment) are available under either framework. CON's rebuttals undermined PRO's claim that the reparations label alone guarantees irrevocable ownership or legal agency, and exposed the tension in PRO's position by highlighting that descendants of the colonized would be taxed to fund their own restitution. PRO's moral case remained coherent but could not overcome CON's crystallization: when the only disputed element is a receipt that lets the state declare itself finished, the remedy benefits the wrongdoer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (B4), which collapsed the debate to the single contested framing of "debt settlement," demonstrated that irrevocability is a property of legal instruments rather than labels, and reframed PRO's concession on taxpayer inclusion as "two wrongs taxing the same nurse."
