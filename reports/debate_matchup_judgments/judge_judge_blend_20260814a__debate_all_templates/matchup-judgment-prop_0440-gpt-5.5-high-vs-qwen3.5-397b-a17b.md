### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): PRO consistently maintained the single most important distinction in the debate: that police capacity being necessary does not mean marginal staffing expansion is the best buy against gun violence. CON's strongest moves—clearance rates require capacity; programs are fragile when grants expire—proved only that some enforcement backbone is needed, which PRO conceded from Rebuttal 1 onward. PRO absorbed that concession and turned it: if you already have police, the question is where the next dollar goes, and the marginal officer's time is diluted across 911 calls, traffic, reports, and transport, while the marginal focused-program dollar lands at the concentrated source of shootings. CON never cracked this framing. The "backbone" metaphor was deployed repeatedly but never evolved into a positive argument for why expansion rather than reallocation and prioritization is the right buy. PRO's closing crystallization—"necessary capacity is not the same as cost-effective marginal expansion"—was the cleanest decision rule offered, and it had been built across all prior rounds. Two execution costs (clipped opening and clipped Rebuttal 1) imposed minor costs on PRO but did not materially damage the argument since the core moved through. CON's closing was largely repetitive and added nothing new to the comparative weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answers to CON's pressure questions (A1/A2): PRO correctly explained that triage narrows the deterrent universe rather than requiring more detective headcount, and that the permanent enforcement mechanism is still the criminal legal system—interruption was never offered as a substitute sovereign. This directly absorbed CON's strongest pressure (clearance rates, fragility) without conceding the core comparative claim, and it exposed CON's central leap: proving enforcement is necessary does not prove expansion is the best marginal investment over focused prioritization of existing capacity.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): Side A won by cleanly separating 'necessary baseline capacity' from 'marginal cost-effective expansion,' proving that while police are needed for deterrence, adding general staff dilutes the next dollar. Side B relied heavily on broadening the metric to general public safety, which Side A successfully defeated by anchoring the debate to the proposition's specific focus on gun violence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction in Rebuttal 2 that 'CON jumps from detectives are overloaded to expand staffing as if a new patrol hire clears shootings' effectively neutralized Side B's best argument about deterrence requiring expanded capacity.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by drawing a clean distinction between necessary police capacity and cost-effective marginal spending. Even conceding that police must exist to enforce consequences, PRO showed that gun violence is concentrated and that focused deterrence and interruption spend the marginal dollar closer to the trigger, while expanded general staffing is diluted across non-gun tasks and saddles cities with expensive permanent obligations. CON landed strong blows on clearance rates, sustainability, and the fragility of soft-money programs, but repeatedly tried to rewrite the metric to general public safety and never fully proved that adding general patrol officers prevents more shootings per dollar than targeted programs. PRO's reframing of the debate around marginal cost-effectiveness—crystallized in the closing—provided the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to CON's "backbone" argument in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2, reframing the issue as "necessary capacity is not the same as cost-effective marginal expansion" and pointing out that a new patrol hire adds general capacity rather than clearing shootings, which blunted CON's attempt to equate focused deterrence's dependency on police with a mandate for broad staffing expansion.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued capably, but Side B identified and held the decisive cost-accounting flaw: PRO's efficiency claim depends on borrowing enforcement infrastructure (identification, warrants, witness protection, follow-through) while pricing only the messenger. PRO never fully resolved this. In A1, PRO said focused deterrence "reduces downstream demand," which is speculative and does not directly answer whether the enforcement costs enabling deterrence are included in the comparison. Side B's pressure question (Q1) crystallized this cleanly, and the closing metaphor—"a bridge is valuable only if there is something on the other side"—answered PRO's "keys to the door" framing directly. Side A's community-trust and intelligence-access argument is genuinely strong and creates a live route: interrupters unlock witness cooperation that more officers cannot. However, PRO never showed this unlocked cooperation is sufficient to make deterrence credible without additional investigative capacity—precisely what CON argued. That gap kept PRO's cost-effectiveness claim from fully landing. Side B's multiple clipped speeches are a real execution cost and prevent a wider margin, but the core argument—targeted police staffing (detectives, gun-crime units, warrant teams) is not the same as undifferentiated patrol expansion, and focused deterrence is hollow without it—survived the clipping and was never squarely neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 identifying that PRO "borrows CON's infrastructure and calls it PRO's efficiency" was the most damaging single move. PRO's entire cost comparison assumes existing police capacity can absorb the extra investigative and follow-through burden at no marginal cost—precisely the condition that does not hold in the high-violence, low-clearance cities the proposition targets. PRO's answer (interrupters reduce downstream demand) was speculative and did not close the accounting gap, leaving Side B's framing standing at the close.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully exposing a fatal flaw in Side A's cost-effectiveness calculus. Side B persuasively argued that focused deterrence relies on police capacity to deliver credible consequences, meaning Side A was borrowing Side B's enforcement infrastructure while only counting the cost of the messengers. Side A's pivot to framing interrupters as intelligence-gatherers was clever, but Side B easily neutralized it by pointing out that turning intelligence into lawful consequences still requires staffed policing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 point that Side A is 'borrowing CON's infrastructure and calling it PRO's efficiency' decisively dismantled the proposition's cost-effectiveness comparison.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by exposing a fatal cost-shifting problem in PRO's comparison: focused deterrence requires credible arrests, warrants, and witness protection to work, yet PRO priced only the community messenger while silently depending on existing police capacity. This undermined PRO's core cost-effectiveness claim. CON also effectively turned PRO's clearance-rate stagnation point into evidence that cities need more targeted investigative staffing, not less, and showed that trust without enforcement capacity cannot incapacitate high-risk shooters. PRO's case remained rhetorically vivid but could not resolve the tension between claiming police fail to deliver consequences and assuming those same police could credibly back deterrence for free.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's cost-shifting rebuttal (A4.4-A5.3), which showed that PRO's cost advantage disappears once the necessary police enforcement and investigative costs are honestly included in the focused-deterrence model, rather than treated as freely available infrastructure.
