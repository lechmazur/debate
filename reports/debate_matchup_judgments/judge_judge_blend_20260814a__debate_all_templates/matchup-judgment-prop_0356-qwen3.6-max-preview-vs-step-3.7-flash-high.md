### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate on the strength of its structural coercion argument, which CON never adequately neutralized. PRO's core actuarial claim—that as low-risk drivers migrate to tracked pools, the non-tracked pool becomes increasingly adverse-selected, organically inflating standard rates without any deliberate insurer manipulation—is a genuinely sound mechanism that CON's "competition-and-regulation" answer only partially addresses. State insurance rate regulation prevents deliberate manipulation but does not stop organic adverse-selection drift, and market competition in most regional insurance markets is not aggressive enough to prevent this dynamic. The pressure question exchange sharpened this advantage: PRO's Q1 exposed a real contradiction in CON's position (if exclusions already work voluntarily, codifying them should not break the system), and CON's own A2 in Rebuttal 2 essentially conceded the point, undermining its "regulatory blank check" objection. CON's strongest counter—that UBI discounts are funded by lower overall claims costs rather than cost-shifting—is real but does not address the adverse-selection trajectory PRO described. CON also repeatedly dismissed PRO's harms as "speculative" and "non-existent" without fully engaging the forward-looking structural dynamic, and this framing grew thinner across rounds. PRO's consistent "incentive vs. ransom" framing was more memorable and did real argumentative work throughout. CON scored for identifying that PRO's specific examples (nurses penalized for night shifts in current programs) may not reflect actual practice, and for grounding in real programs like State Farm and Progressive, but these gains were offset by the unresolved structural objection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The pressure question exchange in Rounds 5–7 was decisive. PRO's Q1 forced CON to explain why codifying existing voluntary exclusions would destroy telematics if those exclusions already work. CON's A2 in Rebuttal 2 conceded they would not break things, effectively surrendering the "regulatory blank check" objection—its primary counter to PRO's proposed fair-terms standard. PRO then used that concession in Rebuttal 2 to reframe the motion: the regulatory floor simply locks in protections insurers can already quietly roll back, turning corporate discretion into enforceable rights. That exchange shifted the burden and CON never recovered it.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the cleaner path to victory across the full exchange. Its core insight—that car insurance is legally required, that current "opt-in" models are coercive because unaffordable base rate inflation eliminates genuine choice, and crucially that the proposition does not ban behavior-based insurance but only requires fair consent—directly dismantled CON's central comparative. CON repeatedly argued the proposition forces a retreat to demographic proxies (zip codes, credit scores), but PRO answered this plainly and consistently: voluntary behavior-based discounts remain fully intact under the proposition, making CON's false-dichotomy framing a recurring misreading of the motion that went unrepaired. On workability, CON's strongest line, PRO responded in Rebuttal 2 with concrete, enforceable guardrails (no sensitive non-driving data collection, plain disclosure, no punitive markups, data deletion rights) and correctly noted that regulators already enforce analogous baseline fairness standards in other consumer financial markets. CON's response was to simply reassert "no objective metric" without engaging the specific guardrails named, which looked evasive rather than decisive. CON's best moves—the actuarial defense of base rates as actuarially neutral, the "physics" argument about night driving, and the state rating-filing defense against destination data misuse—were genuine contributions, but PRO's counter-framing around the legally required nature of the product and the real-world inadequacy of existing oversight kept those points contested rather than settled. PRO's clipped rebuttal in Round 1 is a real execution cost, and CON ran disciplined, technically grounded arguments throughout. But CON's core failure to accept and argue within the actual scope of the motion—fair opt-in, not a ban—left its central comparative structurally weak by the closing rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 answer in Rebuttal 2 to CON's pressure question: CON asked how stripping safe drivers of their only tool reduces inequity. PRO answered cleanly that the proposition does not strip that tool—drivers can still voluntarily share limited, relevant driving data for discounts. This directly neutralized CON's most repeated comparative, and CON never recovered from it in the closing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO built the stronger case by directly neutralizing CON's actuarial and workability claims through targeted answers on guardrails and coercion, while exposing the limits of existing rules and preserving voluntary data sharing. CON's core routes (existing oversight suffices; fairness is unworkable) were left partially unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's direct A1/A2 answers to pressure questions on metrics and the zip-code fallback, which crystallized the coercion comparison without widening the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON successfully undermined PRO's central harms by distinguishing behavior telematics from location surveillance, showing that modern programs price driving mechanics rather than sensitive destinations and that actuarial filing requirements and liability concerns already discourage the privacy abuses PRO described. CON's persistent pressure on the vagueness of "genuinely fair terms"—and the resulting compliance risk that would terminate usage-based programs—remained largely unanswered, while PRO's clipped first rebuttal and reliance on unverified worst-case scenarios about destination-based pricing created real execution costs. PRO's coercion narrative was reframed by CON as standard risk pricing for an unmonitored pool, and CON's cross-subsidy argument shifted the inequity back onto the proposition. PRO failed to prove that the specific privacy and discrimination harms were actuarially incentivized or regulatorily permitted, leaving CON with the cleaner live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's distinction between behavior telematics and location tracking, demonstrating that insurers price acceleration, braking, and time-of-day rather than destinations or passenger identities, which neutralized PRO's core privacy and discrimination examples and exposed them as conflations.
