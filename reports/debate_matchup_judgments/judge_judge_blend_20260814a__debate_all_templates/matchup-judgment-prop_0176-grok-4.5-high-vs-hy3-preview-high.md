### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, competing causal stories (SWFs smoothing volatility and preserving intergenerational wealth vs. cash bypassing corrupt intermediaries and meeting urgent needs), and both landed real punches on elite capture. The pivotal exchange was in Pressure Questions: B's Q2 asked how "universal" cash disbursement escapes elite-controlled registries, IDs, and mobile-money platforms, and B's follow-up (B4.2) developed this into a genuine structural rebuttal of A's central claim that cash "strips control" from the state. A never directly answered this point in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, instead re-asserting that money is "decentralized the moment it leaves the treasury," which sidesteps how it gets distributed fairly in the first place. By contrast, A's parallel pressure on SWF rule-enforcement (Q1) was at least partially answered by B's "public ledgers/legal separation raise the cost of looting" argument, which, while not airtight, engaged the objection rather than ignoring it. B also maintained a clean, consistent decision-rule ("which instrument leaves residual national wealth after crash") that anchored its closing, while A's closing reasserted rhetoric ("lootable piggy bank") without closing the registry gap. This makes B's route to victory more clearly intact by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q2 and its Rebuttal 2 answer (B4.2) exposing that "universal" cash disbursement still depends on state-controlled registries and payment systems—an objection A's case never squarely answered.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that both instruments face elite capture in weak states, but only sovereign wealth funds convert finite windfalls into a residual asset that outlasts price crashes and can sustainably finance the very human-capital investments Side B prioritizes. A’s permanent-income synthesis defused B’s urgency argument, and B never squarely answered how cash protects past human-capital gains once transfers disappear during a commodity bust. A also maintained the cleaner macroeconomic story on Dutch-disease sterilization, while B’s claim that SWF returns later replicate the same problem ignored the value of intertemporal smoothing and rules-based spending filters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A’s second rebuttal reframing of the capture debate: conceding that capture is endemic, A argued the decisive question is which instrument still leaves national wealth standing after politics extracts its toll, and showed that cash liquidates the windfall into consumption that evaporates, while a fund at least preserves diversified assets and permanent-income capacity.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins the central governance and macro tradeoffs. CON's strongest argument — that SWFs are a centralized lootable pot — is neutralized when PRO shows cash still needs state-built registries, IDs and payment plumbing that can be politicized, so capture merely changes form. PRO then wins on residual wealth: a fund sterilizes surplus and preserves principal to finance health/education through a bust via a permanent-income rule, while a pure cash model liquidates the windfall and leaves no fiscal buffer when prices crash. CON's irreversibility and urgent human-capital claim is powerful but does not answer how that human capital is maintained without any leftover asset.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B3 Q1 — pressing that universal cash still requires government-designed eligibility and payment systems, undercutting A's core "strips control away" claim and shifting the capture debate to symmetric vulnerability where SWF's smoothing and residual asset then decides.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a sharper, more durable throughline: SWF capture under weak institutions is "total and invisible" while cash leakage is "partial and messy," so residual delivery to citizens survives even corrupt intermediation better than a fund that can vanish entirely. PRO (B) never squarely closed this loop—its answer to the pressure question ("ring-fencing creates a high-profile legal hurdle") asserted difficulty without explaining why elites who can rewrite laws can't also loot funds completely, and it never answered A's point that an SWF's eventual domestic spending reproduces the same inflation/Dutch-disease risk it claims to avoid. B's Dutch disease and "permanent expectation" arguments were real and pressed hard, but A's boom-year-share reframing and remittance analogy blunted them without a decisive B counter. B relied on a specific unsupported inflation figure (20-30%) that reads as brittle fake precision, while A stayed within plausible mechanism-level claims. B's Rebuttal 2 was also clipped before its closing synthesis reached the judge, an execution cost under the stated rules. A's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("Cash makes ownership real; SWFs merely relocate the curse"), while B's closing mostly recapped without resolving the capture asymmetry. Overall A made the debate easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's "SWF capture is clean, total, and invisible... cash leakage is messy and incomplete" (A2.1), reinforced through the pressure-question exchange, was the pivotal clash B never fully neutralized.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing the institutional feasibility gap that Side A could not close. While PRO mounted a strong theoretical case about Dutch disease and long-term investment, CON successfully showed that in the weak institutional contexts typical of resource-rich developing countries, SWFs face total elite capture while cash distributions retain residual benefits for millions. PRO's ring-fencing defense relied on legal mechanisms that the same corrupt regimes could rewrite, and the "20-30% inflation" claim was brittle unsupported precision. CON's reframing of the 20-year metric to include compounding early human-capital gains, combined with the devastating "leakage vs. total erasure" comparison, provided a cleaner route to victory under the proposition's real-world constraints. The clipped ending to A's Rebuttal 2 added a minor execution cost, but even the visible portion failed to neutralize the residual-delivery problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1/B4.3's comparison of partial cash leakage (which still delivers nutrition and enterprise to millions) versus total SWF capture (which leaves citizens with nothing) proved decisive and was never fully recovered from by PRO.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won the institutional-weakness clash. PRO's core Dutch-disease and expectation arguments were met with A2.1/A4.1-A4.2: partial cash leakage still delivers residual nutrition/enterprise that compounds, while SWF capture is total and invisible. PRO's ring-fencing defense was directly questioned in Pressure Qs and left unconvincing, compounded by a clipped Rebuttal 2. CON's 20-year weighing — early human-capital > promised public goods that rarely arrive — survived.
