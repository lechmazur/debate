### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won by making the key implementation clash decisive: fragmented sectors lack real bargainers, tiered sectoral rules invite capture and classification gaming, and a universal indexed wage is easier for workers to know and enforce. A had a plausible tailoring and package-benefits case, but leaned on shaky Seattle specifics, overstated that scheduling rights cannot be legislated directly, and repeatedly misdescribed indexing as a concession to patching. B’s dishwasher example crystallized the worker-level harm of tiering and was never adequately answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s tiering claim was most decisive: if sectoral standards vary by employer type or sub-sector, identical workers can be paid less and employers gain incentives to reclassify; if they do not vary, A loses the tailoring advantage and has recreated the minimum wage with more bureaucracy.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A won by exposing B's contradictions on complexity/tiering, neutralizing the Seattle example as mere legislation, and leaving the tiered subminimum wage for the dishwasher unanswered while showing all claimed benefits are replicable without boards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 directly dismantling B's answers on scheduling bundling and enforcement authority.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON won by exposing a structural dilemma that PRO never escaped: in fragmented service industries, sectoral boards either create genuinely different tiers—inviting classification gaming and paying identical workers differently based on employer size—or they converge on a single number, becoming a slower, appointed version of a minimum wage with extra litigation. CON’s "dishwasher" frame made this concrete and morally salient, while PRO’s idealized vision of appointed boards exercising industry-specific enforcement remained abstract and ungrounded. CON also cleanly neutralized PRO’s "shock cycle" argument with indexing, and showed that bundling scheduling protections is ordinary legislative work, not a unique board power. PRO conceded that board members are government appointees, undermining the "bargaining" framing, and never squarely answered who protects the worker in the cheaper tier. After the full exchange, CON had the only live route to victory still standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s complexity dilemma: PRO’s model must either produce genuinely different tiered rates (inviting reclassification and permanent subminimum wages for identical work) or collapse into a single number (making the board an unnecessary, slower substitute for a statute). Paired with the unanswered "dishwasher" equity challenge, this rebuttal gutted PRO’s claim to precision and left PRO defending a mechanism that was either unworkable or redundant.
