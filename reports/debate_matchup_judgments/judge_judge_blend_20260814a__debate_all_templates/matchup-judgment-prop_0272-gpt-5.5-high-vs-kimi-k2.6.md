### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): PRO wins on the strength of its equity-as-adequacy vs. equity-as-reach distinction, landed clearly in Rebuttal 2: a digital platform "reaches" a student in a thin sense but cannot supply quiet space, adult oversight, motivation, safety, or the developmental work of embodied cooperation. CON never squarely answered this—its computer-lab counter actually concedes that supervised on-campus presence is valuable, which undermines its own cost argument. PRO's "lowered ceiling" framing held across all rounds: telling disadvantaged students their after-school future is more screen-based remediation while affluent peers get teams, studios, and stages is not equity. PRO also defused CON's sharpest budget pressure in Rebuttal 2 by clarifying the proposition targets the next expansion dollar, not existing academic programs—this deflated CON's most dramatic framing without conceding anything real. CON's transportation and late-bus argument is the strongest live pressure and was never fully resolved, but PRO's reply—that the answer is to prioritize making school-based opportunities genuinely accessible rather than redesigning after-school life around home isolation—is more compelling than CON's implicit concession that poor students should simply accept a login as the realistic ceiling. CON's academic-mission argument that sports and arts belong to community centers is undercut by CON's own equity framing elsewhere, since PRO correctly notes those community options are fee-based and transport-dependent. PRO's two clipped speeches are a real execution cost and prevent a larger margin, but the visible portions were coherent and the core comparative case remained intact through the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 distinction between reach and adequacy: "A digital program can reach a student in the thin sense that it appears on a laptop. It does not mean the student has quiet space, adult help, motivation, safety, or the developmental benefits that after-school time is uniquely positioned to provide." This directly answered CON's equity-as-reach argument and reframed the whole debate around whether "something" should be the ceiling for disadvantaged children—a framing CON never neutralized.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A won by maintaining a highly consistent equity narrative and effectively exposing a contradiction in Side B's case. Side B argued that physical programs exclude students who lack transportation home, yet when pressed on unsafe home environments, Side B suggested keeping computer labs open after school—which would require the very same transportation and staffing Side B claimed were impossible. Side A's framing that "reach is not adequacy" decisively won the equity clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal correctly identified that defining equity as mere "reach" allows schools to offer a "lowered ceiling" to disadvantaged students, perfectly answering Side B's scalability arguments.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by maintaining the core equity and developmental clash: physical programs address what disadvantaged kids uniquely lack at home (safety, supervision, embodiment) while digital offers inferior "reach." CON's cost, scalability, and academic-mission arguments were competently pressed but neutralized by PRO's direct answers on not cutting academics and reframing equity as adequacy over mere access. PRO better crystallized the "before" sequencing throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's direct A1/A2 answers in Rebuttal 2 [B4.1-B4.2] and adequacy-vs-reach comparison [B4.3] most cleanly damaged CON's equity and safety rebuttals without widening the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): PRO built and sustained a coherent developmental hierarchy across every round: embodied programs provide the neurological and social substrate that digital enrichment cannot substitute, and physical programs require the least infrastructure to reach the most children. The "no broadband, no login, no tech support" equity argument was simple, structurally tight, and never fully neutralized—CON's counter (school-hosted access provides devices and connectivity) required its own staffing and infrastructure assumptions that PRO kept pointing back to. On the threshold question, PRO gave a direct and plausible answer in A1: functional accessibility (gym open, art room supervised, playground safe) is the standard, and the school board applies it the same way it applies any other budget priority. CON's threshold-instability pressure was the debate's sharpest moment, but PRO held the line well enough. CON's counter-equity case and "flexible provision vs. mandatory queue" framing were genuinely strong, but both of CON's rebuttals were clipped for exceeding the word limit, leaving core arguments incomplete—a real execution cost in the rounds most critical for deepening pressure. PRO's arguments remained consistent and fully developed throughout. CON's closing was clean but did not land a decisive turn; PRO's closing crystallized the comparison more memorably with the floor/ceiling image and the "childhood is not content to be downloaded" line.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was around the equity argument: PRO's Rebuttal 1 countered CON's scaling/equity claim with the observation that a basketball and an unlocked gym require no bandwidth, no login, no software license, and no tech support—framing physical programs as the most democratically accessible option. CON tried to answer this by noting supervision and staffing costs, but PRO's point held that those costs are smaller and more evenly distributable than the layered infrastructure of a proctored digital program, and PRO answered the follow-up pressure question (Q2/A2) by directly tying the tutoring-need argument back to the physical-deficit root cause, keeping the hierarchy intact.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully framing the motion as a rigid administrative queue that harms students with specific, immediate needs. Side A's attempt to defend this sequencing by claiming that academic struggles are merely symptoms of a lack of physical play was unconvincing and failed to address Side B's concrete examples of rural, ESL, or disabled students. Despite minor clipping in Side B's speeches, their comparative weighing was highly effective.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal decisively exposed the dilemma in Side A's "baseline" threshold and dismantled the claim that a student's need for tutoring or advanced coursework is secondary to unstructured play.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the stronger overall case by consistently defending developmental foundations and equity advantages of physical programs as substrate rather than interchangeable options, directly answering pressure questions with a clear board-decided baseline threshold and tying urgent needs to play deficits, while Side A's flexibility argument suffered from incomplete clipped rebuttals that failed to fully neutralize the sequencing claim or endpoint concerns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers, which established the modest threshold for "more available" and rebutted delay for academic needs by linking them to foundational deficits.
