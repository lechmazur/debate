### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Both sides opened with competent, grounded cases, but A progressively sharpened its comparative advantage through the rebuttals. The decisive clash was whether national currencies would have delivered smoother adjustment or simply relocated the crisis. A's core rebuttal—that free capital movement turns anticipated devaluations into speculative runs, capital flight, and banking stress rather than technocratic rebalancing—directly undermined B's counterfactual mechanism. B's A1 answer (ERM procedures and central bank intervention) was its weakest moment: managed bands historically became targets precisely under capital pressure, which is why the euro was adopted in the first place. A exploited this gap effectively. A's pressure questions were also sharper than B's, targeting the evidentiary basis for the counterfactual (Q2) and the capital-mobility mechanism (Q1), while B's questions, though reasonable, allowed A to respond clearly. B's "damage control" framing was rhetorically effective and largely unanswered in substance, and the concrete unemployment figures landed well. But B's closing was brief and left the "competitive devaluations corrode the single market" argument uncontested. A's closing directly dismantled B's "contained" framing by correctly noting that cross-border asset holdings mean currency crises propagate across borders regardless. B bore the harder burden—demonstrating a counterfactual no-euro world would be stronger today—and never fully discharged it. Multiple clipping notes on both sides imposed mild execution costs throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal of B's "periodic realignments" claim was the most decisive exchange. B relied on smooth, managed devaluations as the counterfactual relief valve, but A showed that in a free-capital integrated market, anticipated devaluations become invitations for capital flight before any adjustment occurs—turning sovereignty into crisis management without a shared central bank. B's A1 response (ERM procedures and reserves) actually confirmed the weakness, since managed ERM bands historically broke under speculative pressure, the very reason the euro was pursued. This exchange fatally weakened B's core causal story.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's core mechanism: the idea that national currencies would act as a clean, local shock absorber. Side B convincingly argued that in an integrated European market, currency devaluations would inevitably trigger cross-border contagion, capital flight, and political friction, rendering Side A's "contained" crises impossible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing and second rebuttal effectively neutralized Side A's claim that crises would remain "local" by pointing out that in a single market with cross-border banking and trade, one country's devaluation immediately hits its neighbors' creditors and exporters.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by building a cleaner comparative counterfactual. A showed the euro crisis was painful, but B demonstrated that a no-euro Europe would not have avoided severe shocks: cross-border financial claims and integrated supply chains would have transmitted currency crises across borders anyway, while competitive devaluations and German monetary dominance would have corroded the single market without shared governance. B’s direct answers to pressure questions—especially that national currencies would have changed the form of crisis rather than eliminated the need for rescues—exposed a real burden A never met. B’s closing demolition of A’s claim that separate currencies would keep conflicts "local and contained" was decisive, because it destroyed the core mechanism A needed to prove the EU would be stronger today without the euro.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s closing rebuttal of A’s "contained" thesis: B showed that in a deeply integrated market with cross-border debts, contracts, and bank assets, currency crises would not stay local, directly neutralizing A’s central argument that separate currencies would allow contained national adjustment and preserve EU cohesion.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Three decisive moments stand out. First, the "fire extinguisher" framing in Rebuttal 1 landed a real blow: CON's core move of counting rescue funds and banking union as proof of euro strength was credibly reframed as proof the design created the danger in the first place. CON never escaped this circularity charge; its A1 in Rebuttal 2 essentially restated the claim rather than breaking the loop. Second, PRO's distinction between rule-bound flexible adjustment and predatory competitive devaluation answered CON's most persistent attack. CON kept invoking pre-1999 currency crises, but PRO's response—that coordinated rules can constrain predatory moves while still allowing genuine adjustment—was live at the close and never squarely neutralized. Third, the Denmark/Sweden example survived. CON tried to dismiss it as "shallow integration," but this contradicted CON's own framing that they are "highly integrated," creating a visible tension PRO exploited cleanly. CON's strongest genuine asset—the ECB's lender-of-last-resort capacity—is real and concrete, and PRO never fully negated it. But it could not carry the whole case against PRO's structural argument that a better-designed Europe would have needed less emergency rescue precisely because exchange-rate flexibility would have absorbed more shocks before they became systemic. PRO also landed sharper pressure questions that exposed the circularity in CON's case, while CON's questions received adequate if abstract answers. Side B's repeated clipping is a real execution cost, but the core arguments visible in the transcript were coherent, cumulative, and ultimately more convincing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's fire-extinguisher analogy in Rebuttal 1 [B2.7]—"A house is not stronger because the fire forced you to buy better extinguishers"—was the most decisive exchange. It reframed CON's entire institutional-development argument as evidence of design failure rather than design success. CON's A1 in Rebuttal 2 attempted to answer it by arguing shared monetary stakes created the political will for mutual support, but this response was circular: it counted the euro's danger as its own justification. PRO's follow-up crystallized the point in the closing, and it remained live and unanswered at the end of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by effectively exposing the circularity in Side B's core argument. Side B heavily relied on the idea that the euro forced the creation of collective rescue tools, but Side A consistently countered that building emergency machinery to fix a vulnerability the euro itself created does not constitute a net gain in strength. Side B struggled to explain why these tools were intrinsically better than the natural shock absorbers of national currencies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized Side B's 'institutional progress' argument by pointing out that 'a house is not stronger because the fire forced you to buy better extinguishers' and that the EU wouldn't need equivalent risk-sharing tools if exchange rates had absorbed the shocks.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by demonstrating that the euro eliminated essential shock absorbers for diverse economies, forcing adjustment through unemployment, austerity, and politically toxic creditor-debtor conflicts that corroded EU legitimacy. A successfully reframed B’s crisis institutions as compensatory fixes for euro-created vulnerabilities rather than net gains, and the Sweden/Denmark counterexample fatally undermined B’s claim that monetary union was necessary for deep integration. A’s weighing—defining strength as resilience plus legitimacy—provided a clearer route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s rebuttal that post-crisis rescue funds and banking union were not proof of strength but compensation for self-inflicted fragility, using the “house is not stronger because the fire forced you to buy better extinguishers” analogy and arguing the euro was an engine that made solidarity unavoidable under emergency conditions rather than the only possible engine of cooperation.
