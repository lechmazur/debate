### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the stronger path through the debate across three interlocking arguments that were never fully neutralized. First, the capital-fungibility point: PRO pivoted to "smart capital" after conceding money reroutes, but CON then demonstrated that the mentorship, networks, and institutional prestige PRO fears also travel through consulting contracts, talent recruitment, and open research partnerships that no equity screen can intercept—a point PRO answered only weakly by claiming equity backing is necessary to afford elite hiring, a chain of causation with multiple breakable links. Second, the compliant-vs.-adversary asymmetry crystallized cleanly in A4.3–A4.4: law-abiding universities, pension funds, and domestic startups absorb the full compliance burden while threat actors simply reroute through third-country subsidiaries. PRO never squarely answered this distributional critique. Third, CON's "imperfect visibility beats calculated blindness" framing in A4.1 successfully defended the equity-as-window argument against PRO's co-optation rebuttal. PRO's best late contribution—the compute-threshold (FLOPs) answer to the definitional overbreadth challenge—was genuinely clever and partially defused one CON concern, but it did nothing to address fungibility rerouting or the asymmetric burden problem. PRO's closing mostly recapitulated earlier points without resolving these live clashes. CON's closing was tighter, crystallized the core logic efficiently, and landed the "self-sabotage dressed up as vigilance" framing as a memorable summation of why the policy would reliably punish defenders while adversaries adapt. The margin is real but not dominant because PRO's smart-capital argument has genuine intuitive force and the FLOPs answer showed analytical sharpness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's answer (A4.3) to PRO's pressure question B3.2, which tried to expose a contradiction between fungibility and paralysis. CON cleanly separated the two channels: evaders use offshore shells precisely because those structures are outside the screening perimeter, while compliant actors (universities, pension funds, startups) face the full regulatory burden. This answer turned PRO's pressure question into support for CON's own case, showing that screening filters out the law-abiding while missing the actual threat—a reframe PRO never effectively countered.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A narrowly wins because it preserved the core proposition as a targeted screening tool that reduces the transfer of Western “smart capital,” not a perfect embargo on all technology diffusion. Side B had sharper rhetoric and strong pressure on evasion, non-equity channels, and compliance burdens, but often treated screening as if it had to stop every workaround to be worthwhile. A’s argument that equity-linked investment brings incentives, prestige, networks, and active incubation remained live, and B’s visibility/board-seat alternative was not persuasive enough in authoritarian strategic sectors to outweigh the security risk of helping scale rival AI and semiconductor capabilities. A had some overclaims and reference errors, but its partial-mitigation logic better fit the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was pressing that “smart capital” can move through consulting, talent, cloud, and research rather than equity. Side A’s answer that equity creates the central financial incentive and ecosystem alignment did not fully eliminate the concern, but it was enough to show screening still meaningfully reduces active Western incubation.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A wins by establishing that equity investment transmits irreplaceable "smart capital" incentives and networks that fungible cash reroutes cannot replace, then defending narrow hardware thresholds as feasible extensions of existing controls. Side B's visibility and bypass arguments remain live but are undercut by A's direct answers on authoritarian co-option and incentive removal. A crystallizes the decisive national-security tradeoff more cleanly across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 directly resolving the pressure questions on equity incentives and objective thresholds, neutralizing CON's core fungibility and paralysis claims while sharpening the comparison.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO built and maintained a cleaner path to victory. The decisive analytical move was distinguishing fungible cash from non-fungible tacit knowledge and embedded expertise—if capital were truly fungible, authoritarian rivals would have already closed the semiconductor gap through state funding, but they have not. CON never squarely answered that challenge. PRO's pressure questions then exposed a genuine contradiction CON carried throughout: investment cannot simultaneously be innocuous money (easily replaced by state funds) and an irreplaceable intelligence window. CON's Rebuttal 2 attempted a reconciliation—capital is fungible for adversaries but export controls handle tech while investments handle intel—but this was strained and required accepting that investment does carry strategic information, which supports PRO's core case. PRO's rebuttal argument that investors hold fiduciary duties, not intelligence obligations, was never rebutted. CON's strongest pillar—chilling effects on domestic innovation—was eroded by PRO's distinction between domestic R&D (unimpeded) and outbound flows to adversarial ecosystems (screened). The "managed friction" framing PRO used was an honest concession that helped rather than hurt: CON tried to weaponize it as a fatal admission, but PRO had already framed managed friction as the goal rather than a failure. Both sides were disciplined on sourcing and grounding; neither inflated claims with fake precision. PRO's closings crystallized the live clash cleanly. CON's closing was rhetorically polished but largely recapped without fully resolving the internal tension PRO had opened.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Questions round (Q1/Q2) was the most decisive exchange. Q1 exposed that if capital is fungible and easily replaced, it cannot simultaneously chill domestic innovation when blocked—those two claims pull in opposite directions. Q2 forced CON to choose: is outbound investment harmless money or a strategic intelligence channel? CON never resolved this tension, and PRO's subsequent rebuttal correctly identified it as fatal to the CON case, noting that roadmap access and embedded engineering support cannot stay inert on a spreadsheet—they harden into adversary capability.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by making the key distinction between replaceable cash and non-fungible strategic capability. Its case that outbound investment bundles money with tacit knowledge, roadmaps, engineering support, and relationship access survived the exchange better than Side B's claims about fungibility and innovation chill. Side B had a plausible warning about bureaucracy and lost visibility, but it overextended the impact and never fully resolved why investment could be an irreplaceable intelligence window while supposedly not being a meaningful technology-transfer channel.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure and second rebuttal were decisive: it forced Side B to defend the tension between calling outbound investment harmless, fungible money and also calling it a crucial strategic window. Side B's asymmetry answer was coherent but under-warranted, while Side A's export-controls-inspect-cargo versus screening-severs-the-relationship framing clarified the unique benefit of the policy.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): PRO built the stronger overall case by cleanly exposing CON's core contradiction on whether capital is fungible money or an irreplaceable intelligence/relationship channel, directly answering both pressure questions, and crystallizing that screening targets sustained knowledge pipelines export controls cannot reach. CON's responses remained defensive, repeated the speed-vs-walls framing without neutralizing the tacit-knowledge distinction, and conceded "managed friction" that PRO used to weaken their position.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answers to the pressure questions, which simultaneously exposed CON's inconsistency on capital's role and reframed screening as blocking the "railroad" rather than cargo.
