### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A kept the debate anchored to the narrow motion ("solely because") and consistently showed that landlords retain every legitimate screening tool CON worried about—rent-approval failure, inspection timing, lease incompatibility—so the rule only removes the categorical stereotype veto, not real risk management. A's rebuttal that this isn't "conscription" (government pays, landlord delivers the same housing, still gets rent) went largely unanswered by CON, which kept repeating the third-party-payment-risk framing without directly refuting that reframe. CON's strongest point—that upfront costs (holding the unit, inspection prep) occur before any concrete failure is knowable—was met by A's counter that landlords can enforce documented timeline/condition requirements case-by-case, which is a workable answer CON never fully dismantled. Pressure-question answers were properly formatted on both sides, but A's answers more precisely closed the loop on its own case, while CON's answers largely restated its opening thesis. A's clipped opening and closing are a real execution cost, and CON's "conscription/litigation chilling effect" framing was rhetorically effective, but on substance A built the cleaner, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's [A2.4]/[A4.2-4.3] response reframing CON's "conscription" argument—landlords already provide the same housing and receive rent, so the rule only forecloses refusal without inquiry—combined with the point that landlords may still reject for documented, applicant-specific incompatibilities, most directly neutralized CON's core cash-flow-risk objection, and CON never squarely answered that reframe in Rebuttal 2 or Closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side B won the debate by successfully reframing vouchers not just as an alternate source of income, but as a third-party payment contract that imposes pre-approval costs. Side B effectively countered Side A's claim that landlords can simply reject failed vouchers by pointing out that landlords must first absorb holding costs and bureaucratic delays to reach that point of rejection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first answer in Rebuttal 2 decisively dismantled Side A's argument by explaining that the 'additional right' landlords need is the right to avoid a costly pre-payment process, effectively exposing the flaw in Side A's 'concrete grounds for denial' framework.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): B wins because it successfully framed the decisive clash as a choice between documented, case-by-case screening and categorical exclusion without inquiry. CON landed a real point about pre-lease bureaucratic costs and litigation risk, but PRO showed these were speculative program-friction harms that could be addressed through actual assessment and record-keeping, whereas PRO's harm—making lawfully funded vouchers unusable and concentrating poverty—was certain and immediate. PRO's direct answers to pressure questions and its "compare the errors" crystallization in the second rebuttal and closing resolved the central burden cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal (Turn 7) exposed CON's sleight of hand in treating every voucher as carrying unacceptable pre-emptive risk while quietly ignoring that all income streams carry uncertainty, and established the decisive "compare the errors" frame that carried the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but PRO more successfully defended the line that actually decides the motion: categorical refusal based on the payment source versus individualized, documented burdens. CON's strongest move was seizing on PRO's "concrete, documented reason" concession (A2.2) and arguing it swallows the rule (B2.1, B4.3-B4.5), forcing PRO to clarify its standard. PRO's pressure questions (A3.1-A3.2) sharpened this precisely, and PRO's answers (A4.1-A4.2) held a workable distinction—blanket proxy exclusion is banned, case-specific proven burdens are not—which is analogous to familiar individualized-assessment frameworks and survived CON's vagueness objection without collapsing. CON's vagueness critique (B4.5) is a real point about implementation difficulty, but it attacks administrability rather than disproving the underlying normative distinction, so it lands as pressure rather than a knockout. CON's fallback—that government should fix programs before compelling landlords—was directly answered by PRO's closing point that this would let private refusal nullify public aid in the interim, and CON never fully rebutted that temporal asymmetry. CON's rebuttal 2 was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost per the rules, though the lost content appears to mostly restate an existing point. Overall PRO gave the cleaner, more resolved path to a verdict.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The chain from A2.2 (PRO allows refusal for "documented agency delay") through B2.1 ("that concession defeats the categorical rule") to A4.1 (PRO's categorical-proxy vs. individualized-burden distinction) was the decisive exchange, and PRO's clarification held up better than CON's vagueness rebuttal in B4.5.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully reframing the debate from 'income discrimination' to 'declining a government-regulated transaction.' When Side A conceded that landlords could reject applicants for specific voucher-related burdens like delays or mandatory terms, Side B decisively weaponized this concession, proving that Side A was functionally carving an exception into the proposition's categorical rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal and subsequent pressure questions brilliantly cornered Side A by pointing out that agency delays and administrative burdens are inherent features of the voucher transaction, meaning rejecting them is fundamentally inseparable from rejecting the voucher itself.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins because it maintained a consistent, defensible distinction between categorical voucher discrimination and individualized rejection for concrete, documented burdens. Side A (CON) exposed real tensions in this distinction and showed the proposition sweeps broadly across materially different assistance programs, but could not fully capitalize after its Rebuttal 2 was clipped for exceeding the word limit. PRO’s closing crystallized the clash more cleanly: the practical effect of CON’s rule is preemptive exclusion of low-income households, while PRO preserves legitimate screening for real risks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s “fatal admission” argument that PRO’s “concrete documented burden” exception contradicts the text of the proposition, and PRO’s response distinguishing categorical proxy from specific transaction costs—this exchange defined the central fault line of the debate.
