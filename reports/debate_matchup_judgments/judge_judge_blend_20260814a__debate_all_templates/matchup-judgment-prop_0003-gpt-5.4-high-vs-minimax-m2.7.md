(2) On "permanent loss," PRO established early and maintained that blocking homes is also a permanent, irreversible choice — not a neutral status quo. CON never answered this symmetry directly. PRO's implicit response — that those institutions need to weigh housing need more heavily than they currently do, which is precisely what the motion envisions — is consistent with the motion as written and CON never acknowledged it directly, instead repeating that PRO "borrowed CON's answer." PRO's closing crystallized this cleanly: CON's position ultimately gives the significant building an absolute veto, which is exclusion protected by aesthetics. CON's alternatives (build elsewhere, ADUs, adaptive reuse) were genuine but PRO correctly identified they collapse into the same result — preservation wins — when those measures are insufficient. CON's strongest contribution was the "relax" word critique, which was clever and pointed, but PRO had enough ground to hold the core case throughout.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins on the decisive clash around necessity and limiting principles. The most consequential moment came when Side A's Rebuttal 2 conceded that relaxing historic protections is merely "a tool" whose benefits depend on many other reforms passing simultaneously. Side B correctly exploited this in its second rebuttal: if the proposition is just one contingent instrument in a larger package, the bar for accepting irreversible, certain cultural loss rises sharply. Side A never adequately answered this. On the limiting principle, Side A's answer—"the same democratic institutions"—was defensively adequate but hollow; Side B correctly identified that under sustained housing pressure, that logic makes almost any central site expendable, which is precisely why historic protections exist in the first place. Side B also correctly reinterpreted Side A's own examples: Tokyo's affordability stems from broad citywide permissiveness rather than heritage sacrifice, and Houston's reflects abundant land generally, not a lesson about demolishing significant urban buildings. Side A's strongest genuine counter—that housing scarcity itself imposes certain, measurable costs—was real and should have been developed further, but it addressed the general urgency of the housing crisis rather than whether this specific policy produces commensurate benefits. Side A's rhetoric was consistently stronger and the "paralysis with a preservationist label" framing was memorable, but emotional resonance did not compensate for the structural gap in the necessity argument. The clipped closings partially offset each other as execution costs, but Side B's complete and disciplined close, which crystallized the necessity-vs.-urgency distinction cleanly, compares favorably to Side A's incomplete one. Side B's pressure questions were sharper and did more to expose genuine burdens, while Side A's questions were reasonable but led to answers that slightly favored B's position.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 response to A's "just a tool" concession was the debate's decisive exchange. Once Side A admitted the proposition operates as one optional instrument in a larger reform package, Side B correctly raised the bar: if the housing benefit is contingent on many other reforms also passing, why normalize permanent demolition of architecturally significant buildings for a marginal, uncertain payoff? This move converted A's reasonable flexibility into a structural vulnerability that A never neutralized.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): B won by showing the motion requires proof of necessity and a workable limit; A's answers on democratic discretion and "a tool" left both unaddressed while B kept the comparison on scalable non-heritage options vs irreversible loss.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 directly dismantled A's "who decides" answer by showing the elasticity of "blocking needed density" and forced the concession that the policy is merely marginal.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully reframing the debate around necessity rather than urgency. B demonstrated that relaxing historic protections is neither a necessary nor sufficient housing strategy, characterized it as a politically convenient dodge that avoids harder broad reforms, and exposed PRO's lack of a credible limiting principle. A's concession that the policy is merely one contingent tool among many, combined with a weak answer on democratic oversight as a safeguard, left PRO's core burden unmet after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response in Rebuttal 2 and Closing that historic-district demolition is a politically easier dodge avoiding broader reform, paired with the exploitation of PRO's concession that the policy is only one non-decisive tool, collapsing the necessity claim.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully narrowing the clash to necessity and limiting principles. While Side A delivered powerful moral framing around human need versus architectural preservation, Side B effectively argued that historic districts represent a small fraction of urban land and that broad reforms elsewhere offer scalable housing gains without irreversible cultural loss. Side B’s pressure on Side A’s limiting principle (“democratic institutions”) exposed a real vulnerability, and Side B capitalized on Side A’s concession that the policy is merely “one tool” with contingent benefits. Side B’s weighing of certain, irreversible heritage loss against speculative, indirect affordability gains provided a cleaner, more decisive path to victory. Side A’s rhetoric was sharper, but Side B’s comparative analysis and strategic burden management were stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 effectively turned Side A’s “tool” concession and “democratic institutions” answer into a decisive weighing advantage, arguing that normalizing irreversible loss for a contingent benefit with elastic limits fails the necessity test and leaves the motion unjustified.
