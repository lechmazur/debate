### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently framing the policy comparison around marginal outcomes rather than average graduate outcomes. Side A established that completed degrees can generate high lifetime earnings, adaptability, and mobility, but repeatedly assumed those returns carried over to subsidy-induced entrants without adequately establishing completion or marginal graduate returns. Its claims that apprenticeship wages broadly plateau and that a 40% completion rate still decisively favors college were asserted with unsupported precision. Side B better answered specialization and mobility concerns through portable credentials, employer co-investment, and advancement into supervision or ownership, while emphasizing wages, forgone earnings, and downside risk. Both sides occasionally used incorrect reference labels, but this did not alter the substantive result.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered the plateau question with retraining and career ladders, answered subsidy capture through registered-apprenticeship design and employer co-payment, then returned to the central asymmetry between a marginal enrollee and an already successful graduate. Side A’s pyramid response created some pressure but did not repair its missing marginal expected-value case.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won the marginal vs. average clash that decides the proposition. CON kept pricing the biography of a graduate, while PRO forced the debate onto the fence-sitter who generates most new enrollment: ~40% non-completion, debt, and two years of forgone wages means zero premium, not 40% of a premium. CON's explicit concession in B4.1 that even with lower completion expected value favors college allowed PRO's close to dismantle the expected-value math, and CON never described the marginal non-completer at 50 or answered capture and wage-from-day-one. Employer co-investment and portable licensed credentials answered the plateau and exploitation pressures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A2/A4 marginal-student critique — that subsidies buy enrollment/attempts not degrees, so B's 40-year compounding curve doesn't apply to the induced student, while apprenticeships bank wages, tenure and a credential every year.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the debate around the marginal student induced by subsidies, rather than the average graduate. While Side A relied heavily on the long-run college wage premium, Side B effectively argued that this premium does not apply to the marginal enrollee who is most likely to drop out with debt and no credential. Side B's closing speech decisively dismantled Side A's expected value defense by highlighting the catastrophic downside risk for non-completers, making Side B's "wages, not attempts" framework much more persuasive and grounded in the actual mechanics of the proposed policy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing attack on Side A's expected value argument. Side B brilliantly exposed the rhetorical and practical flaw in applying average graduate premiums to marginal dropouts, pointing out that a non-completer receives zero percent of the premium plus debt, effectively neutralizing Side A's primary mathematical defense.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by keeping the comparison on long-run adaptability and the marginal effects of expansion. It directly answered both pressure questions, exposed Side B's selective comparison between weak college outcomes and successful technical survivors, and repeatedly pressed whether subsidies create genuinely additional apprenticeship slots. Side B offered plausible fixed-cost and retention mechanisms, but relied heavily on asserted lifelong continuity, unsupported outcome specificity, and several invalid reference IDs. Its closing largely repeated the early-career compounding case without resolving occupational obsolescence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal was decisive: it challenged the bottom-quartile-versus-successful-specialist comparison as selection bias, then used Side B's own reliance on firm-specific integration to renew the portability and obsolescence pressure. Side B's fixed-cost answer was coherent, but A made the unresolved long-run risk and additionality questions more important to the motion.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON won the central clashes. The portable-vs-specific fork showed apprenticeship expansion either won't scale or won't stay valuable, and PRO's own answers conceded firm-specific retention. PRO's marginal-college debt/underemployment point was met by B's completion and snapshot-vs-lifetime reply and bottom-quartile vs survivor correction. On the word "expanding," college seats scale while apprenticeship slots depend on firms, leaving deadweight concerns unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1/Rebuttal 2 fork - that if credentials are portable firms won't fund them, if firm-specific they depreciate - and using A4.2's own "firm-specific integration" concession to resolve the fork for CON.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively exploiting the internal contradictions in Side B's case, particularly the "fork" between credential transferability and employer funding. Side A's argument that the firm-specific skills employers are willing to fund are exactly the ones that depreciate was highly persuasive and effectively neutralized Side B's long-run earnings claims. Furthermore, Side A's critique of the word "expanding"—noting that policy can scale college seats by removing price barriers but cannot artificially manufacture employer demand for apprenticeships—was a devastating point that Side B failed to overcome, ultimately conceding that subsidies would merely fill existing vacancies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it used Side B's own answer about "firm-specific integration" to prove that the skills employers fund are the ones that expire, brilliantly resolving the transferability fork in CON's favor.
