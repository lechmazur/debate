- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): PRO (A) built a cleaner, more durable case: it distinguished brain signals from other biometric/behavioral data on causal grounds (pre-choice, continuous, unfakeable), and it introduced a workable line between on-device closed-loop "core functionality" and third-party monetization that survived CON's challenge in the pressure round. Crucially, in Rebuttal 2 CON conceded that apps "don't strictly require third-party data sales to survive," and A seized on this concession in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing to reframe CON's affordability argument as a "decoy" — a point CON never squarely answered in its final speech. CON's smartwatch analogy and "efficiency, not exploitation" reframing were reasonable but never fully neutralized A's mechanism claim (that low cognitive-control states are commercially valuable precisely because they signal reduced resistance), and CON's closing largely re-asserted autonomy/affordability points rather than repairing the conceded ground. A also answered the Q1/Q2 format cleanly with A1:/A2: labels and used the answers to advance its case, while CON's answers, though present, didn't reclaim the initiative on the core-functionality distinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.5), which used CON's own admission that ad-supported sales aren't strictly necessary for app viability to dismantle the affordability defense and sharpen the core-functionality vs. third-party-monetization line — a point CON's final speech never adequately rebutted.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A narrowly won by exposing the proposition's overbreadth. Side B answered by relabeling such uses as core functionality and limiting the ban to external monetization, but that carveout is not in the motion. Side B was more rhetorically forceful and made the stronger case against neural advertising and vulnerability-based pricing, yet its model did not justify the categorical prohibition as written. Side A's affordability claims were under-supported, but its benign-personalization counterexample remained a decisive live route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange began with Side A's Q1 about distinguishing banned personalization from core neurofeedback. - **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won by cleanly resolving the line-drawing debate and successfully turning Side B's own examples against them. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): PRO opened with vivid framing (cognitive liberty, "line at the skull," neurological predation) but its central technical claim—that EEG is categorically different because it's "central nervous system" data—was directly and effectively dismantled by CON's point that consumer EEG is still peripheral, low-fidelity, and less predictive than existing behavioral signals like scroll speed or camera data. The decisive exchange came when PRO, pressed on enforcement, pivoted to a "pipeline governance" solution (ban ingestion at the source rather than auditing price outputs). CON immediately showed this reintroduces the exact audit problem PRO had used to dismiss outcome-based regulation: proving forbidden neural data entered a black-box pipeline requires the same detection capability PRO called impossible. PRO's closing never resolved this self-contradiction, simply reasserting the pipeline-ban framing without answering CON's point that it "widens" rather than solves the enforcement problem. CON also maintained a consistent, well-supported proportionality argument (subsidized accessibility vs. medical-device walls) and consistency argument (genetic/location data already permitted under similar consent standards) that PRO never fully neutralized. CON's answers to pressure questions were more directly responsive, while PRO's answers, though clearly labeled, didn't escape the internal tension CON exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing showing that PRO's "pipeline governance" enforcement mechanism requires the same impossible black-box audit that PRO itself used to argue outcome-based regulation was unworkable—this self-contradiction was never resolved by PRO.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by making proportionality and actual inferential power decisive. Side B repeatedly asserted that consumer EEG reveals pre-conscious intent and bypasses rationality, but did not adequately answer A’s point that scalp signals may be noisy and less revealing than existing behavioral proxies. B’s pipeline-audit distinction was persuasive, yet its blanket prohibition remained underjustified because it swept in benign personalization and treated anatomy itself as the principle of sensitivity. A’s subsidy claims were speculative, but its consent analogies, low-fidelity comparison, and narrower-regulation alternative better defeated the categorical motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to the “central nervous system” argument was most decisive: it reframed sensitivity around reliable inferential power rather than where a signal originates, then argued that B’s manipulation scenario depended on capabilities not established for consumer EEG.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by systematically answering Side B's pressure questions with sharp, grounded distinctions. A's differentiation between static data (genetics) and the real-time mechanics of choice (brain data) resolved the consent clash, while A's distinction between data pipeline governance and transaction-level AI explainability neutralized B's enforcement turn.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A4.1 and A4.2 directly dismantled B's two pressure questions by introducing principled distinctions (real-time choice vs static blueprint, and pipeline flow vs black-box outputs) that B failed to collapse in the closing.
