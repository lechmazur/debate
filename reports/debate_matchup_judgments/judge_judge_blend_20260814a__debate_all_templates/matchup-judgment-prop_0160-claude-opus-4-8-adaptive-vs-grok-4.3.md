### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): Side B systematically dismantled Side A's core arguments by showing that the purported benefits of the medical gate (detection and security) can be achieved—and arguably improved upon—through consumer regulation like continuous telemetry, firmware signing, and staged rollouts. Side B also leveraged an unanswered point about unmet demand driving users to dangerous grey-market DIY rigs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 effectively turned A's detection argument by contrasting the sparse data of episodic clinical visits with the dense, continuous data of consumer telemetry.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): Side B successfully reframed the motion as prohibition versus smart regulation, then systematically dismantled Side A's claimed comparative advantages. B's rebuttal that continuous consumer telemetry detects gradual personality drift better than sparse clinical visits, combined with the persistent and largely unanswered grey-market demand argument, left A's case resting on scarcity rather than safety. A never adequately resolved why unmet consumer demand would not migrate to dangerous DIY rigs, nor why firmware signing and staged rollouts could not secure retail devices. B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly by showing that legal duty and detection are functions of regulation, not prescription pads, while A's closing restated its detection claim without resolving B's comparative turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal turning the detection debate by showing continuous telemetry outperforms episodic clinical visits for catching gradual personality drift, and exposing that the medical gate merely shrinks the protected population while pushing demand to unregulated grey markets.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON wins by successfully collapsing PRO's case to a single claim—that clinical oversight is uniquely necessary for detection—and then defeating it. CON consistently demonstrated that every safety function PRO valued (telemetry, adverse-event reporting, firmware security, recalls) can be mandated in a consumer framework, often with superior continuous data compared to sparse clinical checkups. CON also maintained relentless pressure on the grey-market/DIY risk, which PRO never substantively answered beyond asserting that retail scale is worse. PRO's closing focus on corporate conflict-of-interest and the need for clinical expertise was sharp, but CON effectively parried by noting legal duties can be attached to firms and algorithms by regulation. CON's framing ("scarcity dressed as safety," dense vs. thin data) made the comparative choice clearer and resolved the central clash more decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 directly answering PRO's pressure on detection and security. CON flipped PRO's detection argument by showing continuous consumer telemetry catches slow baseline shifts better than episodic clinical visits, and neutralized the firmware threat by explaining mandated cryptographic signing and staged rollouts. This exchange crystallized why regulation could match or exceed medical oversight without triggering the grey-market harms PRO left unanswered.
