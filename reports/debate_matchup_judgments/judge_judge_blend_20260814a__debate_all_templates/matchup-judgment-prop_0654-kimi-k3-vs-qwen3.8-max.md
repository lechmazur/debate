### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but PRO more effectively converted the pressure-question exchange into decisive leverage. When pressed on what detects a 2 a.m. mid-flood failure, CON's answer ("event-triggered responses—flood-stage thresholds, remote hydraulic gauges, closure protocols") was functionally a description of sensor-based monitoring, and PRO's closing seized on this to argue CON had conceded the core mechanism it spent four speeches opposing. CON's rebuttal that this was "targeted, not universal" was a fair defense but arrived reactively rather than preempting the damage. PRO also sustained a clear asymmetry frame throughout (visible/fixable sensor failures vs. invisible/fatal structural failures) that made the comparative stakes easy to grasp, and turned CON's own examples (I-35W) back into an argument for embedding sensors at construction time. CON's strongest point—that PRO's flagship evidence involved old infrastructure while the motion governs only new assets—was a real mismatch that PRO never fully resolved beyond "today's new bridge is tomorrow's old one," but CON never fully closed the loop on how their "risk-based" system differs in substance from continuous monitoring once specifics were demanded. On balance, PRO answered pressure more decisively and closed with a cleaner, more memorable line of attack, giving it a modest but real edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question (2 a.m. mid-flood detection) and CON's answer describing event-triggered flood-stage thresholds and remote gauges, which PRO then used in closing to argue CON had effectively conceded the need for continuous sensor-like monitoring.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side B won by consistently tying the debate back to the specific burdens of the motion (universal application, "main reliance") and successfully neutralizing Side A's core examples. Side B demonstrated that Side A's primary example of inspection failure (I-35W) was actually a governance failure to act on known data, which sensors would not solve.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 response regarding I-35W, which decisively showed that the tragedy was caused by a failure to act on a known deficiency rather than a lack of detection data.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B made the blind-interval case vivid, but Side A kept the debate anchored to the proposition as written: all new assets and main reliance on embedded sensors. A showed universal mandate creates an 80-year electronics liability, downgrades contextual visual checks, and diverts resources from existing deficient assets. B's scale-down to "tilt nodes" and "sensors as primary but inspectors remain" collapsed toward A's risk-based position, and B never solved lifecycle/power-loss or governance-chain objections — that sensors also need maintenance, power, and action, the same chain that failed on I-35W.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.4 answered the 2 a.m. flood pressure by conceding targeted event-triggered gauges where consequence demands, then turned B's I-35W example: failure was lack of repair triggers, not lack of sensors on new construction, and embedded sensors would still require the same maintained, powered, interpreted chain.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's argumentative arc was sharper and more decisive. B's opening established a strong overbreadth/opportunity-cost frame and, crucially, introduced the lifecycle-mismatch argument (sensors degrade in 10-15 years while dams/bridges last a century, so the mandate protects the healthy decades and lapses before the dangerous ones). A's attempts to answer this ("modular replacement," "accessible conduits," "replaceable pods") were reasonable engineering fixes, but B turned these directly into a devastating meta-point in Rebuttal 2 and Closing: PRO's defense survives only by rewriting the universal mandate into risk-based, maintainable, targeted monitoring — i.e., CON's own position. A never directly rebutted this "redrafting the motion" charge in Closing, leaving a live, unanswered route to victory for B. B's pressure questions were also more incisive, forcing A to concede design details that were then weaponized. A's timing/continuous-detection narrative remained clear and consistently argued, and its "false budget" rebuttal to B's opportunity-cost point was solid, but it did not fully neutralize the lifecycle and funding-gap critique or the motte-and-bailey charge. B's Rebuttal 2 was clipped for length, a minor execution cost, but the substantive content still landed intact before the cut.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2/Closing move labeling A's answers to the Pressure Questions (modular replacement, accessible conduits) as a "redrafting" of the universal mandate into CON's own risk-based framework — a charge A never directly answered in its final speech.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by decisively exploiting the specific wording of the prompt ("all new", "embedded"). B's argument that sensors monitor the healthy decades of a new asset but expire before the dangerous decades was highly effective, and A's attempt to solve this via modular replacement allowed B to solidify the claim that the mandate creates an unfunded, permanent liability for every minor asset.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal and subsequent pressure round, establishing the lifespan mismatch (sensors die at year 12, bridges fail at year 40), which cornered A into defending expensive perpetual replacement cycles.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON won the decisive scope-and-lifecycle clash. PRO's "today's new is tomorrow's old" was turned into "monitors the healthy decades and expires before the dangerous ones" - a 12-year sensor vs 40-year risk mismatch PRO only patched by re-describing the mandate as replaceable pods/accessible conduits, which CON framed as conceding risk-based engineering. PRO never justified universal coverage of every low-risk culvert nor funded century-long replacement, and its timing advantage was answered by targeted post-event tools (sonar, ultrasonics, piezometers). Second-rebuttal clipping hurt CON execution but core route survived; PRO's universal floor stayed under-justified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1 / A4.3 - CON's self-destruct rebuttal that embedding monitors the healthy decades and dies before the dangerous ones, and that PRO's modular-replacement defense rewrites the blanket mandate into the risk-based position CON defended from opening.
