PRO's closing memorable line ("a tool that can be audited but never has to be is just a black box with better marketing") crystallized the existing clash cleanly rather than introducing new material. CON's closing repeated its core argument without resolving the contradiction. Overall, PRO showed tighter progression across rounds, answered pressure questions more directly, and left a live, unrefuted logical path to the vote.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B won primarily by forcing and maintaining the correct comparative frame throughout: the question is not "algorithm vs. fairness" but "algorithm with an auditable paper trail vs. human bias that vanishes into 'culture fit.'" This comparison was introduced in B's opening and PRO never neutralized it. Side B also consistently exploited PRO's tendency to collapse the entire category onto emotion-reading micro-expression AI, which is only one narrow subset of the motion's scope. The pressure questions round sharpened this: B's Q2 caught a real internal tension in PRO's position (automated work-sample simulations might fall within the motion), and B's Rebuttal 2 effectively documented PRO's progressive retreat—from "fundamentally pseudoscientific" across the whole category to carving out skills tests and conceding personality inventories have reliability evidence worth only "heavily restricting." This narrowing was an execution cost PRO never recovered from, because it implicitly validated B's core claim that regulation can distinguish good tools from bad ones, which is exactly the work a blanket ban refuses to do. PRO's best live argument—that validation is "easily gamed" and cannot transmute pseudoscience into validity—had real force but was incompletely developed. B answered it by distinguishing criterion-related validity (scores must predict job performance) from mere demographic balance, and by pointing out that the gaming concern applies equally to pharmaceutical trials, where the answer is stricter protocols, not prohibition. PRO's closing repeated "you cannot validate a tool that measures the wrong thing" without engaging B's response that validation is precisely the mechanism for identifying which tools measure the wrong thing. B's closing, though clipped, directly crystallized the live clash: a ban doesn't improve enforcement, it removes the only tools whose criteria are testable while sending employers back to unauditable human processes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal [B4.5–B4.7] was the most decisive exchange. It documented PRO's progressive case-narrowing in real time—from opening claim that the entire category is "fundamentally pseudoscientific" [A1.2], to carving out skills tests [A4.2], to conceding personality inventories have reliability evidence warranting only heavy restriction [A4.1]. B then crystallized the implication: "identifying which tools are pseudoscientific and which are valid is exactly the regulatory work a ban refuses to do." PRO kept redescribing a broad prohibition as precision targeting, and B exposed that the precision was retroactive and unprincipled.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A wins by effectively leveraging the broad scope of the proposition to show that a ban would destroy valid, non-pseudoscientific tools alongside bad ones. Side A also successfully argued that the scalable fallback to these tools is biased resume screening, which Side B failed to adequately answer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's explanation of criterion-related validity in A4.1, proving that validation tests for predictive performance, meaning true pseudoscience would fail the audit without requiring a blanket ban.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A wins because it consistently exposed the motion's overbreadth, defended the viability of validation and audit mechanisms against PRO's narrowing redefinitions, and showed that the realistic fallback to a ban restores un-auditable human gatekeeping. PRO's case weakened through repeated scope concessions and failure to answer the scalability/access comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.6 exchange on work-sample carve-outs and motion scope, which crystallized PRO's inconsistency and A's regulatory alternative.
