### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): This was a genuine, competitive clash. CON's core reframe—that the real choice is "governed, reviewable prediction versus secret managerial speculation"—created a persistent problem for PRO throughout. The "records vs. rumors" formulation in CON's closing is the single strongest line in the debate and was never fully answered. However, PRO's live route to victory was ultimately more durable for three reasons. First, the invisible-penalty problem: when a manager quietly withholds a project because a dashboard flags an employee as a 70% flight risk, no disclosure regime will capture that ambient non-decision, and CON's repeated answer of "require disclosure" never closed that gap. Second, PRO's Q2 in the pressure round was especially sharp—asking why a company needs a model specifically calibrated to detect organizing likelihood rather than general dissatisfaction. CON's A2 that satisfaction and organizing propensity differ is technically true but actually cuts against CON, implying the tool is specifically tracking protected activity rather than workplace conditions. Third, CON suffered execution costs from two clipped speeches, both in rounds where the full argument mattered most. PRO's "AI laundering" concept—that algorithmic outputs gain a mathematical authority that human hunches never do, making contestation harder rather than easier—was a direct answer to CON's auditability argument and was never convincingly neutralized. On balance, PRO's central principle (workers judged on actual conduct, not invisible algorithmic profiling) held together more coherently under pressure, and the invisible-penalty argument remained a live, unresolved clash at the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 and Q2 pressure questions, and CON's inability to fully answer them. Q1 (how does a worker appeal an opportunity they never knew they lost?) exposed the hollow core of CON's "require disclosure" solution—ambient non-decisions are structurally invisible to any disclosure regime. Q2 (why would a benevolent employer need a unionization-likelihood model rather than a satisfaction survey?) forced CON to concede the tool tracks something distinct from general grievance, which is the organizing drive itself—precisely the protected activity PRO argued was being surveilled.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): CON wins by showing the ban is overbroad—it blocks clearly benevolent uses like retention outreach and burnout prevention—while remaining underinclusive because supervisors can still make identical predictions invisibly. PRO’s best objections, invisible ambient penalties and union busting, were met by a coherent regulatory alternative (disclosure, logs, and penalties for adverse use), leaving PRO without a harm unique to AI that demands total prohibition rather than governance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s reply that banning AI merely shifts forecasting from logged, reviewable models to undocumented managerial “hunches,” turning PRO’s “authority of math” concern into an argument for transparency rules rather than prohibition.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by consistently framing the choice as auditable, governed AI versus opaque human instinct, and by effectively turning Side A's core "invisible penalty" argument. While A made a strong principled case about algorithmic determinism and corporate incentives, B exposed the ban's overbreadth (conceded by A on burnout tools) and underinclusivity (human managers still predict informally). B's closing turn—that if hidden non-decisions are the real harm, banning the logged tool while tolerating secret managerial bias is counterproductive—provided the cleaner comparative path. B also handled pressure questions directly and maintained tighter progression across rounds, despite minor clipping penalties.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to the "invisible penalty" clash. When A argued workers cannot appeal opportunities they never knew they lost, B turned this by showing that human managerial bias creates the exact same invisible losses with zero audit trail, making a transparency-and-logs regime strictly superior to a ban that only removes the reviewable mechanism. This turn crystallized the debate's central tradeoff and neutralized A's strongest offensive point.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built a more durable path to victory and defended it well across rounds. Three live routes survived to the end: (1) the unionization prediction point — CON never convincingly explained why any employer legitimately needs an individual-level AI score for "likelihood to organize" rather than simply improving systemic conditions; A's Q1 exposed this gap and CON's A1 in Rebuttal 2 answered only the general distress-diagnosis rationale without touching the individual targeting problem; (2) the "prediction makes punishment deniable" problem — CON's most interesting counter was the digital paper trail argument introduced in Rebuttal 2 (AI creates auditable records that help prove retaliation), but PRO had already framed why that fails: the adverse action (fewer shifts, withheld promotion) can be traced to a dozen other business reasons, and the score itself doesn't constitute actionable retaliation under current law; (3) the "alternatives exist" argument — PRO consistently showed that every benign use CON named (burnout detection, early retention intervention, talent development) can be achieved through universal policies, team-level analysis, or current observable performance, so the hidden individual score is not necessary for support and is distinctively useful only for selective, deniable control. CON scored genuine points with the auditable-AI-vs.-unauditable-human-judgment contrast and the paper trail reversal, and the retention economics framing was plausible. But CON leaned too hard on the repeated "cynical assumption" phrase without squarely neutralizing the unionization targeting problem or the opacity-makes-enforcement-toothless argument. The clipped Rebuttal 2 was a small execution cost for PRO, but the content visible before the clip was substantive and already answered both pressure questions directly. A's closing crystallized the decisive clash cleanly; B's closing largely recapitulated earlier points without sharpening the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Pressure Question Q2 — asking how a worker is supposed to prove the algorithm drove a decision when the harm appears as fewer shifts or withheld training — was the most consequential exchange. It directly exposed the fatal weakness in CON's "punish the action, not the prediction" framework. CON's response in Rebuttal 2 (A2: algorithms create a digital paper trail that aids legal discovery) was the debate's most original counter-move, but PRO's closing absorbed it cleanly: even a documented score doesn't constitute retaliation, and linking that score to a deniable downstream adverse action remains practically impossible for the worker. That asymmetry — employer has the score and the discretion; worker has neither — was never resolved by CON and is what ultimately decides the round.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by establishing that individual AI predictions create hidden, unchallengeable inferences that enable deniable discrimination and surveillance of protected collective action. A cleanly rebutted B's false dichotomy between AI and human intuition by showing workable alternatives (team-level analysis, universal support, observable criteria), and A's unionization argument—that predicting individual organizing likelihood is inherently abusive—was never adequately answered by B. B's best turns on auditability and proactive retention were plausible but did not overcome A's core framing that the prediction itself is the harm when workers cannot see or contest their scores. A minor execution cost applies because A's Rebuttal 2 was clipped, but the closing recovered the essential clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal that the employer's incentive structure permits both "supportive" and punitive uses of the same hidden score, which makes "punish the action, not the prediction" toothless because the worker never sees the label and the harm appears as ordinary managerial decisions (fewer shifts, stalled promotions).
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by consistently grounding the debate in workplace power dynamics and the inherent opacity of algorithmic scoring. A successfully dismantled B's core premise that AI predictions naturally lead to proactive support, showing how hidden scores enable deniable opportunity-rationing and self-fulfilling prophecies. A also drew a clean, workable line between banned individual forecasts and permitted team-level or current-performance tools, directly answering B's workability pressure. B's case relied heavily on optimistic assumptions about corporate incentives and the legally/practically naive claim that proprietary algorithms create easily discoverable "paper trails" for workers. A's framing of unionization prediction as an inherent surveillance of protected collective rights remained largely unanswered, giving A the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 directly neutralized B's "auditability and paper trail" defense by explaining how opaque scores buried in routine management decisions make legal discovery practically useless for workers, while simultaneously offering concrete, non-predictive alternatives (team-level analysis, universal mentoring, transparent criteria) that preserved B's stated goal of proactive support without crossing the rights line.
