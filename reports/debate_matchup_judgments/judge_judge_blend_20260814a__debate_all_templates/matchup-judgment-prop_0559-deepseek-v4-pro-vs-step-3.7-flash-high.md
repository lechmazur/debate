### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Both sides made substantive arguments throughout, but PRO (B) built the slightly cleaner path to victory across the full exchange. PRO's structural diagnosis — lock-in is real, voluntary efforts like the Data Transfer Project have failed, so a legal mandate is necessary — was maintained coherently and never genuinely dismantled. On the two main sub-clashes: (1) Competition: PRO's Q2 was the most pointed question in the debate, asking why a uniform interoperable standard raises barriers compared to today's costly, fragmented API access. CON's A2 response (startups can delay integration without the mandate) is plausible but incomplete — it concedes that without portability, the startup is just a ghost town that cannot attract an existing network, which is the very problem the mandate solves. PRO won this sub-argument on balance. (2) Privacy: CON's domestic violence survivor example was the strongest concrete moment in the debate, and it carried real weight. But PRO's Rebuttal 2 A1 answer — proposing a specific pre-transfer notification and 14-day opt-out mechanism and clarifying that only the bare connection list (not interaction history or shared content) transfers — was a genuine design response that materially reduced the force of the objection rather than just reasserting position. CON's counter that a real legislature would mandate a fuller transfer is a reasonable concern, but it relies on a pessimistic implementation assumption that PRO credibly contests. CON's case was well-grounded and the third-party privacy concern is real, but CON's competition reversal argument was partially undercut by PRO's pressure question, and the privacy argument, while not neutralized, was answered with a credible safeguard mechanism rather than silence. PRO's case thus had two live routes to victory against CON's one, giving it a narrow but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 in the Pressure Questions round — asking how a single interoperable standard raises entry barriers compared to today's fragmented, unpredictable API access — was the most decisive exchange. It exposed a genuine tension in CON's competition argument that CON never fully resolved in Rebuttal 2, where the answer (startups can scale integration to their growth) glosses over the fact that without portability, there is no network to scale into, undermining the core premise of the competition reversal claim.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON wins: stronger clash on third-party privacy override (sensitive examples stick) and fixed-cost barriers to startups; PRO relies on unspecified “well-designed” safeguards and a weak phone-number analogy that CON damages. Clipped openings and rebuttals hurt both but hit PRO harder on execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2 and A4 on privacy override and cost structure, directly neutralizing B’s consent and competition claims.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): A built the stronger live route to victory by maintaining a coherent, textually grounded case that lock-in is real and that a narrowly scoped mandate—with notification, opt-out, and bare-connection limits—can break it without the systemic harms B alleged. B’s privacy warnings relied on asserting that any mandate would necessarily include interaction history and sensitive context, but A successfully framed that as an unsupported caricature no serious law would enact. B’s strongest point, the vulnerable-user examples, remained a live edge-case concern, yet A’s pre-transfer opt-out mechanism provided a direct, plausible answer that B never proved would be infeasible. On costs, A’s standardization logic undermined B’s fragmented-compliance narrative, and B never squarely explained why replacing multiple proprietary APIs with one interoperable interface raises rather than lowers startup barriers. A’s progression stayed focused on the core clash, answered pressure directly with required A1/A2 labels, and crystallized the false trade-off effectively in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s rebuttal exposing that B’s compliance-cost argument contradicted itself—if the mandate is truly interoperable, entrants build once rather than negotiating fragmented proprietary APIs—and that B’s privacy nightmare assumed a maximal data breadth no sensible legislature would include.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a competitive but clear debate on balance. PRO's strongest sustained argument was the market-failure/lock-in case, grounded in the phone-number portability precedent that CON never fully dismantled—CON correctly noted the structural difference between an identifier and a relational web, but PRO's point that network-effect lock-in is the decisive barrier to competition remained live throughout. PRO also landed the most damaging single move of the debate in its Q1: "platforms already sell bulk connection graphs to advertisers without any individual consent—why is user-controlled opt-in portability worse?" CON's A1 response (current sharing is aggregated/pseudonymous, not structured) is defensible but was asserted rather than established, and it ceded significant moral ground. CON's best arguments—non-consenting contacts in follower lists, and the abuse-network migration problem (an abuser exports their own follower list including victims)—were genuine and partially live at the end. PRO's pre-blocking response to the abuse point was reasonably answered by CON ("requires perfect cross-platform identity resolution"), so that sub-clash narrowly favors CON. But PRO successfully reframed the overall comparison: speculative worst-case harms from the mandate against the guaranteed, ongoing harm of unregulated lock-in and unregulated ad-data sharing. PRO's tiered-compliance answer to the startup-burden pressure was direct and adequate. CON's innovation argument was clipped and never completed. The asymmetry framing in PRO's closing crystallized the core comparative case more cleanly than CON's closing, which, while crisp on relational-web vs. identifier, relied on points that were already partially absorbed by PRO's responses. Both sides followed format requirements, so no structural penalties differentiate them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question—exposing that CON's privacy concern ignores the far less regulated existing practice of platforms selling user connection graphs to advertisers—was the most damaging exchange. CON's response (aggregated vs. structured) was the right instinct but was underdeveloped and unestablished, leaving the moral asymmetry live for the rest of the debate and weakening CON's credibility as a privacy champion.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B wins: stronger defense of user rights and competition via the phone analogy, clearer contrast to unregulated status-quo harms, and direct answers to pressure questions on privacy without evasion; Side A lands solid critiques on relational data and abuse pipelines but clips and weaker weighing leave its burdens less resolved overall.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2 answers on tiered thresholds and downstream retention limits, turning CON’s privacy claim back on the unregulated ad-sharing status quo.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B wins by establishing that mandated social-graph transfer inherently treats relational webs as individual property, exposing non-consenting contacts and creating systemic abuse risks that user-side consent cannot sanitize. B sharply turned A's GDPR analogy against A and, in the decisive exchange on harassment, showed that abusers could export their own follower lists to follow victims regardless of the victim's export choices, materially undermining A's pre-blocking defense. A remained competitive on competition and autonomy, but B's relational-privacy framing created a persistent, unresolved burden that A's appeals to tiered compliance and downstream deletion rules could not fully discharge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second-rebuttal answer (A2) demonstrating that an abuser exporting his own follower list would carry the victim and her contacts to a new platform even if the victim exported zero connections, gutting A's claim that selective export and pre-blocking could protect refugees.
