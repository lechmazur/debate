### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): A wins because it preserved the core priority case: services and credentials face large duplicative barriers, and even conditional mutual recognition can remove far more friction than another round of mostly residual tariff cuts. B effectively exposed overstatement in A’s early “seamless” examples and landed strong hits on GDPR/Washington Accord, but A’s later EU professional-qualifications answer gave a workable model with safeguards that neutralized the race-to-bottom dilemma. B’s tariff alternative remained underdeveloped once A showed that the cited high tariffs are themselves politically entrenched and not obviously the quick wins B promised.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer to B’s harmonization/arbitrage dilemma was decisive: compensatory measures and host-state safeguards showed mutual recognition need not mean either full harmonization or unsafe dilution, while still avoiding full requalification.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won by successfully proving that mutual recognition offers higher economic payoffs and defending its feasibility. While Side A landed a brilliant rhetorical blow by framing B's reliance on "safeguards" as a broken promise of seamlessness, this essentially conceded that mutual recognition is safely achievable. Furthermore, B effectively neutralized A's main advantage—that tariff cuts are "quick wins"—by pointing out that A's own high-value examples (agricultural tariffs) have been bogged down by lobbies for decades.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was strategically brilliant in weaponizing Side B's explanation of safeguards into a "collapsed value proposition," though Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing ultimately survived the hit by contextualizing the massive relative gains of avoiding full requalification.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): PRO won by showing that mutual recognition with safeguards—exemplified by the EU Professional Qualifications Directive—is a feasible, large-scale model that delivers larger economic gains than marginal tariff cuts. CON’s critique that compensatory measures make recognition conditional rather than seamless was absorbed and effectively reframed by PRO in closing: avoiding full requalification is transformative enough to warrant priority. Meanwhile, CON’s claim that tariff cuts offer quick, reliable wins was undermined by the empirical persistence of double-digit agricultural duties despite decades of negotiations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over the EU Professional Qualifications Directive: PRO used it in Rebuttal 2 to prove mutual recognition works without harmonization via compensatory measures, while CON countered that such conditions negate the promised seamlessness; PRO’s closing resolved this by showing conditional recognition still vastly outweighs marginal tariff gains.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): B won by making the central comparison clearer: tariff cuts still affect broad consumer prices and labor-intensive sectors, while mutual recognition is likely to remain narrow, opaque, and captured by professional gatekeepers. A’s strongest point was that tariff peaks show political limits, but B answered that mutual recognition faces even more concentrated resistance and lacks the mass domestic constituency of consumers, importers, and retailers. A’s claim that prioritization would magically broaden recognition deals was plausible but under-proven.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered A’s political-stalemate argument by identifying the domestic coalition behind tariff cuts and contrasting it with regulator-led, guild-friendly mutual recognition processes.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A won the central clash on political economy. Side B argued that tariff cuts have stalled because they lack a domestic constituency, but Side A successfully pointed out that lower prices mobilize a massive coalition of consumers, retailers, and importers. Side B ignored this point in closing. Additionally, Side A effectively countered Side B's claim that prioritization would organically expand mutual recognition deals by pointing to the structural reality that gatekeeping regulators negotiate them, citing the EU as a grounded example.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A1 and A2) cleanly dismantled Side B's political economy claims by introducing the consumer/importer constituency and using the EU example to prove that prioritizing mutual recognition does not automatically prevent guild capture.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO wins because CON concedes that peak tariffs survive due to entrenched protectionism yet still advocates prioritizing the same stalled tool, whereas PRO offers a coherent theory of change for mutual recognition: reciprocity creates domestic professional beneficiaries who counterbalance guild capture, while tariff cuts only aid foreign producers with no inside political voice. CON also never engages the digital-services half of the motion, where regulatory—not tariff—barriers are the primary obstacle, leaving PRO the cleaner comparative path on the fastest-growing trade frontier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's pressure questions on political economy (A4.1–A4.2): the argument that mutual recognition generates a domestic pro-opening constituency through reciprocity, whereas tariff cuts benefit foreign producers who lack political voice inside the bloc. This reframed feasibility and undercut CON's claim that tariff cuts possess the superior coalition.
