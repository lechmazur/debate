### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins on the decisive clashes. First, the mandate question: CON's consistent argument that descriptive resemblance is not democratic authorization—"a microcosm can resemble the public; it is still not the public"—was never squarely answered. PRO fell back on "equal chance of being selected," which CON correctly identified as not equivalent to actual political voice. Second, the accountability clash: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer that "accountability rests in the process" and "future bodies can amend mistakes" gave the game away, and CON's closing crystallized this perfectly—accountability requires removability after failure, and "someone else can fix it later" is an admission that legitimacy still flows from accountable institutions outside the assembly. Third, CON's use of Ireland was analytically sharper: the assemblies gained legitimacy precisely because they fed into an all-electorate referendum, not binding small-group authority. PRO used Ireland to show deliberation works, which CON never disputed; the dispute was about finality of that deliberation. CON's pressure questions were more penetrating than PRO's, especially the escape-hatch question exposing the contradiction in PRO's "expert oversight" answer. PRO had real substance—the advisory-only problem is genuine, and the symmetry argument on expert influence in legislatures lands partially—but PRO never resolved the core mandate and accountability deficits. Both of CON's rebuttals were clipped, which is a real execution cost, but the visible portions contained stronger argumentative content than PRO's complete responses. CON's closing crystallized the debate more cleanly, turning PRO's own answers into evidence for the CON position.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's pressure question exposing PRO's "expert oversight and review mechanisms" escape hatch: if oversight bodies can alter the assembly's decision, it is not binding; if they cannot, no one is accountable when policy fails. PRO's answer—comparing it to courts reviewing laws—was a weak analogy (courts apply established law rather than make contested binding policy), and CON correctly pressed that "accountability rests in process" is not political accountability at all. This exchange stripped PRO of its primary fallback for the accountability problem and left its binding-power claim without a credible remedy for errors.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by maintaining that descriptive representation does not equal democratic authorization to make binding law. A exposed a fatal tension in PRO's account: expert oversight and future amendment either make the assembly non-binding or outsource real accountability to elected institutions, undermining the case for sovereignty by sortition. A also turned PRO's Ireland example against it, showing legitimacy flowed from referendum ratification rather than the mini-public alone. B delivered a spirited case for deliberative quality but never bridged the gap from 'better informed' to 'rightfully binding,' leaving the mandate question unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question and follow-up rebuttal on expert oversight, which forced PRO into a dilemma: if oversight bodies can alter assembly decisions, the assembly is not truly binding; if they cannot, no one is politically accountable for policy backfires. PRO's resort to 'process legitimacy' and 'future amendment' conceded that real accountability lies outside the assembly, effectively collapsing the case for binding power.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by decisively controlling the accountability clash. While Side B (PRO) effectively highlighted the gridlock and distortions of elected politics, it failed to justify granting binding power to an unaccountable, temporary body. PRO's rebuttals to pressure questions—claiming accountability "rests in the process" and that "future democratic bodies can amend" bad decisions—directly undermined the case for binding authority, playing into CON's framing that advisory roles capture deliberative benefits without severing power from democratic consent. CON's sharp distinctions between descriptive representation and a mandate to rule, along with its effective turn of the Ireland example, provided a cleaner, more resilient path to victory. Side A's clipped speeches are a minor execution cost but do not offset its analytical dominance on the core tradeoff.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 exchange where it dismantles PRO's accountability concessions. By pointing out that "future bodies can amend" admits real legitimacy comes from outside the assembly, and that "expert oversight" creates a contradiction (either it's not truly binding or the public bears unaccountable risk), CON collapsed PRO's defensive framework and crystallized the decisive tradeoff between deliberative quality and democratic removability.
