### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate, primarily because of complete speech delivery throughout versus CON's repeated clipping in the opening, both rebuttals, and closing—each clip cutting off synthesis moments that might have landed decisive blows. PRO also grounded its case in concrete plausible examples (Memento Park, Berlin's Spandau Citadel) that made the preservation-with-context thesis tangible, while CON relied more on abstract reasoning. PRO's permanence asymmetry argument—you can improve curation later but cannot un-smash a monument—was live at the close and never truly neutralized; CON called it a "preservation reflex," which was clever but not analytically sufficient. PRO's "captive not idol" framing, though more rhetorical than analytical, was memorable and unmatched in the closing exchange. CON's best moves were analytically sharper: the "same property" argument (the visceral power that makes preservation valuable is the same power that makes it risky), the "teaches two things" point (destruction communicates both the injustice and the repudiation simultaneously), and the curation capacity challenge that cornered PRO into conceding "basic storage" suffices. PRO never fully answered the same-property argument analytically, relying on "captive" framing instead. But those CON arguments were either partially clipped or incompletely crystallized. PRO consistently met its burden—showing relocation with context preserves options and unique evidentiary weight—while CON's live route (records plus victim-centered replacement memorials serve better) remained compelling but underdeveloped at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The sharpest exchange was PRO's Q1 contradiction charge—if photos and fragments are safe for education, why does the whole contextualized object become dangerous?—met by CON's A1 distinction that the whole monument carries aesthetic force, scale, and collectible symbolism that documentation does not, and thus centering the full honor-object risks perpetuating its dominance even under glass. CON's answer was analytically sound and resolved the contradiction charge cleanly. However, PRO never fully answered the underlying "same property" implication: if the object's visceral power is what makes it uniquely educational, that same power is what makes continued custody risky. This remained the most live unresolved analytical tension in the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the stronger path to victory by maintaining a consistent, coherent narrative: controversial monuments in museums become educational captives rather than civic idols, and destroying them removes the visceral evidence of past oppression, inadvertently aiding historical sanitization. Side B effectively answered Side A’s pressure questions about resource-limited communities by arguing that even basic preservation maintains option value, whereas destruction is permanent. Side A (CON) landed strong conceptual blows—particularly the argument that monuments are engineered to dominate attention and that destruction itself constitutes democratic repudiation—but suffered severe execution costs with four of five speeches clipped for exceeding word limits, leaving key comparative weighing unfinished. B’s rebuttal that a defaced, toppled monument is a permanent trophy of democratic triumph directly blunted A’s “centering the oppressor” concern, and B crystallized this clash more cleanly in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing argument that destroying monuments does the work of historical denialists by erasing the physical evidence of state-sponsored intimidation, while a relocated, defaced monument becomes a “captive” trophy of democratic victory—directly neutralizing Side A’s claim that preservation inherently perpetuates honor.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully turning PRO's core offensive point and problematizing the generalizability of museum relocation. When PRO argued that monuments are uniquely "visceral" and necessary to convey the scale of past oppression, CON effectively flipped this: that same visceral, attention-commanding power is exactly why preserving the object remains risky, continues to center the oppressor, and drains symbolic oxygen from victims. CON also pressed the "better in general" burden, noting that PRO's success relies on high-quality, well-funded curation that many communities lack. PRO's concession that even a "basic archive" suffices to preserve options played directly into CON's framing of relocation as a mere "preservation reflex" rather than a superior public service. While PRO landed strong rhetorical points about historical denialism and the "captive" statue, CON's comparative weighing on what actually ends a monument's social power and serves affected communities was sharper and more resilient under pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal turning PRO's "visceral scale" argument. PRO claimed the physical object's imposing size makes it an irreplaceable teaching tool; CON countered that this exact emotional potency makes the object bad raw material for public education because it inherently dominates attention and resists neutralization, meaning destruction better serves the public by refusing to keep the propaganda artifact at the center of civic memory.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built a more coherent, motion-focused case throughout the exchange. The decisive comparative argument—that relocation already achieves the moral condemnation CON cares about while additionally enabling richer historical understanding—was consistently maintained and never fully neutralized. Side B's strongest single claim, that physical monuments teach things reproductions cannot (scale, posture, pedestal design, the three-dimensional staging of authority), was introduced in the A2 answer and Side A never offered a strong counter beyond insisting archives are sufficient, which doesn't actually address the dimensionality point. Side A's best arguments—the "ongoing insult" resource burden and destruction as a liberation monument—were genuine but narrow, and Side B's prison/camp analogy at least partially answered the validation concern. Side A's shrine argument was effectively neutralized by Side B's response that extremists fetishize anything, shifting the question to where the broader public is better served. Side A's case also exhibited some logical tension (valuing an empty pedestal as meaningful while dismissing the intact monument as unnecessary), which Side B's Q1 exposed sharply. The repeated Nuremberg invocation gave Side A emotional force but became repetitive rather than advancing the comparative case. Multiple clippings of Side B's speeches were an execution cost, but the visible arguments were consistently stronger on the motion's actual standard: what serves the public better.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's A2 answer in Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7) directly responded to Side A's pressure question about the educational value of physical grandeur. Side B argued that monuments are built to dominate space, and scale, posture, pedestal design, materials, and placement are part of the politics—a photo tells you what it looked like, but the intact object lets the public grasp how authority was staged in three dimensions and why people found that staging intolerable. This directly answered Side A's repeated claim that archives and photographs are sufficient substitutes, and Side A's rebuttal never seriously engaged the dimensionality point, only reasserting that archives "preserve facts perfectly," which is a weaker response to a qualitatively different educational claim.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO)): CON more effectively undermined PRO’s core claim that museums neutrally "demote" monuments, showing that perpetual taxpayer-funded curation in cultural institutions constitutes ongoing validation and moral injury rather than neutral preservation. The distinction between genuine historical sites and manufactured propaganda idols blunted PRO’s "preservation is not praise" defense. Destruction as an active historical verdict, illustrated through compelling examples, proved more consonant with public service than eternal curation, and PRO’s three clipped speeches incurred real execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s distinction between actual historical sites (camps, prisons) and manufactured propaganda monuments, which removed the force from PRO’s argument that preservation implies no endorsement.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by establishing a cleaner comparative framework: relocation achieves moral condemnation by removing civic honor while preserving critical evidence, whereas destruction achieves condemnation only by subtracting knowledge. B effectively neutralized A's "museum as validation" claim by explaining how curatorial context transforms an object from tribute to specimen, and provided a concrete educational rationale (3D scale, staging of authority) that photos cannot replicate. A made a compelling moral case about the insult and cost of preservation, and effectively framed destruction as a historical act of liberation, but struggled to outweigh B's point that archives alone yield a thinner public memory. B's weighing ("condemnation without amnesia" vs. "condemnation by subtraction") directly resolved the motion's standard and remained the live route to victory after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to A's pressure question on taxpayer funding and museum "validation" (B4.1-4.2), where B cleanly separated practical conservation from moral endorsement by comparing it to preserving prisons and camps, and clarified that lighting and security are conditions for critical examination, not praise. This dismantled A's core premise that museums inherently honor what they house and secured B's comparative advantage.
