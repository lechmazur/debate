### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): B won the central implementation clash. A portrayed vouchers as merely a different logo on a reliable check, while B persuasively argued that they can carry distinct inspections, paperwork, delays, and contractual obligations. A's attempt to permit rejection for voucher-specific operational conflicts sat uneasily with its defense of banning voucher-based refusal and never supplied a clear limiting rule. A weighed tenant hardship effectively, but did not adequately justify imposing uncompensated program costs on landlords or answer the supply-exit concern. B's unsupported precision about delays and paperwork limits the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal most decisively exposed A's distinction between prohibited source discrimination and supposedly permissible operational objections: if the objection exists only because of mandatory voucher conditions, A never clearly explained why it is not rejection because of the voucher. A's later case-by-case answer did not resolve that contradiction.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side A won by successfully framing the voucher not just as a payment method, but as a binding, uncompensated government contract. Side A systematically dismantled Side B's counter-arguments, including a decisive takedown of the SNAP analogy, and exposed the flaw in Side B's claim that landlords could still reject for 'capacity' reasons.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal dismantling the SNAP/grocer analogy and exposing that rejecting a voucher due to its mandatory red tape is exactly what the motion bans.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): CON isolated the decisive clash more cleanly: the unavoidable overhead, inspections, delays, and one-sided terms always travel with the voucher, so a ban on rejection "solely because" of the payment method turns legitimate refusal of uncompensated burdens into illegal discrimination. PRO's retained-screening and "streamline bureaucracy" replies never neutralized this inseparability or the conscription critique, leaving CON's burden-of-proof and property-rights path more intact after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.2 (and follow-through in B4): exposing that PRO's "operational capacity" carve-out is illusory because the costs attach exclusively to every voucher and therefore fall under the motion's sole-reason prohibition.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won by establishing that vouchers impose program-level contractual and administrative conditions, making source-based rejection materially different from prejudice based on an applicant's personal identity. Side A never adequately answered rent ceilings and nonnegotiable regulatory burdens, and its empirical claims about every major city, exact acceptance increases, and statistically insignificant substitution effects were unsupported and overly precise. B's supply-backfire claim remained somewhat speculative, but its central compelled-contracting distinction survived.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest response was that individualized screening cannot resolve burdens that attach uniformly to the voucher program itself. This directly defeated A's analogy to age or ordinary tenant risk and exposed why guaranteed payment does not eliminate inspection, pricing, and enforcement costs.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won by locking the decisive clash: vouchers impose non-negotiable, program-level strings (inspections, rent ceilings, tripartite control, eviction delays) that make categorical refusal a business decision about the deal, not bias against the person. PRO's fair-housing analogy and "price it in" reply never fully neutralized that distinction or proved landlords can treat the strings as ordinary individual risk. PRO's later metrics on acceptance rates and no substitution were unsupported and brittle, while CON repeatedly exposed the metric gap without inventing sources. The cleaner comparative path favored CON after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1/B2.4 and later A1 answer: voucher as voluntary state contract rewriting the lease vs. immutable traits, plus acceptance rates failing to rule out substitution effects.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B wins by successfully distinguishing between immutable traits and voluntary participation in a regulatory regime, dismantling Side A's core discrimination analogy. Side B effectively highlights the economic realities of vouchers (rent caps, inspection delays), which Side A completely ignores in their closing by suggesting landlords simply "price it into the rent," failing to address that voucher rents are capped by the housing authority. Furthermore, Side A relies on suspiciously exact, unverifiable claims about "independent follow up surveys" in "every major city" showing no "statistically significant increases," violating the epistemic discipline rule against brittle fake precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where they systematically dismantle the race/gender analogy by distinguishing immutable traits from tripartite government contracts, and expose the flaw in Side A's acceptance rate metric by explaining substitution effects.
