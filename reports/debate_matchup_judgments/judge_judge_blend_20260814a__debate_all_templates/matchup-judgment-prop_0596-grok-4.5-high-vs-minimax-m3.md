### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Both sides built substantive, well-structured cases with real clash: PRO's equity/precedent argument versus CON's capital-formation and manufacturing-complexity argument. CON's opening and Rebuttal 1 were both flagged as clipped for exceeding the word limit, meaning key closing points in those speeches were never seen by the judge—a real execution cost under the stated rules, especially since those were the rounds meant to carry CON's core evidentiary architecture. PRO, by contrast, executed cleanly throughout, followed the A1:/A2: format precisely, and used its pressure question effectively: it exposed that CON's proposed alternatives (Medicaid, NHS, tiered pricing) do not reach patients in lower-income countries, and CON's answer to this (invoking 'capacity building' and the therapy's recent approval) was comparatively thin. CON's own pressure questions about the fragility of the 'essential'/'no close alternative' criteria were sharp and never fully neutralized, but PRO's answer (independent, criteria-bound review) was a plausible rebuttal that CON only reasserted rather than dismantled. On balance, PRO's cleaner execution, more complete use of its speech allocations, and sharper closing weighing (certain present exclusion vs. speculative future pipeline harm) gave it a clear, if competitive, edge over CON's more original but incompletely delivered case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to its own Q1 pressure question (A4.1–A4.4), which highlighted that CON's cited access mechanisms (Medicaid, NHS, assistance programs) never reach lower-income-country patients—a gap CON's answer only partially addressed with an underdeveloped 'tiered pricing and capacity building' claim.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully exposing the geographic limits of Side A's alternative access mechanisms (Medicaid/NHS) and maintaining strong safeguards (royalties, tech transfer) to blunt Side A's pipeline and manufacturing attacks. Side A argued well on the complexities of gene therapy production, but Side B effectively separated IP exclusivity from regulatory quality control.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 effectively used the Pressure Questions to isolate Side A's access solutions to wealthy nations, proving that without the pathway, global exclusion remains permanent.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO wins on weighing and pressure. CON's strongest points on venture financing and manufacturing complexity were blunted by clipped openings and by the concession in B4.1 that few low-income patients reach cures today. PRO consistently answered the scope-creep worry with medical triggers + failed voluntary negotiation + royalties + review, preserving a live path that certain present exclusion outweighs speculative pipeline loss, while CON's tiered-pricing/voluntary answer never solved the low-income gap targeted by A3.1.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Q1 on low-income reach plus A4's answers: A exposed that Medicaid/NHS paths don't travel, forcing B to concede low access and pivot to tiered pricing, while A4.1-4.2 defanged the "affordability bakes in the trigger" / "who decides close alternative" attacks by separating medical criteria from price and requiring independent challengeable review.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): A built a tighter, more exploitable case: the "proves too much" attack on the innovation-collapse claim (compulsory licensing already exists under TRIPS without emptying biotech pipelines) forced B to carve out a one-shot-vs-repeat-dosing distinction that was plausible but never fully substantiated with mechanism. A's pressure question on real-world voluntary access was decisive — B's answer conceded that no low-income patient can currently access these therapies through voluntary deals, undercutting B's own proposed alternative, and B's "nascent industry" reframe never actually neutralized that admission. A capitalized on this concession cleanly in Rebuttal 2 and Closing. B's know-how and capital-flight arguments were coherent and were the strongest parts of its case, but they remained largely assertion-based ("capital migrates," "developers withdraw") without engaging why royalty-preserving, judicially reviewable licensing (as opposed to outright seizure) would trigger full exit rather than negotiated transfer, a gap A pressed repeatedly. A's Rebuttal 1 being clipped is a real execution cost, but the load-bearing arguments had already landed before the cutoff, so it dents but does not erase A's advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question 1 ("name a single gene-editing therapy a low-income patient can actually access via voluntary licensing") and B's forced concession in A1 that none currently exist, later leveraged by A in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to argue B's alternative is currently producing zero access.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won by effectively distinguishing the unique economics and manufacturing realities of gene-editing therapies from traditional pharmaceuticals. By showing that gene therapies are one-shot interventions reliant on complex, untransferable physical know-how, A successfully neutralized B's reliance on historical TRIPS comparisons.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 and A4.2 effectively dismantled B's TRIPS argument by proving that standing seizure risk for single-shot platforms destroys the economic model in a way that multi-dose molecule compulsion does not.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): PRO kept a live route that CON never closed: voluntary mechanisms have produced zero low-income doses (conceded at B4.1), royalty-bearing compulsory licensing already exists under TRIPS with continued R&D growth, and compulsion creates incentive for tech transfer. CON's distinctive one-shot economics and know-how objection were plausible but remained speculative and was turned by PRO's bargaining logic that a veto-free negotiation improves rather than destroys cooperation. The moral urgency plus empirical precedent outweighed the predicted capital flight.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure on real voluntary access and CON's A1 admission at B4.1 that 'None fully deliver a real low-income dose yet' – leveraged decisively in A5.2 closing.
