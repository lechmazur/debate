### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO built a coherent structural case across all five rounds and never abandoned it. The core argument—market failure in hard-to-serve areas requires a durable legal obligation rather than discretionary programs—was established early and survived pressure. PRO's rural electrification parallel in A4.1 was the best single piece of substantive reasoning in the debate: historically grounded, directly responsive to the cost objection, and it explained the concrete mechanism (cross-subsidized funds + public investment) without inventing precision. PRO also landed the key pressure question that CON never fully answered: what concrete mechanism actually reaches the permanently excluded? CON's repeated responses—discretionary subsidies, community networks, municipal broadband—were the very tools PRO had already characterized as politically vulnerable and patchwork. CON never squared that circle. CON's best moment was the internal-contradiction catch in B4.1-B4.2: PRO had dismissed politically vulnerable discretionary programs, then proposed a universal service fund that itself requires legislative appropriations. This was genuinely damaging and PRO's answer that the obligation "can't be silently dropped" was only partly responsive. The obligation creates legal standing and political pressure, but CON was right that a fund requiring annual appropriations still carries vulnerability. This harm to PRO's durability argument is real but not fatal, because PRO's underlying claim—that a legal right changes the political and legal default even when imperfectly funded—is a plausible causal story that CON dismissed without fully disproving. CON's "a courtroom does not lay cable" framing is rhetorically sharp but it overstates: the claim is not that courts lay cable but that legal obligation forces states to fund the mechanisms that do. PRO's clipped closing is a real execution cost that left the final crystallization incomplete, but by that point the key arguments were already on the table. On the decisive clash—who has a claim when budgets tighten—PRO's case remains more live than CON's alternatives after the full exchange. Margin is clear but competitive because CON's internal-contradiction attack was the debate's sharpest blow and came close to neutralizing PRO's most important practical argument.

This was the sharpest targeted attack of the debate. PRO's A4.1 rural electrification parallel was the most effective substantive counter, providing a historically grounded mechanism, but it did not fully resolve how annual appropriations differ from the discretionary subsidies PRO had already dismissed, leaving a live wound in PRO's durability argument.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): CON successfully narrowed the debate to whether legal enforceability actually delivers connectivity or merely generates paper obligations, showing that PRO's concrete remedy—a universal service fund—is subject to the same political vulnerability PRO attributed to discretionary subsidies. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): CON built a marginally stronger path to victory through sharper rebuttal work and a genuinely decisive analytical distinction PRO never fully escaped. PRO's core claim—that legal universal service status is the enabling framework that converts discretionary funding into enforceable obligation—was coherent and historically grounded throughout, and the bundling argument (financing plus accountability as two halves of the same legal duty) survived the exchange. However, CON landed three compounding blows. First, CON separated the REA-style buildout mechanisms PRO kept citing from the individual cause of action the motion actually requires, exposing a genuine gap between PRO's evidence and the motion's language. Second, the trilemma in B3.2—what exactly does a court order when last-mile costs are prohibitive?—was never cleanly resolved; PRO's "universal service fund plus mandamus" answer is partially responsive but assumes adequate funding, and CON correctly noted in B4.6 that a cause of action does not compel a legislature to appropriate more. Third, and most decisively, CON's B4.4 distinction between suing over wrongful termination of an existing connection versus compelling entirely new infrastructure is analytically sharp and genuinely changes the force of the water-and-electricity analogy—it works for disconnection cases but collapses for buildout cases, which are the actual frontier of the access gap. PRO's closing does not address this distinction directly. PRO also suffered an execution cost from the clipped Rebuttal 1, which weakened the first rebuttal round. CON's pressure questions were sharper and generated pressure that persisted to the closing, while PRO's questions, though pointed, yielded cleaner responses from CON. The debate was close and competitive; PRO's framework argument has real force and CON's counter-proposal was thin, but CON's analytical edge on the core enforcement mechanism—particularly the disconnection/buildout distinction—tips the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.4 in Rebuttal 2: the distinction between a right to sue when existing service is wrongfully cut versus a right to compel entirely new infrastructure. CON argued the water-and-electricity analogy works only for the former, while the motion's access gap is overwhelmingly the latter. PRO's closing answer—"the court orders the agency with the backhoe to dig"—did not address the trilemma of what happens when the agency lacks funding to do so, leaving CON with the cleaner analytical position on the motion's hardest enforcement question.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A won because it successfully defended the utility-law model as a bundled framework where legal obligation converts discretionary funding into accountable mandates, and showed that treating connectivity as a market privilege preserves the underdelivery status quo. While B landed sharp analytical blows on individual litigation costs and shifting standards, A&#39;s historical grounding and the closing reframe that &quot;the law is the engine, not the decoration&quot; provided a cleaner path to affirm the motion. B&#39;s closing misattribution of the funding diagnosis to A, and its brittle distinction between historically tractable utility costs and internet buildout, weakened an otherwise competitive case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B&#39;s trilemma in Pressure Questions and Rebuttal 2 about what a court can actually order for remote buildout—open-ended subsidy, forced private loss, or lowered standard—which forced A to defend the feasibility of judicial enforcement in high-cost infrastructure gaps.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): CON wins by successfully fracturing PRO's core analogy and mechanism. CON sharply distinguished between existing regulatory funding tools and the individual cause of action the motion adds, demonstrating that private lawsuits cannot trench fiber, appropriate legislative funds, or overcome geographical cost barriers. CON's distinction between termination suits (standard for water/power) and buildout suits (the actual frontier of the internet gap) decisively undermined PRO's historical comparison. While PRO effectively argued that legal duties prevent regulatory slippage and activate existing funds, CON's fiscal trilemma and "courtroom vs. backhoe" frame exposed a fundamental mismatch between the proposed legal remedy and the material realities of infrastructure deployment. PRO's conflation of statutory funding hooks with individual litigation rights was repeatedly and cleanly exploited by CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 and 2 dismantled PRO's conflation of regulatory buildout funding with individual causes of action. The termination-vs-buildout distinction cleanly broke the water/electricity analogy, and the insistence that courts cannot solve CAPEX/terrain gaps forced PRO onto defensive ground about what a judge can actually order when a fund is under-resourced.
