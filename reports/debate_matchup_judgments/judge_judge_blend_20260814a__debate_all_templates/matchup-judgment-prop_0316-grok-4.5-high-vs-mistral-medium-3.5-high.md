### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): A won a clear but competitive debate by exposing a mismatch between PRO’s proposed disclosure and its claimed remedy. A bare notice that AI is used cannot tell a user whether a dry spell reflects bias, bad luck, or incompatibility; disclosing enough criteria to become actionable creates the gaming risk CON identified. PRO never resolved that dilemma and overclaimed in closing that transparency alone would distinguish systemic rejection from personal shortcomings. CON also offered the cleaner comparative weighing: uncertain psychological relief versus degraded matching quality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s decisive response was that the minimal AI label does not diagnose unfair suppression or restore self-worth, while broader disclosure can enable optimization and manufactured signals. PRO answered that robust algorithms should tolerate scrutiny, but did not explain how the proposed right actually delivers individual diagnosis without creating that tradeoff.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON wins because it successfully undercuts PRO's solvency: merely knowing that AI ranks profiles does not allow a user to distinguish their own bad luck or incompatibility from algorithmic suppression, so the proposed right fails to remedy the psychological harm PRO identifies. Meanwhile, CON's live gaming and product-degradation harms remain plausible and largely unanswered by PRO's "fragile algorithm" rejoinder. CON's framework—market discipline via churn, dating as voluntary private matching rather than a rights-bearing tribunal, and opacity as necessary product design—holds coherently across rounds, while PRO's closing overstates what transparency can fix.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal and closing argument demonstrating that PRO's transparency remedy cannot actually distinguish personal failure from algorithmic suppression for individual users, while simultaneously creating gaming incentives that flood the pool with optimized noise and degrade genuine matching.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by effectively dismantling Side A's core harm claim. Side A argued that transparency cures the psychological toll of rejection by letting users blame the algorithm rather than themselves. Side B decisively pointed out that merely knowing "AI is used" or its broad criteria does not explain individual rejections, leaving the psychological harm untouched. Furthermore, Side B's defense against the gaming objection—explaining that opacity is necessary to prevent proxy optimization and signal degradation—was much more grounded and practical than Side A's idealistic "just fix the algorithm" response. Side B maintained a stronger, more realistic grasp of platform dynamics and user behavior throughout the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to Side A's pressure question about gaming, explaining that opacity is itself the robust design against exploitation, and that revealing criteria simply invites strategic users to flood the pool with manufactured signals, effectively neutralizing Side A's "just build a better algorithm" claim.
