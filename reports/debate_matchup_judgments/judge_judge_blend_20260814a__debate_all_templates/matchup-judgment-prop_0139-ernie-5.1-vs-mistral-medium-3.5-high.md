### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A wins on a clear but competitive margin, primarily because it consistently framed the motion correctly—the proposition asks whether women-only cars should be *allowed*, not whether they are perfect or mandatory. This is a materially lower bar than CON ever fully addressed. CON built its case around showing why the policy is bad, but "bad policy" does not entail "should be prohibited," and that gap was never closed. Side A's partial-solutions analogy (airbags, speed bumps, crosswalk signals) held up well throughout the exchange: we routinely permit harm-reduction tools that are imperfect, and no principled distinction was offered beyond "women-only cars relocate rather than reduce harm." CON's sharpest single move—distinguishing speed bumps (which reduce aggregate harm) from women-only cars (which merely shuffle it)—was genuinely effective and the best rebuttal in the debate, but it rested on an asserted Tokyo audit finding ("overall incident rates stayed flat") that was presented with more certainty than a closed-book debate can support. Side B never provided the causal mechanism proving agencies actually cut other safety measures as a result of adding these cars, and after being pressed on this by Side A's Q2, CON explicitly conceded it could not produce such evidence while unconvincingly shifting the burden. Side A's answers to the pressure questions were direct and structurally sound; CON's closing largely repeated earlier claims rather than sharpening the decisive comparison. The "permission vs. mandate" framing advantage for Side A was the clearest uncontested route to victory remaining at the close of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's distinction in Rebuttal 2 between speed bumps (aggregate harm reduction) and women-only cars (harm relocation) was the most damaging single exchange—it directly punctured Side A's most-used analogy and introduced the memorable "harm shuffling" frame. However, it required accepting disputed displacement data at face value and still did not address the motion's "should be allowed" threshold, limiting its ultimate decisiveness.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins because its displacement and incentive-shift arguments were never neutralized; A squarely showed localized gains without net system reduction and exposed B’s lack of causal evidence for catalysis, while B’s partial-solution defense remained unweighted against zero-sum harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 directly answered both pressure questions with A1/A2 labels and reframed displacement as harm shuffling plus incentive evaporation, landing the decisive blow on B’s net-benefit claim.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side B wins by successfully dismantling Side A's core weighing mechanism. While A effectively argued for the moral urgency of immediate, localized protection and leaned heavily on analogies like airbags and speed bumps, B delivered a decisive comparative rebuttal by distinguishing genuine harm reduction from harm relocation. B's argument that women-only cars shuffle risk rather than eliminate it, combined with the structural point that such policies shift political incentives and stall universal safety upgrades, gave B a cleaner, more rigorous path to victory. A never fully answered why zero-sum displacement justifies public policy, leaving B's systemic framing dominant by the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's direct counter to Side A's speed bump/airbag analogy: "Speed bumps reduce accidents; women-only cars relocate harassment." This sharply exposed the logical flaw in treating harm displacement as acceptable partial protection and decisively shifted the weighing metric toward net systemic impact.
