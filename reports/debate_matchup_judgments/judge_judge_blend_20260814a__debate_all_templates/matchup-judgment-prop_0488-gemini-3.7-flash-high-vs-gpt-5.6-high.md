### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won on remedy fit. PRO diagnosed concentration risks well but never showed that breakup reliably creates field diversity rather than re-labeling inherited patents, germplasm, fixed costs, and high-volume incentives. CON’s design dilemma (vertical vs horizontal), genes-not-shareholders weighing, and comparison to direct breeding/supply measures stayed live and decided the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s vertical/horizontal design dilemma (A2.1–A2.2, pressed again in A4): severance need not create competing breeders, and splitting may leave packages or brittle contracts intact.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won the remedy-fit clash. PRO argued integrated seed-chemical bundles drive monoculture but never showed breakup creates new germplasm or competing breeders; successors inherit same patents, factories, and incentive for blockbusters. CON's vertical vs horizontal dilemma and "pathogen attacks genetics, not shareholders" went largely unanswered, and its regulatory-capture boomerang held: if agencies can't police bundling, they can't allocate patents/germplasm after a breakup. CON also answered pressure questions directly with a broader package (public breeding, seed banks, redundant production) that more directly maps to resilience.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's R1 design dilemma – vertical split leaves one dominant seed firm contracting with one chemical firm, horizontal split leaves integrated mini-cartels – plus R2 answer that scale allows trait licensing across diverse varieties and internal redundancy, while breakup replaces it with fragile contracts.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively severing the causal link Side A attempts to draw between corporate ownership structures and biological crop diversity. Side B's core refrain—that pathogens attack shared genes, not shared shareholders—memorably highlights the mechanism gap in Side A's case. While Side A argues that breaking up integrated monopolies will force independent breeders to compete on regional adaptability, Side B effectively counters that high fixed costs, patent inheritances, and processor demand will still push smaller successor firms toward standardized, high-volume varieties. Furthermore, Side B exposes a fatal contradiction in Side A's stance on government capacity: Side A dismisses targeted behavioral regulations as futile due to regulatory capture, yet assumes the same government can flawlessly execute and perpetually police the immensely complex asset allocation and anti-reconsolidation mandates required by a structural breakup. Side B's alternative package directly targets the physical and biological buffers of food resilience, making it the more logically sound and practically grounded path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's regulatory paradox in Rebuttal 2, pointing out that if a government is too captured to enforce anti-bundling rules, it is certainly too captured to allocate patents, germplasm, and factories during a massive corporate breakup and prevent reconsolidation.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO won because it kept the motion on systemic common-mode risk and made the cleaner comparative claim: independent fallback capacity matters more for food resilience than concentrated scale. CON’s capital-intensity and logistics points were real, but they repeatedly collapsed substantial successors into “underfunded regional fragments,” underweighted correlated failure, and answered Q2 thinly with gene banks and residual rivalry rather than a genuine independent supplier. PRO better resolved ownership versus productive capacity, treated public breeding as complementary not substitute, and crystallized transition costs versus permanent single-owner risk.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal defeating CON’s logistics/innovation scale case by separating internal conglomerate “redundancy” from genuine multi-firm independence ([B2.1]–[B2.4], [B2.7]).
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central comparison: capacity vs ownership. B showed breakups can create several still-global, viable firms preserving labs, germplasm and factories, so assets are reallocated not destroyed, while removing single balance-sheet, cyber-perimeter, and strategy failure. CON kept assuming successors become small, underfunded regional shops and never answered Q2 about shelved tech or correlated disruption, while its own A2 answer relied on existing rivals and gene banks that PRO had already shown are not planting-window suppliers. That made CON's scale/innovation story less resilient than PRO's insurance logic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3-B2.6 exposing that conglomerate internal redundancy is subject to one cost-cutting decision, versus genuine market redundancy from independent global successors, plus incentive to shelve competing trajectories.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by decisively dismantling Side B's core assumption that structural breakups inherently destroy productive capacity and R&D scale. By clearly distinguishing between ownership concentration and physical/scientific assets, Side A successfully argued that multiple substantial, global successor firms can maintain innovation while eliminating the correlated, systemic risks of a single point of failure. Side B's reliance on the "frictionless fantasy" and "underfunded fragments" strawman was effectively neutralized, leaving Side A with the cleaner comparative weighing between manageable transition costs and permanent common-mode failure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which sharply exposed Side B's central mistake of treating ownership concentration as synonymous with productive capacity, effectively neutralizing Side B's claims that breakups destroy laboratories and R&D pipelines.
