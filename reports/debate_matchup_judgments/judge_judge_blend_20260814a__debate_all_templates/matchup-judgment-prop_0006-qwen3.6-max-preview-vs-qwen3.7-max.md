### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): PRO built the stronger cumulative case. Its core legal anchor—that "engaged to wait" has always been compensable, and that algorithmic management is functionally employer control—was well-grounded in general labor-law principles and was never squarely refuted by CON. The forklift-operator analogy was more apt than CON's freelance-plumber counter, and PRO's pressure question on algorithmic deactivation effectively exposed the disanalogy CON relied on throughout. PRO's "per employer" answer to the multi-apping concern was legally coherent: wage liability attaches to each platform for the time it controls, which is the standard rule in concurrent employment and is consistent with how platform tracking actually works. CON hammered the multi-apping hazard in nearly every speech but never adequately dismantled the per-employer response, making the repetition feel like a stall rather than a genuine refutation. PRO's reframe—"flexibility without a wage floor is just unpaid availability disguised as freedom"—was the most memorable and accurate crystallization of the key stakes. CON's "zone full" scenario had real rhetorical force, but PRO answered it coherently by distinguishing real-time demand caps from advance-commitment shift scheduling, and by pointing out that artificial oversupply currently forces workers into longer unpaid waits than dynamic caps would. CON's closing largely recycled the multi-apping argument without sharpening the comparative case. PRO's closing was tighter and more decisive. The debate was competitive—CON's access-for-casual-workers concern was genuine—but PRO progressed more effectively across rounds and left more live, unanswered arguments standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 answer to the multi-apping concern [A2.2] was the pivotal exchange. By invoking standard per-employer wage liability and noting that platforms already track active time, PRO converted CON's best empirical objection into evidence that platforms currently exploit unpaid idle time as a free operational subsidy. CON never produced a compelling reason why the long-established per-employer principle should not apply here, making the repetition of the multi-apping argument throughout the debate weaker each time it appeared.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side B wins a clear but competitive debate. Side A built a strong fairness/control case around engaged-to-wait doctrine and platform oversupply, but Side B’s multi-apping and login-cap consequences became the decisive comparative issue. A repeatedly answered that wage duties are “per employer,” but that did not resolve B’s core point that simultaneous app use would create overlapping idle-time liability and push platforms toward exclusivity, caps, or shift-like gating. A’s dynamic throttling answer partly conceded B’s access concern, while B framed that concession effectively as the loss of on-demand flexibility.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s first rebuttal was most decisive: it turned A’s “per employer” answer into proof of the double-payment problem and tied A’s proposed login throttling to the very rigidity A claimed to avoid.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side A won by establishing that mandating paid idle time creates an unresolvable multi-apping double-dipping hazard and forces supply caps that destroy spontaneous access; Side B's answers on per-employer liability and dynamic throttling did not neutralize these mechanics or the resulting loss of flexibility.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 repeatedly pressed the overlapping-wage impossibility and quota consequences, forcing B to reframe without closing the gap.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Both sides were competitive throughout, but CON built a slightly cleaner and more consistently developed path to victory. PRO's principled standby-rules argument and economic risk-shifting claim were genuinely strong, and PRO's Q1 (if platforms can identify dead hours to avoid paying, why aren't they already logging workers off?) was sharp. But CON's answer—that logging workers off now would drain network liquidity and there is no payroll penalty, so the current equilibrium is rational, but attaching wages to idle time changes the math entirely—was substantively responsive and actually clarified rather than conceded the core economic logic. CON's decisive advantage was the consistently developed trade-off reframe: "paper wage floor that triggers real-world exclusion." CON turned PRO's own concessions against them throughout—when PRO said they "welcomed" forced log-offs as efficient management, CON correctly identified this as undermining PRO's pro-worker framing. The multi-apping implementation question (B3.1) also exposed a real compliance and surveillance problem that PRO's answer (platforms track GPS/app-state data) addressed only partially, leaving the surveillance concern unresolved. PRO's standby analogy and economic captivity argument remained live and were CON's weakest flank—CON never squarely refuted the traditional standby-pay principle in labor law. But on balance, CON's consequentialist argument was more concretely developed across rounds, and the trade-off narrative (tangible access vs. theoretical protection) was more persuasive to a third-party reader making the overall policy judgment the motion requires. The margin is narrow and reflects a genuinely competitive debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (B4) was the most decisive exchange. CON answered PRO's Q1 by explaining that platforms allow idle workers voluntarily because network liquidity has value but no payroll cost—and then immediately turned this into a forward-looking argument that the policy changes the incentive structure entirely, making aggressive access rationing predictable rather than speculative. CON also showed that PRO's multi-apping answer (platforms can just prompt workers to pause) would require banning concurrent logins, stripping workers of their primary earnings-diversification lever. These two moves together—neutralizing the sharpest pressure question while weaponizing PRO's own proposed solution—were the clearest demonstration of sustained argumentative pressure in the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side B won by making the strongest comparative clash about open access versus rationed employment. Side A gave a compelling exploitation story and framed unpaid waiting as a platform subsidy, but it never fully answered the practical cost of making every logged-in minute compensable: login caps, forced logoffs, acceptance thresholds, and loss of flexible access. Side A’s answer that platforms can simply manage or pause workers partly conceded Side B’s central harm. Side B also better exploited multi-apping, showing that overlapping wait-time liability either pays workers for availability they may be using elsewhere or requires intrusive policing and bans on concurrent use. Side A was rhetorically strong, but Side B gave the cleaner reason the proposition as written would harm the workers it aims to protect.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s multi-apping exchange was decisive: its pressure question forced Side A to say a platform may owe pay during overlapping availability, and Side B then used that to argue the policy would require surveillance, bans on concurrent logins, or access rationing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A won by showing that mandating pay for waiting time triggers real exclusion and loss of open access, with stronger direct answers to pressure questions on multi-apping and caregivers. Side B's case relies on an unproven assumption that platforms will simply schedule efficiently without rationing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answers pressure questions on multi-apping and exclusion, crystallizing the open-access tradeoff over B's responses.
