### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): Side A wins on the two most decisive clashes. First, the victim-centered data argument (911 calls, hospital trauma admissions, acoustic gunshot sensors) is the most important live claim in the debate. It directly severs the feedback loop by showing that violent crime generates independent evidence regardless of patrol presence. Side B's responses—escalation creating assault charges, displacement—were partially responsive but never cleanly neutralized the core mechanism: if violence displaces to Zone B, victim reports follow the blood there, not phantom arrests from Zone A. Second, Side A landed the sharpest dialectical turn of the debate: Side B used decades of discriminatory human enforcement as the source of corrupted input data, but then celebrated human accountability through litigation as the remedy. Side A correctly pointed out that those same decades of litigation failed to stop the discriminatory enforcement, making "we can sue officers" a weak restoration of equity. "We cannot subpoena the subconscious, but we can regulate algorithms" crystallized this effectively. Side B's strongest remaining argument—trade secrets making audit impossible—was addressed with a plausible contracting mechanism (mandatory source code disclosure to civilian auditors), which Side B challenged but never decisively rebutted. Side B's closing largely recycled earlier points rather than narrowing the residual clash. The comparison Side A established—prohibition trades a reformable tool for the unaccountable human discretion that generated the corrupted history in the first place—was the cleaner path to a verdict and was never overturned.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answer to Q2: using Side B's own evidence that decades of litigation failed to stop discriminatory enforcement to undercut the claim that human bias is "accountable." The phrase "We cannot subpoena the subconscious, but we can regulate algorithms" landed cleanly and Side B never recovered the accountability distinction after that exchange.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): B won by making the cleaner comparative case: prohibition returns cities to opaque human discretion, while regulated systems can be constrained, audited, and focused on victim-centered violent-crime data. A’s accountability claim also weakened because B persuasively argued that the same human discretion A wanted to restore produced the biased historical record in the first place. The debate stayed competitive because A had a coherent legitimacy and civil-liberties case, but B better resolved the central tradeoff.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was the repeated answer to the feedback-loop claim: violent victimization data follows “the blood, not the badge,” so patrol density may affect discretionary arrests but does not similarly manufacture homicides, shootings, trauma admissions, or emergency calls.

Furthermore, Side A brilliantly flips the 'black box' critique by arguing that human intuition is historically un-auditable, whereas algorithmic transparency can be mandated via city contracts.
