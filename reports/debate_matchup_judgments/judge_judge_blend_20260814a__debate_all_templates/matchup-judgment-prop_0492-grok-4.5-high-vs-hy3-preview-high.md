### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but PRO (B) built a more coherent, consistently reinforced case: the externality framing (private gains land immediately, social costs land later and are absorbed by communities that never chose the risk) was repeated and defended, and PRO used the carbon-tax/R&D-credit analogy to answer the causal-attribution objection with a plausible institutional precedent. CON (A) landed its strongest blow on measurability/causal attribution of robotics-driven layoffs, which PRO never fully resolved, but CON's "private loss, not externality" framing was weaker and PRO exploited that gap effectively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing. Both sides answered pressure questions with proper A1/A2 structure, but A's Rebuttal 2 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost that weakened CON's final substantive engagement and left its closing as largely a repeated assertion rather than fresh weighing. PRO's closing did the better job of crystallizing the decisive comparison ("progress without priced transition is temporary; priced transition makes the robots sustainable"), giving the debate a cleaner resolution in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to the causal-attribution pressure question (A2/B4.2), arguing firms already track robotics-linked headcount for investors and tax credit precedents show feasibility, directly neutralized CON's strongest and most repeated objection, even though it did not fully close the gap.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won a competitive debate by sustaining the clearer comparative case: automation creates transition costs extending beyond individual workers, and a modest levy can fund wage insurance, mobility aid, and employer-linked training. Side B exposed a serious attribution and enforcement problem, but overstated it as impossible and never convincingly answered the broader public spillovers or A's support package. B's clipped second rebuttal and unsupported claims that business formation is the only sustainable alternative also weakened its path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's distinction between aggregate workforce reporting and provable robotics-caused layoffs was the sharpest attack, materially weakening A's implementation story. A nevertheless limited the damage by proposing documented headcount or wage-bill triggers and showing that the policy's adjustment benefits extend beyond conventional retraining.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by sustaining a clearer live route to victory on the externality and transition-lag framing. While Side B generated real pressure on causal attribution, its private-loss re-characterization never fully negated public spillovers, and its business-formation alternative remained underdeveloped. Side A successfully neutralized capital-flight concerns and converted retraining objections into specific design features of an earmarked fund. Side B’s clipped Rebuttal 2 and repetitive fatal-flaw rhetoric added execution costs, whereas A’s closing crystallized the comparative stakes with disciplined focus.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal answers (A4.1–A4.2), which directly engaged Side B’s pressure questions by reframing capital mobility as manageable and converting retraining and geographic-mismatch objections into solvable design features of the earmarked revenue stream.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but Side A's line of attack on implementation—how regulators can objectively identify "direct replacement" versus ordinary process redesign, capacity expansion, or offshoring—was never fully neutralized. B offered a concrete mechanism (before/after headcount at a function) in the pressure-question answer, which was a reasonable partial defense, but A's Turn 8 follow-up (that processes continually redesign, freeze headcount, or relocate residual tasks) reopened the gaming problem and went unanswered in B's closing, which pivoted to emotive framing ("hope" vs "plan") rather than re-engaging the definitional attack. A also sustained a clear, if contestable, alternative funding story (general revenues scale with the growth automation creates) that B never fully dislodged beyond asserting political fragility. B's externality and "privatized gains/socialized losses" framing was compelling and consistently pressed, and its border-adjustment proposal showed originality, but that proposal was rebutted as converting domestic distortion into trade retaliation without a real counter. On balance, A's rebuttals did more direct damage to B's live mechanism than B's rebuttals did to A's growth-and-flexibility alternative, giving A a clear but not dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Turn 8 point that B's "150 workers to zero" baseline freezes a static snapshot while real automation involves continual redesign, task-shifting, and relocation—left unaddressed in B's closing—was the most decisive unrebutted blow in the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by combining two mutually reinforcing objections: identifying taxable replacement requires contestable counterfactuals, and taxing productivity risks reducing the growth and broad revenues available for support. Side A established a compelling fairness concern but answered administrability with an oversimplified headcount snapshot and answered economic harm largely by asserting smart calibration. Its border-adjustment patch also extended beyond the stated policy without resolving the same classification problem. B’s mistaken reference label was a minor execution cost, not enough to overturn its clearer comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s claim that 150 workers becoming zero makes replacement objectively factual was decisive: observed headcount change does not establish how much employment would have persisted absent redesign, expansion, outsourcing, or relocation. That directly weakened both the tax trigger and the reliability of its earmarked revenue.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing the tax is definitionally fraught—requiring discretionary counterfactuals about vanishing jobs—and self-defeating because it shrinks the productivity base needed to fund retraining. Side A’s externality frame and smart-calibration defense never overcame B’s historical pattern that automation broadly raises living standards, nor did A squarely answer B’s critique that an earmarked displacement tax creates a bureaucracy with perverse incentives to expand taxable categories. B’s rebuttals, especially the “vanishing snapshot” response, did more damage to A’s feasible route than A managed against B’s abundance case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Rebuttal 2, Side B answered Side A’s pressure question about factual measurability by arguing that even a headcount drop from 150 to 0 “freezes a vanishing snapshot,” ignoring process redesign, residual tasks, and capacity expansion—turning A’s strongest administrative answer into a deeper problem of permanent discretion and gaming.
