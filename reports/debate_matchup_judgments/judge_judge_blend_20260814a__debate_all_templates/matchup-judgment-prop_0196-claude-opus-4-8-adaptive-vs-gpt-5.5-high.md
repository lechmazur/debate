PRO’s tiered feasibility standard answered the heterogeneity worry, and CON’s clipped speeches left key burdens underdeveloped.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A established a dominant comparative framework around resource scarcity, successfully demonstrating that the marginal cost of mandating "readiness" on already-efficient new homes crowds out high-impact retrofits on dirtier existing homes. Side B suffered from severe cap management issues (clipping in 4 out of 5 speeches) and struggled to defend the practical impacts of pricing marginal buyers out of new homes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 response on affordability, noting that renaming a priced-out buyer as "a failure of housing finance" doesn't change the fact that they remain in a high-emitting 1960s flat.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that the mandate diverts scarce capital and skilled labor into gold-plating the lowest-emitting slice of the housing stock (new builds already meeting modern codes) for speculative "readiness" features—heat-pump circuits and solar-ready roofs—that may never be used. CON's "checkbox regulation" framing and concrete affordability scenario (marginal households priced out into drafty old flats) undermined PRO's lock-in narrative. PRO never substantively rebutted the opportunity-cost argument or showed that the marginal gain from net-zero-ready over standard new construction outweighs retrofitting existing high-emitters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON in Rebuttal 1 [A2.5] seized on PRO's admission that the motion only delivers "readiness," not actual net zero, validating CON's claim that the policy forces buyers to pay upfront for unused hardware—a costly regulatory checkbox rather than guaranteed abatement. This narrowed the clash to whether speculative mandates on the cleanest homes are worth their certain cost, a framing CON sustained through the close.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON wins by successfully narrowing the clash to the marginal abatement cost of "net-zero-ready" mandates versus the high-impact retrofitting of existing stock. While PRO effectively argued that construction-stage readiness prevents irreversible lock-in and stops the retrofit backlog from growing, CON's consistent pressure on opportunity cost, scarce capital/labor, and the reality that modern-code new homes are already relatively efficient created a stronger comparative frame. CON's closing crystallized this clash memorably ("They compare a brand-new home to itself..."), and PRO's dismissal of affordability concerns as a "failure of housing finance" left a key practical burden under-answered. Both sides were disciplined and followed format rules well, but CON's weighing of real-world tradeoffs and marginal impact gave it the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 and 2 effectively dismantled PRO's "tomorrow's mistakes" framing by establishing that modern building codes already ensure baseline efficiency, shifting the debate to the costly marginal jump to "readiness" and highlighting how that spend crowds out high-impact retrofits of older, dirtier stock. PRO's defense of readiness capturing irreversible design choices was strong, but CON's opportunity-cost weighing and sharp closing comparison ultimately proved more decisive.
