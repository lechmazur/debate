- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (PRO)): CON built the stronger technical case by focusing consistently on the specific language of the motion—a legal performance guarantee requiring near-parity in wait times and convenience—rather than a general accessibility principle. Three live advantages survived the full exchange. First, the definitional vagueness problem: CON's Q1 asked what "close to standard service" actually means at the enforcement level, and PRO's A1 response ("benchmark," "oversight bodies," "performance tracking") was circular and evasive, never specifying who decides compliance, at what granularity, or what triggers a violation. Second, the dispatch-vs.-design distinction: CON's sharp point that low-floor buses succeeded as a standardized mass-fleet design change whereas ride-hail parity is a live spatial matching problem was never adequately answered—PRO's "modernize dispatch" response restates a goal rather than solves the scarcity problem. Third, CON's Q2 forced PRO to choose among cutting coverage, raising prices, or degrading standard service in low-demand areas, and PRO responded with "false choice / unify the fleet," which begged the question. PRO's civil rights framing and segregation argument were compelling and gave it a genuine values advantage; the "permanent underclass of travelers" framing was effective. But PRO's case depended on treating the operational tradeoffs as illegitimate rather than answering them. CON's closing was more focused and directly resolved the live clash, while PRO's closing recycled the civil rights frame without engaging the definitional and operational pressures still on the table. The three clips on CON's speeches are a real execution cost that likely cost CON some argument density, but the core reasoning came through clearly in each round and was well-supported in the closing. PRO's evasion on the A2 answer in Rebuttal 2 and the recurring reliance on "innovate/modernize" as a magic answer were the decisive weaknesses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 turning the low-floor bus example against PRO was the most decisive exchange. PRO had offered it as proof that mandated integration drives innovation for everyone. CON responded that low-floor buses worked because a design change could be standardized across a mass fleet, whereas ride-hail accessibility is a live dispatch-matching problem—having sufficient accessible vehicles nearby at the right time, with trained drivers and loading time built in—that software can help allocate but cannot erase. This distinction undermined PRO's central innovation-forces-compliance argument and was never adequately answered in subsequent rounds.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built and sustained the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Three decisive advantages accumulated and were never squarely neutralized. First, PRO correctly identified that CON's feasibility/cost argument does not answer the underlying principle: it shows that equal access requires cross-subsidy, not that it is unwarranted. CON conceded at every turn that accessible service can exist—only that it costs more—which PRO correctly argued merely proves the network must spread that cost, exactly as transit networks always have. Second, PRO exposed CON's "targeted subsidies" alternative as providing no enforceable floor. CON never answered this: subsidies without a binding service standard yield paper access and real delay, the precise outcome the motion addresses. Third, PRO consistently and accurately noted that the motion says "close to" standard service, not instant perfect parity. CON's collapse scenario depended on rewriting the proposition into an impossible absolute, and PRO called this out in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, while CON never meaningfully defended the actual motion wording. CON had genuine strengths—the curb-cut category error in Rebuttal 1 was sharp, the airport cherry-picking point was fair, and the mechanism question about independent contractors was real pressure. But CON overreached by calling compliance "mathematically impossible," leaving itself open to PRO's flexible-floor answer, and then repeated "targeted subsidies" and "market exit" in the closing without engaging PRO's most important running arguments. PRO's moral framing ("managed exclusion," "everyone else can go now; you can go later") was rhetorically crisp and connected to live argumentative work. The best rebuttal exchange was PRO's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2: the contractor question was answered by pointing to existing platform-level tools (contracts, bonuses, leasing), and the sustainability question was answered by distinguishing the actual flexible standard from CON's caricature, leaving CON's two strongest lines materially weakened with no adequate response in A4 or the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 responses to CON's pressure questions: A1 dismantled the "can't force contractors" point by cataloguing the real supply-shaping tools platforms already use, and A2 turned CON's sustainability framing back around—the proposition covers only systems that choose to operate, making equal access a cost of entry rather than a mandate to operate everywhere regardless of viability. CON's closing never engaged either answer.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): Side A wins: established a clear equality-of-access obligation that CON never escaped; directly answered pressure questions on mechanisms and avoided CON's strawman of perfect parity; CON's collapse narrative and subsidy-only alternative remained unpersuasive after weighing. Clipped A turns are a cost but did not neutralize the decisive comparative frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A1/A2 answers to B's Pressure Questions, clarifying fleet-shaping tools and rejecting forced market exit.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): Side A won by controlling the framing of the motion and neutralizing Side B's operational objections. A established that "close to" standard service is a flexible floor, not rigid mathematical parity, and successfully shifted the debate to whether society should accept a permanently slower, less convenient tier of mobility for wheelchair users. B never defended that inequality in principle; instead, B attacked a caricature of perfect parity and repeatedly claimed market collapse without engaging A's concrete mechanisms for ride-hail supply shaping (contracts, leasing partnerships, incentives, and service-area obligations). A also exposed that B's alternative of voluntary subsidies lacked any enforceable performance standard, essentially conceding the "paper access, real delay" problem. While B raised genuine economic tensions about ride-hail structure, B overextended by treating the whole proposition as impossible and ignored A's clarification that operators choose to enter markets and must then internalize accessibility costs. A's pressure questions were sharper, A's answers were more direct, and A's closing crystallized the moral and policy stakes more cleanly.

This directly defanged B's core "impossible inventory" and "market exit" arguments.

CON's repeated warnings of total service collapse relied on a stricter parity standard than the motion requires, and their alternative of targeted subsidies failed to answer PRO's pressure on how it would prevent permanent second-tier wait times. PRO's rebuttals were sharper, directly answered pressure questions, and crystallized the ethical stakes more memorably.
