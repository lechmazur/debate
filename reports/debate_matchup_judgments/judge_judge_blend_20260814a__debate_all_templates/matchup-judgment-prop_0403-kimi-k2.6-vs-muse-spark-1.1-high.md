### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): CON built the more durable case by relentlessly pressing the mechanical gaps in PRO's proposal: who counts as "the community," what legal standard ever lets a team leave, and whether the rule applies retroactively. PRO's Pressure Questions answer that the exit standard is simply "whatever the enabling ordinance prescribes" conceded exactly the unlimited-discretion problem CON had been alleging, and CON's closing exploited that admission decisively, tying it back to the "functional ownership/servitude" framing from the opening. PRO's moral framing (subsidy as social contract, structured extraction, monopoly leverage) remained forceful throughout and was never fully rebutted on the fairness question, but PRO's practical remedy stayed vague under pressure, while CON's counter-proposal (repayment of unamortized debt plus better contract terms going forward) offered a cleaner, more resolvable alternative. CON's "zombie franchise" mechanism and the point that scared-off owners simply avoid public subsidies (leaving taxpayers with neither team nor repayment) were raised repeatedly and never squarely neutralized in PRO's closing, which mostly reasserted the moral case rather than repairing the mechanical objections. The debate progressed cleanly, with both sides honoring the Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 structure, but CON's rebuttals did more comparative damage to PRO's actual mechanism than PRO's rebuttals did to CON's counter-remedy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4) seizing on PRO's Pressure Questions answer that the exit standard is "whatever the ordinance prescribes" was the most decisive exchange: it converted PRO's attempted clarification into proof of the very unlimited discretion/servitude problem CON had been alleging since the opening, and PRO's closing never adequately repaired it.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won a competitive debate by establishing that subsidies create enduring public exposure beyond outstanding debt and that municipal competition prevents contract-by-contract bargaining from reliably protecting communities. B also turned A’s prediction that owners would reject subsidies into a public benefit. A exposed genuine problems involving retroactivity, voter discretion, and hostile “zombie” franchises, but overstated repayment as making communities whole and repeatedly treated the absence of subsidies as a taxpayer loss without explaining why.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was its answer to A’s contract-and-repayment alternative: repayment cannot restore land use, infrastructure, or neighborhood opportunity costs, while a uniform approval rule addresses the intercity race to the bottom. A’s sharpest response—that B supplied no limiting standard for withholding approval—narrowed but did not defeat that case.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by framing the proposition as enforcing the missing half of a subsidized bargain rather than ownership or imprisonment, neutralizing CON’s zombie and perpetual-trap fears while showing repayment-plus-better-contracts leaves the race-to-the-bottom extraction intact. CON’s property and incentive critiques were sharp and consistent but never displaced A’s live comparative claim that only a uniform binding checkpoint cures the monopoly asymmetry that individual city contracts repeatedly fail to solve.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A1/A2 answers plus Rebuttal 2 blunting B’s servitude charge: approval is a checkpoint voters will grant for genuine distress, not eternal captivity.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases with real clash, but PRO (B) ultimately had the more decisive path. B's "taxpayers bought the house" frame was reinforced by concrete, unrefuted historical evidence (St. Louis's dome debt, Oakland's three lost teams), which did double duty: it grounded the harm B identified and simultaneously undercut CON's repeated fallback proposal ("just enforce clawbacks") by showing that existing contractual protections already failed in practice. CON's strongest weapon — the "roaming veto vs. fixed contract" distinction and the sharp line "you'd need an accountant, not a referendum" — genuinely exposed a coherence gap in PRO's redefined mechanism, and PRO's closing patch ("an accountant with a ballot") was underdeveloped. But CON's own core harms (zombie assets, city-side hostage-taking, spatial sorting) remained speculative and were explicitly conceded as unenacted/hypothetical, while CON's closing never answered PRO's specific claim that private contract enforcement fails against a cartel that "can out-lawyer any clause" — leaving CON's proposed alternative remedy undefended against PRO's best empirical counterexample. Both sides handled pressure questions with proper A1/A2 format and honesty, but PRO's answers left it with more grounded, evidence-backed momentum through the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Pressure Questions exchange on whether "community approval" is just a disguised fixed repayment penalty: A's follow-up ("if repayment alone settles it, you need a calculator, not a referendum") was the sharpest single hit of the debate, but B's underlying evidence (St. Louis/Oakland showing existing legal protections already failed) meant CON's proposed alternative (enforce clawbacks) never recovered persuasive force after that exchange.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing a central dilemma in Side B's model. If full repayment automatically permits relocation, the approval process merely duplicates contractual clawbacks; if the community may still refuse, it creates the open-ended political veto and investment risks Side A identified. Side B offered stronger concrete examples and memorable rhetoric, but never established why community approval was superior to fixed, enforceable contractual remedies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.1-A4.2 directly answered Side B's repayment model: an automatic financial condition needs accounting and enforcement, not a binding vote, while a meaningful vote can move the goalposts. Side B's claim that community sign-off was uniquely necessary against a cartel remained asserted rather than demonstrated.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): CON more cleanly won the decisive clash over fixed contract penalties versus a political approval process. PRO's strongest principle and real relocation harms (St. Louis/Oakland) were blunted when CON forced the distinction that a binding community vote is inherently roaming and goalpost-shifting, not mere repayment certification; PRO's narrowing of the motion to "accountant with a ballot" conceded the superiority of CON's preferred clawbacks while still carrying the downsides of political control and spatial sorting for mid-sized markets. PRO answered pressure directly but could not neutralize this structural mismatch.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1/B5.1: if full repayment settles it, an accountant/calculator suffices and a ballot is unnecessary, exposing the motion as open-ended political leverage rather than a lease term.
