### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by establishing that high rates reflect imperfect group-level risk prediction, so many high-risk borrowers can still use expensive credit successfully as short-term liquidity. Side A repeatedly treated spiraling debt as mathematically inevitable and successful repayment as a small minority without grounding either claim. Its proposed substitutes were uncertain and did not answer B’s immediate-emergency comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s A1 response was decisive: portfolio risk explains why borrowers who repay quickly may still receive high rates. This directly defeated Side A’s inference that a high rate proves the loan is individually unaffordable, while exposing the cap’s inability to distinguish successful short-term users from chronic revolvers.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) narrowly won by showing that a rate cap is a blunt instrument that cannot sort predatory traps from legitimate short-term liquidity, and that the resulting exclusion pushes the most vulnerable toward worse, less regulated substitutes. Side B (PRO) powerfully framed high interest as systemic extraction, but never fully resolved the tension between accepting binding access cuts and claiming that sympathetic short-term borrowers would still qualify. CON’s portfolio-risk explanation and “usable formal flexibility versus guaranteed absence” frame provided the cleaner comparative weighing after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over portfolio risk and short-term use, where CON explained that group pricing necessarily places viable short-term borrowers in the same high-risk pool, directly undermining PRO’s claim that a cap would spare the responsible while only cutting off the doomed.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won the central trade-off. PRO framed cutting access as rescue from a toxic product, but never solved the $600-tomorrow problem — offering payment plans and community aid that CON showed already exist and leave gaps. CON's portfolio-risk answer to Q1 explained why short-term successful repayers still get priced high, and its math correction to Q2 — one month of 35% on a bridge loan is ~$17, not guaranteed ruin — survived. That turned the resolution's conceded cost into a net injury rather than harm reduction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 vs B3 on portfolio pricing and the $600 emergency: CON showed group-risk pricing does not equal individual doom and that short-term users gain liquidity without guaranteed extraction, while PRO's reply that reliable repayers would still qualify under a cap and could use forbearance failed to provide a regulated credit substitute.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B won by showing that cutting regulated credit neither removes emergencies nor reliably ends extraction; it can instead cause immediate job loss or eviction and push borrowers toward much costlier alternatives. Side A never adequately proved that long-term card interest is generally worse than payday rollovers or absolute illiquidity. Its reliance on family loans, charities, temporary hustles, and supposedly beneficial payday “hard stops” made its answer to the central access tradeoff especially weak.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B decisively turned Side A’s claim that payday loans terminate sooner: those “hard stops” may mean drained accounts, repossession, or repeated rollovers, while their monthly cost can equal a year of card interest. This directly undermined Side A’s core comparison and exposed the inadequacy of its proposed alternatives.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing PRO's inability to explain how borrowers survive emergencies without credit; PRO's reliance on family loans, charities, and temporary hustles proved empirically hollow against CON's concrete math showing 30 percent APR with principal paydown is vastly less extractive than 300 percent payday rollovers. CON successfully turned the accepted harm of credit rationing into the decisive impact by proving it forces families into immediate catastrophe rather than saving them from debt traps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal dismantling PRO's claim that payday loans offer preferable hard stops by reframing them as catastrophic seizures and repossessions, while exposing PRO's alternatives of family loans and employer advances as detached from the reality of subprime borrowers who need credit in the first place.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won the central trade-off. PRO argued cutting access ends a wealth siphon, but never solved migration to worse traps. CON showed with concrete math that 30% revolving with principal paydown costs ~$150/year on $500 versus $150/month in payday fees, that payday volume is also rollover-driven, and that denied credit means eviction and job loss, not a temporary sting. PRO's A1 answer relying on hustles, family loans, and charities and its defense of payday hard stops collapsed under that pressure, leaving CON's expensive-safety-net-is-better-than-no-net weighing intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.5 - turned B4.1/B4.2 into the decisive moment: exposed the detachment of hustles/charity/employer advances, flipped the hard-stop claim into seized accounts/repossessed cars, and used principal-paydown math ($500 at 30% = $150/year vs $150/month payday) to prove revolving is less extractive than the alternatives PRO would force borrowers into.
