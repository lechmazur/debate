### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): PRO built a vivid, physically grounded case for cascade risk and verifiability, but never closed two structural holes CON kept hammering: (1) the holdout problem — visibility of a test does not compel a non-signatory to join or comply, so a "verifiable" ban may just disarm the compliant while leaving major testers untouched; and (2) the collapse of PRO's "no clean test" claim once CON reframed the metric from zero-contamination to persistence, correctly noting that low-altitude intercepts decay far faster due to atmospheric drag than high-altitude ones. PRO's answers (A4.1, A4.2) restated its priors ("tracking makes cheating impossible," "altitude is a loophole") without directly refuting why detection compels non-signatory compliance or why persistence isn't the operative cascade variable. CON's Pressure Questions were sharper and exposed a live, unresolved tension in PRO's case, and CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing kept pressing that exact gap rather than repeating talking points. CON loses some credit for having its Rebuttal 2 clipped for length, an execution cost, but the visible portion still delivered the decisive A1/A2 answers and the core comparative argument. Overall CON provided the clearer, more adaptive path to victory by directly undermining PRO's two load-bearing claims (verifiability-as-compliance and altitude-agnostic catastrophe), while PRO's case, though rhetorically strong, became repetitive without fully neutralizing those attacks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's shift in Rebuttal 2/Closing from "zero contamination" to "persistence" as the correct metric, paired with the unanswered holdout question from Pressure Questions (B3.1), most damaged PRO's core claim that a verifiable, universal ban would reliably prevent cascades.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): PRO successfully established that kinetic ASAT tests uniquely and irreversibly poison the orbital commons, while CON's attempts to distinguish "clean" low-altitude tests were undermined by PRO's physics-based argument about omnidirectional fragmentation and by CON's own admission that no altitude guarantees zero contamination. CON's deterrence and holdout arguments had force but were not developed enough to overcome PRO's compelling commons framing, and CON's second rebuttal suffered from a word-limit clip that left key execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's dismantling of the "clean low-altitude test" distinction by demonstrating that hypervelocity collisions inevitably eject fragments into higher, persistent orbits, which CON never squarely refuted and ultimately conceded was not guaranteed to be zero-risk.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the core physics and verifiability path alive: kinetic tests are the only deliberate act that instantly shatters tons into thousands of crossing, untrackable fragments, and they cannot be hidden, unlike jamming/cyber. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built a cleaner, more resilient causal case: kinetic tests create observable, irreversible orbital debris, and the treaty targets only that specific self-defeating act, leaving other counterspace tools and readiness options intact. CON's strongest weapon was the dual-use missile-defense dilemma, but PRO's pressure question forced a dichotomy CON never escaped: either a "loophole" lofted intercept seeds lasting debris (and is thus itself the cascade the ban rightly covers) or it decays quickly (and there is no loophole to worry about). CON's attempt to answer this in Rebuttal 2 — noting fragments "decay rapidly" while algorithms are still validated — actually concedes that safe, low-perigee testing can continue, undermining its own claim that the treaty cripples missile defense. CON's remaining pillar, that unused wartime arsenals guarantee a future cascade regardless, was never turned into a reason to prefer additional peacetime pollution now; PRO repeatedly noted this deterrence benefit was asserted but never quantified, and CON's closing still did not quantify it. CON's "moving goalposts" charge against PRO's definition had some bite but wasn't decisive since PRO's debris-longevity framing was present from the opening. Overall PRO answered the central pressure more convincingly and gave the cleaner comparative reason to vote for the ban.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Questions Q1 (A3.1), forcing CON's "loophole" intercept into a dichotomy — either it creates lasting debris (making it itself a cascade covered by the ban) or it decays and poses no loophole — was the most decisive exchange; CON's Rebuttal 2 answer partially conceded the safe/decaying option exists, which undercut its own claim that the ban forces a choice between toothlessness and crippling missile defense.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins because it dismantled CON's central dilemma and upheld a cleaner comparative frame. CON argued the treaty must either be toothless (allowing missile-defense loopholes) or gut legitimate ballistic-missile defense. PRO showed the ban targets the observable effect—long-lived orbital debris—regardless of target labels. If lofted suborbital intercepts seed lasting debris, they are the cascade and are covered; if fragments fall, there is no loophole. CON never escaped this contradiction, instead asserting an unquantified deterrence benefit while conceding that wartime arsenals remain intact. PRO successfully framed the choice as certain, irreversible peacetime pollution versus a speculative deterrence story, making the treaty the obviously better bargain. B's strategic concerns were coherent in the opening but eroded under direct pressure, and its closing merely repeated unresolved claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.5), which exposed that CON's purported missile-defense loophole either produces lasting orbital debris (and is therefore prohibited) or does not (and therefore poses no cascade threat), nullifying the alleged dilemma and proving the ban is both verifiable and non-disarming.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the dual-use clash. By shifting from target-status to observable long-lived clouds, B defused CON's dilemma: if a lofted intercept leaves lasting debris it is the cascade PRO bans, if fragments fall it is legitimate MD and no loophole. CON conceded simulation/fly-bys can refine weapons [A1.4], undermining its own deterrence-loss claim and leaving only certain peacetime debris versus an unquantified readiness gain. Wartime arsenals remaining does not make preventing certain, irreversible peacetime pollution worthless.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 in Pressure plus A1 in Rebuttal 2: exposing that CON's lofted test cannot both create operational orbital debris and be defended as clean missile defense; if it lingers it's the cascade, if it falls there is no loophole.
