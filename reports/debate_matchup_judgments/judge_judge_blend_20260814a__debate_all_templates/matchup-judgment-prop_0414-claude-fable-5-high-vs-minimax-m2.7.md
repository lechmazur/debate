### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's defenses while tracking dropped arguments perfectly. A's distinction between gas taxes and tourist taxes in Rebuttal 2 neutralized B's main historical analogy, and A capitalized on B's concessions regarding baseline adjustments and temporary funding reductions. B also entirely ignored A's point that the proposed earmarks exclude direct tourist harms like policing and heritage repair.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 response distinguishing gas taxes (where revenue and need rise together) from tourist taxes (where the policy goal is to shrink the revenue source) was a devastating and decisive counter.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side A wins because it exposed PRO's dilution of "primarily" into a supplemental stream, forced explicit concessions on uncertain net gains and volatility costs, and kept the motion's exact wording central while unanswered harm-follows points lingered. Side B's clipped turns and pivot away from the proposition weakened its path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4) directly answers B's pressure Qs with labeled A1/A2, then weighs conceded volatility and backfill to crystallize decisive tradeoffs.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): CON won by exposing three decisive flaws in PRO's case that survived the entire exchange unrepaired. First, CON's fungibility argument showed that earmarked tourist taxes for housing can be neutralized by quiet baseline adjustments in general funds, rendering the "lockbox" symbolic; PRO never solved this invisible-backfill problem, instead retreating to a "supplemental stream" that contradicted the motion's demand for "primary" earmarking. Second, CON extracted a damaging concession under pressure: when Venice caps visitors, earmarked housing revenue falls, proving that PRO's model structurally penalizes the exact crowd-reduction policies residents want. Third, CON's argument that tourist harm falls on policing, heritage, emergency services, and enforcement—none covered by the motion—was raised in the opening and never answered by PRO across four speeches. CON's crystal-clear comparative weighing (certain costs versus uncertain benefits) and memorable framing ("a wall guarding a door nobody needs to walk through," "the problem wearing a ribbon") made the decision easier, while PRO's clipped opening and closing weakened their ability to close the gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's fungibility rebuttal and the scope-gap extension ([A1.6]). PRO argued earmarks protect resident housing, transit, and sanitation; CON showed that because money is fungible, councils can leave the labeled fund untouched while reducing baseline general funding—making the wall decorative. CON then layered the unrebutted point that the motion excludes the very services most damaged by tourism (policing, heritage, crowd control), so PRO's "revenue follows harm" principle actually indicts the motion. This combination trapped PRO between a mechanism that doesn't work and a scope that doesn't fit.
