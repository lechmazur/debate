### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built and sustained a stronger path to victory across the full exchange. The decisive clash was whether transparency and a cap trade a real foreign threat for a worse domestic one. B consistently answered this by turning A's own logic: the status quo without disclosure already hands foreign governments silent influence, so transparency removes an existing tool rather than creating a new one. B's answers to the pressure questions were substantively adequate—A1 correctly identified that domestic government funding already runs through public accountability mechanisms (budget hearings, oversight), meaningfully distinguishing it from opaque foreign-state channels; A2, while imperfect, explained that the cap enables portfolio management rather than a hard freeze. A's strongest live argument—that domestic corporate and state funding causes more bias and the rule ignores it—never quite landed as a decisive defeat of the proposition, because even a partial fix for a real gap can satisfy the motion as written. A's weaponization argument was plausible but speculative, and B effectively reframed it: the harassment A fears from disclosure labels is less dangerous than the invisible foreign control that exists right now. A also incurred a real execution cost from the clipped Opening, leaving its core case slightly underdeveloped at the most important stage. B's closing crystallized the key comparative—"secret, uncheckable censorship" versus "visible and contestable influence"—more memorably than A's closing, which overstated that B "never denied" the weaponization claim when B had in fact addressed it in Rebuttal 2.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answering A's Q2 on the cap math: rather than conceding the rigidity problem, B reframed the 5% limit as a portfolio ceiling that departments can stay under by reallocating other lines, directly neutralizing A's arithmetic scenario and reinforcing that the cap prevents dominance without banning collaboration. Paired with B's A1 response distinguishing domestic accountability mechanisms from opaque foreign channels, these answers collectively dismantled A's two most concrete objections and preserved B's live route to victory through the rest of the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A demonstrated that the mandate is both underinclusive—leaving dominant domestic funding biases untouched—and actively harmful, creating a domestic tool to smear and censor legitimate scholars under the guise of transparency. Side B never adequately answered why the 5% cap would not block urgent cross-border projects at the margin, nor why domestic state funding should be exempt from the same logic. A’s concrete chilling-effect scenarios and sustained comparative weighing proved the more live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s pressure question on the 4.9% + 0.3% cap scenario and the subsequent rebuttal reframing PRO’s “focused fix” as an active distraction from larger domestic biases, exposing that the rule would block legitimate research while ignoring the main threats to academic independence.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by more effectively crystallizing the oversight gap and turning the weaponization argument. While Side B raised strong concerns about domestic political harassment and the arbitrariness of targeting foreign funds, Side A successfully clarified that domestic state funding already operates under legislative and budgetary transparency that covert foreign channels lack. Side A's closing analogy—framing the status quo as a wide-open back door for secret authoritarian influence—cleanly outweighed Side B's domestic censorship fears. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built the stronger cumulative path. The most decisive moment was B's rebuttal to CON's claim that audits show foreign state funding is "marginal": B correctly identified that voluntary audit figures can only measure what is already disclosed, making them useless for sizing hidden funding streams—and CON never cleanly recovered from this logical trap, which ran directly through CON's core proportionality argument. B also maintained a consistently coherent policy scope (state-linked funding only, not all foreign engagement), which blunted CON's chilling-effect and definition concerns. CON had genuinely sharp moments: the internal contradiction attack in the closing (B admits disclosure gets weaponized, yet mandates disclosure) was the strongest single turn for CON, and the "peer review takes years" counter to B's proposed chilling-effect safeguard was well-aimed. But CON never offered a convincing affirmative mechanism for addressing the documented failure of voluntary transparency, and B's answer that universities already collect the relevant compliance data narrowed the administrative-burden objection adequately. The "existing export controls and IRBs already handle this" line from CON was raised but underdeveloped, leaving B's core documented-harm argument largely standing. On balance, B answered more of the live burdens across the exchange and kept the central logical problem in CON's case visible throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal [B2.1] to CON's reliance on audit figures: CON used voluntary audit data to claim the threat is marginal, but B pointed out those figures by definition only capture voluntarily disclosed funding, making them structurally blind to the very concealment under dispute. This was the sharpest single rebuttal in the debate and directly undermined CON's proportionality case, which CON never fully repaired.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a central tension in PRO's case: PRO argued that covert foreign funding is widespread and deliberately hidden, yet simultaneously claimed universities already track these streams under existing federal compliance rules, making mandatory disclosure a negligible burden. CON showed that if the funding is truly covert, existing frameworks clearly fail to capture it, so expanding those frameworks into a mandatory regime will not expose the hidden flows and will only manufacture politicization, chilling effects, and bureaucratic drag. CON also decisively rebutted PRO's peer-review safeguard by demonstrating that researchers self-censor during the multi-year research process long before publication, keeping the chilling effect argument live. While PRO effectively turned CON's audit statistics and established a concrete harm from undisclosed state influence, PRO never adequately resolved how disclosure plus vague "limits" would actually stop foreign state control rather than merely make it visible, and never recovered from the anticipatory censorship problem. The burden of proof rests on the proposition, and CON demonstrated that the proposed cure is more certain and widespread than the disease.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response in Rebuttal 2 that PRO's peer-review safeguard fails because researchers will self-censor during the years-long research window before publication, neutralizing PRO's primary defense against the chilling effect and weaponization risks.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): CON wins by successfully dismantling PRO's core safeguard and turning PRO's compliance argument. When PRO introduced a late safeguard limiting disclosure to peer-reviewed outputs to avoid chilling effects, CON sharply countered that self-censorship occurs during the years-long review window, neutralizing the fix. CON also effectively argued that if existing compliance frameworks already track this data, their failure proves mandatory disclosure won't solve the covert threat, while existing export controls offer a more targeted alternative. PRO's case remained strong on framing but couldn't overcome CON's tighter causal analysis and comparative weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's peer-review safeguard, pointing out that the chilling effect operates during the multi-year review window rather than after publication, which cleanly defused PRO's main answer to the politicization charge.
