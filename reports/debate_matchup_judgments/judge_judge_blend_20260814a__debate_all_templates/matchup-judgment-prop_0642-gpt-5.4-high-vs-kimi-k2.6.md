### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A (PRO) built a more durable path to victory across the full exchange. The core asymmetry argument—minimum errors are universal and locked-in at every project approval, maximum errors are localized and revisable—was more persuasive than CON's counter-claim of "option value" in excess parking. CON's reversion argument (excess can be repurposed; shortages cannot be retrofitted) is clever but weaker in practice: repurposing parking lots requires major future capital, while adjusting a cap or managing curb on the few affected blocks costs comparatively little. PRO's answer to CON's B3.Q1 dilemma ("if generous, what does it constrain?") was genuinely adequate—lender habits, old formulas, and competition for peak demand are real reasons private actors overbuild beyond tenant need even in car-dependent markets. Most decisively, PRO's A4.5 contradiction catch—CON simultaneously argues developers need freedom above any cap (market knows best) and government floors because the market cannot be trusted to meet demand—was never answered in CON's B4 or closing. CON's strongest live pressure was the political-will failure problem: if permits and meters never materialize, neighbors absorb systemic spillover rather than a localized glitch. PRO conceded this partially but correctly characterized it as a reason to set a higher local maximum rather than impose minimums everywhere. Side B's clipped Rebuttal 2 is a modest additional execution cost, leaving A4.5 explicitly unaddressed. CON's option-value and neighbor-harm arguments gave it a genuinely competitive case, keeping this a real contest rather than a blowout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.5 contradiction catch: CON simultaneously claims developers need full freedom above any cap because the market knows best, and yet need government-imposed floors because the market cannot be trusted to meet parking demand. The two positions cannot both be true, and CON never resolved the tension in B4 or the closing. This cut to the structural inconsistency at the heart of CON's case and went unanswered through the rest of the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side A built a cleaner, more progressive case across the full debate. The core structural argument—that regulatory floors have no inherent brake and ratchet toward oversupply, while ceilings allow developers to match actual demand—was introduced clearly, survived pressure, and was crystallized effectively in closing. The causation reversal (minimums created car dependency, not the reverse) gave PRO a principled reason to change the regulatory direction rather than just calibrate the existing tool. Most decisively, PRO exploited Side B's own concession [B2.7] that minimums are "often too high" with a sharp pressure question and strong answer: if cities can calibrate data to set a smarter minimum, they can use the same data to set a meaningful maximum, so the "smarter minimum" rebuttal largely collapses into PRO's framework. PRO's asymmetry framing—visible, fixable curb problem versus invisible, permanent housing cost—was memorable and never clearly overcome. Side B's responses were competent: the spillover externality argument is real, the "smarter minimum" answer is logical, and the mainstream two-car household point is relevant to the motion's specific context. But the smarter-minimum response concedes PRO's critique of current floors without explaining why a floor is structurally superior to a ceiling as a regulatory mechanism, and the spillover argument was partially neutralized by PRO's point that free curb space, not project-level caps, drives the spillover problem. Side B's opening was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost. Side A answered the pressure questions directly with A1/A2 labels, handled the hardest challenge (interim spillover) honestly by acknowledging it and arguing the trade-off favors housing, and maintained consistent framing throughout. The debate was close and both sides engaged seriously, but Side A made the decision easier to reach.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's exploitation of CON's admission [B2.7] that minimums are "often too high and should be reformed" was the most decisive exchange. PRO pressed this with a sharp pressure question (Q1 in A3), and CON's answer—use smarter minimums instead—inadvertently accepted PRO's causal critique while offering no structural reason why calibrated floors are inherently more reliable than calibrated ceilings. PRO then used A4.4 to crystallize: a floor with no ceiling has no brake, and the history of minimums shows exactly that ratchet in practice. CON never fully recovered from this asymmetry.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully weaponizing the 'car-dependent' clause of the prompt. B demonstrated that Side A's cap relies on a 'rescue plan' of secondary policies (curb pricing, future transit) to handle the immediate externalities of underparking. Side B also provided a decisive economic explanation for why regulatory floors are needed even when demand exists: developers will otherwise externalize parking shortages onto public streets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's argument that A's reliance on curb pricing and permits proves maximums relocate demand rather than solve it, functioning as a 'rescue plan' that makes caps a dangerous default.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B wins by establishing a clearer long-term asymmetry between minimums' invisible permanent housing tax and maximums' manageable visible spillover, directly answering pressure questions in Rebuttal 2 with the required A1/A2 format while showing caps use real demand data. CON concedes minimums often need reform but offers no limiting principle or proof that flexible floors outperform hard caps in car-dependent areas without relying on unproven external rescues.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 pair that ties the cap to observed ownership data, contrasts on-site cost permanence with fixable curb issues, and forces CON to defend open-ended floors despite their own concession on excess minimums.
