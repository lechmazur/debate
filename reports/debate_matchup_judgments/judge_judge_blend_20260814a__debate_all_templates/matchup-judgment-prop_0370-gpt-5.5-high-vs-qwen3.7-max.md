### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A wins on the decisive clashes. Its core move—that the default matters because workers cannot meaningfully consent when refusal risks employment—was never adequately answered by B. B's best argument, that containerized apps limit employer access to work data only, is real but was effectively undercut by A's point that B was always describing the ideal implementation rather than the routine reality, and that even a perfect container still imposes compliance requirements, notification pressure, battery and storage costs, and trust in a vendor on a device the worker did not choose for work. A's burden-reversal framing ("CON places the burden on employees first; PRO places it on large employers first") is the clearest comparative weighing in the debate and B never answered it directly. The line "A uniform does not ask for location permissions; steel-toed boots do not create after-hours pings; a wrench does not carry your children's messages and two-factor authentication" is the most substantively damaging single passage—it dismantles B's equipment analogy in a memorable, factually grounded way and B's response in Rebuttal 2 never engages with the intimacy distinction, only reiterating that containerization makes the phone "functionally identical to a company-issued scanner," which is precisely what A had already refuted. B's logistical-friction and electronic-waste arguments have real weight and A's answer—shared devices, terminals, opt-in BYOD—is adequate but not fully developed. Still, B's persistent failure to address the power-dynamic problem (that "you can ask for a company phone" is not a real opt-out when the baseline expectation is already set) leaves B's live route to victory dependent on assuming the very benign employer behavior that the proposition is designed to guard against. A's closing crystallizes the externalization point cleanly; B's closing is more repetitive. Both sides followed format correctly after accounting for A's clipped speeches, which cost A some execution but not enough to change the outcome given what was visible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answer to B's "personal vehicle/personal tools" pressure question: the phone is not a purpose-specific, outward-facing tool but a communications hub, location record, password gateway, and family archive—compounded by the line "A uniform does not ask for location permissions; steel-toed boots do not create after-hours pings; a wrench does not carry your children's messages and two-factor authentication." This directly dismantled B's running equipment analogy, and B never recovered a substantive answer to it.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by establishing a clearer route to victory around coercive defaults, employer responsibility for infrastructure, and the categorical distinction of personal phones; their pressure-question answers and weighing on power imbalances remained live and unneutralized while CON's analogies and efficiency claims were effectively challenged without new evidence dumps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 directly resolved pressure on defaults and phone uniqueness, sharpening the decisive comparison on burden allocation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by establishing that the decisive issue is who controls the default when work requirements reach into uniquely personal infrastructure. CON’s defense relied heavily on idealized containerization and logistical necessity, but PRO consistently showed that even containerized apps require permissions, notifications, and metadata access, while the power imbalance makes "you can object" an illusory safeguard. PRO also effectively turned CON’s analogies—pointing out that unlike steel-toed boots or uniforms, phones are general-purpose hubs for banking, health, family life, and authentication, and that work apps create after-hours reach that physical tools do not. CON overcharacterized the proposition as a rigid hardware ban, allowing PRO to clarify that voluntary BYOD remains possible; what the proposition rejects is making personal-phone installation the routine condition of employment. PRO’s closing crystallized the clash cleanly around externalization of infrastructure costs and default-setting in unequal workplaces, leaving CON’s efficiency argument looking like cost-shifting. CON’s points about waste and logistics were competent but never fully overcame the principled burden that work tools should be employer-provided by default, not extracted from private life.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s dismantling of CON’s analogies in Rebuttal 2 and Closing—showing that a uniform does not request location permissions, steel-toed boots do not send after-hours pings, and a wrench does not carry family messages and two-factor authentication—neutralized CON’s attempt to normalize the expectation and exposed the categorical difference between general-purpose personal devices and job-specific tools.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): The decisive moment came when CON's pressure question Q1 forced PRO to admit they would prohibit even a limited, reimbursed scheduling or safety app with no access to personal data and location only during paid field work. PRO's A1 answer confirmed this: "Yes, I would prohibit it." That admission validated CON's core framing throughout — that PRO's position is "technological absolutism" rather than a proportionate worker-protection principle. CON consistently landed a sharper comparative argument: the real harms (uncompensated costs, invasive permissions, off-hours pressure, unjustified tracking) are better addressed through enforceable rules, least-permission requirements, reimbursement, and alternatives than through a categorical ban that also blocks harmless routine uses. CON's "right to disconnect comes from availability rules, not plastic casing" rebuttal directly answered PRO's physical-separation argument, and the observation that employer-controlled OS devices can be more intrusive than a sandboxed personal-phone app was a genuine analytical insight. PRO had real rhetorical force — "colonize personal devices," "fantasy of corporate benevolence," the bedroom/bathroom distinction — and the privacy case had intuitive power. But PRO never recovered from the overinclusiveness exposed in the pressure round. PRO's closing recycled the same physical-boundaries framing without addressing why that framing survives the A1 admission. CON's three clipped speeches are a real execution cost that kept the margin competitive rather than dominant, but the visible argumentative work was consistently stronger in the clash that mattered most.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question Q1 ("Would you prohibit a limited, reimbursed scheduling or safety app with no contact/photo access and location only during paid field work?") and PRO's direct answer ("Yes, I would prohibit it") was the pivotal exchange. CON then correctly labeled this "categorical technological absolutism" and reframed the comparative: scope creep is an argument for enforceable scope limits, not a blanket ban. This exchange collapsed PRO's path to victory by revealing that their principle over-prohibits ordinary, low-risk coordination and is therefore not a credible worker-protection standard.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A won by exposing PRO's categorical refusal to allow even narrow, reimbursed, on-shift-limited apps, proving the motion overbroad and turning real-world safeguards (rules + least-permission) into the decisive comparison. B's physical-boundary absolutism remained unadapted and weakened by the pressure-question exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2/A4 pressure exchange: B's "yes prohibit limited app" answer allowed A to reframe as technological absolutism vs. targeted rules.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON demonstrated that the proposition is overbroad by forcing PRO to admit in the Pressure Questions round that even a limited, reimbursed scheduling or safety app with no access to personal data should be categorically prohibited. This concession made PRO&#39;s position seem like technological absolutism rather than a targeted worker protection. CON effectively argued that the real harms—uncompensated costs, excessive permissions, off-hours pressure, and unjustified tracking—are better addressed through reimbursement, least-permission requirements, deletion rights, and enforceable availability rules than by mandating a second device for every worker. CON also landed a sharp turn that employer-owned phones can be more intrusive because the employer controls the OS, settings, and location services, undermining PRO&#39;s claim that physical separation alone guarantees privacy or a right to disconnect. CON&#39;s closing crystallized the clash well: the protections PRO wants require rules, and with those rules, a limited app on a personal phone is sufficient; without them, a second phone still buzzes at dinner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON&#39;s pressure questions exposing PRO&#39;s categorical absolutism, followed by CON&#39;s turn that employer-owned devices can be less private because the employer controls the OS and settings—sharpening the clash around whether physical or regulatory boundaries actually protect workers.
