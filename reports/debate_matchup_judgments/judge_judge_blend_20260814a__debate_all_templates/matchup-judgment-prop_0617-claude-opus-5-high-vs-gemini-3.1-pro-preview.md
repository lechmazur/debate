### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A won by framing uncertainty as a burden-allocation problem and showing why dominant-platform acquisitions create uniquely irreversible risks. A effectively narrowed the motion, distinguished B's Android and Siri examples, and explained that expected integration benefits need verification rather than certainty. B's innovation-pipeline concern was plausible, but repeatedly treated a rebuttable presumption as a systematic ban and asserted an exceptionally rigid proof standard without establishing it. A's multiple clipped turns were an execution cost, keeping the result competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to the claimed guillotine was decisive: the unknowable counterfactual is different from the acquirer's knowable plans, capabilities, and reasons for purchasing. This directly undercut B's claim that proving substantial expected benefit is inherently impossible.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won the central burden clash. It narrowed scope to dominant-only (not all tech M&A), distinguished knowable acquirer plan from unknowable counterfactual to defuse the "impossible hurdle," and turned CON's own Android/Siri examples into easy benefit cases. CON never solved B's core pressure: the purely defensive "growing faster than us, buy and fold" deal that clears under status quo. B's irreversibility weighing — presumption as a reversible dial vs. tipped market that cannot be re-founded — was more precise than CON's symmetric irreversibility claim about unfunded startups, despite B's clipped openings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1/R2 turnaround of the unknowable future into knowable integration capability (Siri distribution example) plus the closing line that "seamless access" under CON is access to a product consumers already had from a rival that no longer exists — directly neutralizing CON's consumer-benefit claim.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by successfully narrowing the scope of the proposition to dominant platforms, dismantling Side B's broad argument that the policy would destroy all venture capital. A also provided a superior weighing mechanism, contrasting the permanent, irreversible harm of a tipped market with the elastic, correctable nature of capital flows. A's persistent pressure on the "killer acquisition" scenario forced B into a weak defensive position, making A's case for shifting the burden of proof much more compelling and logically sound.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it clarified that the presumption requires the acquirer to prove its own capabilities and integration plan rather than the unknowable counterfactual, effectively neutralizing Side B's core argument that the legal burden is an impossible guillotine.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing a persistent mismatch between the harm Side A targeted and the motion’s actual test. Defensive acquisitions may be harmful, but requiring provable substantial consumer benefit also blocks candid, uncertain investments without reliably identifying predatory intent. Side A conceded both broad cross-market scope and blocking deals whose benefits remain uncertain, while its claim that startups would simply find other buyers remained speculative. Side A had a strong irreversibility case, but B showed that false positives are also irreversible and invisible. B’s clipped opening was an execution cost, while A also repeatedly used nonexistent C-reference IDs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it used Side A’s direct answers to show that the rule reaches outside core markets and blocks honest uncertainty, then distinguished defensive intent from the consumer-benefit screen. That sharply undermined A’s claim that the presumption was targeted rather than effectively prohibitory for many innovative acquisitions.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (A) won the central clashes. PRO's info-asymmetry claim that board models equal consumer-benefit proof was undercut by A2.1-2.4 that models show private returns and become litigation targets. A's scope pressure in A3.1 forced B4.1 to admit presumption reaches outside the core market while hand-waving Android, which A4.7 then framed as blocking tomorrow's Android, and B's admission in B4.3 that 'we don't know' = blocked conceded A's option-value critique. A's invisible-error weighing (wrongly blocked deals leave no docket) answered B's irreversibility better than B answered A's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.6-A2.9 and A4.5-A4.7 - the market-specific vs firm-wide dominance dilemma that traps Android, plus the pipeline price concession and the uncertain-upside = guilt critique, which B4.1-B4.3 openly conceded.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing the critical flaw in the proposition's mechanism: shifting the burden to prove "substantial consumer benefit" does not solve the forecasting problem, it merely punishes honest uncertainty and option-value investing. B forced A into a dilemma regarding the scope of the rule (adjacent markets) and effectively argued that the test screens for provable certainty rather than defensive intent, allowing sophisticated predators to fake a benefit story while blocking candid innovators. While A's points on the irreversibility of "unscrambling" tipped markets were strong, B's counter that wrongly blocked mergers are equally irreversible and leave no record for correction neutralized this advantage. B's analytical distinctions were sharper and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of the "deal memo" argument and the pressure question forcing Side A to concede that the presumption applies to adjacent markets and blocks honest uncertainty, exposing the collateral damage to the broader startup ecosystem.
