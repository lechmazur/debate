### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B identified and sustained the decisive structural tension in PRO's proposal: that meaningful, comparable published evaluations necessarily double as capability certifications for malicious actors ("shopping catalog"/"search-cost collapse"). PRO's answer in Rebuttal 2 (abstract graded proxies via a versioned standards body) tried to have it both ways—comparable enough to end lab secrecy, vague enough to avoid advertisement—and B's closing rebuttal directly named this as a self-contradiction that PRO never resolved ("you cannot have comparability without a shopping list"). B also exposed an unresolved dilemma in PRO's compliance-and-exemption scheme (exempt open-source and risk proliferation, or don't and concentrate power/drive activity offshore), which PRO's "can be scoped or exempted" line never actually settled. PRO's status-quo critique (secret self-reporting, race-to-ship incentives) remained live and was never fully neutralized, keeping this competitive rather than a blowout, but B's sharper, more original framing (crash dummies ducking the test, painted floor over a sinkhole) and its closing crystallization of the comparability/advertisement bind gave it the cleaner, more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 turn (B4.4), later echoed in its closing: pointing out that PRO's own answer—graded proxies meaningful enough for outside comparison—necessarily functions as the "shopping recommendation" PRO had denied, a contradiction PRO never squarely resolved.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing a central tradeoff in the proposition: evaluations detailed enough to enable meaningful public comparison also reduce attackers' search costs by identifying the most capable targets. B paired this critique with a credible confidential-audit alternative that preserves cross-lab oversight and penalties without publishing capability rankings. A never established why public access adds defensive value that regulators cannot provide, and its closing mischaracterized mandatory confidential audits as optional branding. A defended a useful baseline against gaming objections, but could not resolve the publication dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered A's questions with the search-cost-collapse mechanism and explained how confidential regulators can compare labs, condition releases, and punish noncompliance. It then showed the tension in A's graded-proxy answer: if outsiders can rank models meaningfully, attackers can use the same ranking.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing a fatal contradiction in Side A's case: the publication of scores detailed enough to allow for meaningful public comparison will simultaneously act as a targeting guide for malicious actors, collapsing their search costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 systematically weaponized A4's answers, showing that A's need for "comparability" mandates the very "shopping list" A denied creating, and that A's "safe harbor" concept legally protects stale, gameable tests.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): A built a clear, escalating framework (irreversibility → collective-action failure → auditable floor → publication as risk-labeling, not recipe-sharing) and used it consistently across rounds. The decisive moment came when B conceded in Rebuttal 2/A2 that "both regimes fail the recall test" for leaked weights; A pivoted this concession into the strongest line of the debate—if nothing can un-release a model, the only lever left is preventing the catastrophic version from shipping, which is exactly what a pre-deployment gate targets and liability-after-harm cannot do. B never squarely dismantled this pivot in closing, instead restating that irreversibility "cuts both ways" without explaining how post-hoc liability actually prevents the first catastrophic release. B's strongest structural argument—regulatory arbitrage pushing capability offshore—was met with A's concentration point (frontier training requires known, chip-intensive datacenters), and B's rebuttal ("concentration invites capture") was asserted rather than mechanistically defended. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format cleanly with proper A1/A2 answers, but A's answers (independent standards body, expiring clearances, category-not-prompt disclosure) were more concrete and directly closed the loopholes B's questions opened, while B's answers mostly reasserted prior claims about gaming and false confidence without a new counter-mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4), turning B's concession that "both regimes fail the recall test" into the case that pre-deployment gating is the only tool that can act before an irreversible leak, was the single most decisive exchange; B's closing reasserted the offshore/residual-risk concern but never directly dismantled that pivot.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): A won by showing that incomplete evaluations can still catch serious, irreversible failures before deployment, while monitoring and liability are largely reactive. A also answered false-confidence and publication concerns with dated, qualified reports, redaction, and continued monitoring. B raised credible gaming, offshore, and dynamic-risk objections, but mostly established that evaluations are imperfect rather than that the mandate is net harmful. A did overstate the proposition as a mandatory safety gate rather than merely an evaluation-and-publication requirement, keeping the debate competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest response was that continuous monitoring complements rather than replaces pre-deployment evaluation: B conceded that fixed tests can detect anticipated bioweapon uplift, yet its alternative offered no way to undo an irreversible release. This undercut B’s attempt to make incompleteness a reason to abandon the baseline.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by effectively weaponizing the concept of irreversibility against Side B's post-deployment liability alternative. When A pressed B in the PQ round on how liability fixes irreversible weight leaks, B conceded that neither regime can recall weights and that pre-tests can catch first-order failures. A masterfully crystallized this in closing to prove pre-deployment testing is the only way to prevent un-recallable catastrophes. B also dropped A's critical point about the compute bottleneck limiting offshore evasion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Closing beautifully crystallized the PQ exchange, using B's concession that neither regime can recall leaked weights to prove that A's pre-deployment gate is strictly better than B's post-harm liability.
