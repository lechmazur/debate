### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A successfully exposed that the categorical "only if" structure of the motion is overly rigid and epistemically flawed. By highlighting the contested nature of bias definitions, the local-deployment gap in benchmark validity, and the concrete case of supervised documentation tools that improve on manual notes with mandatory physician review, Side A showed that beneficial, well-governed AI could be blocked by an arbitrary procedural gate. Side B argued powerfully for transparency and the dangers of proprietary opacity but never adequately resolved the ambiguity of who sets thresholds, which fairness definition governs, or why public reporting must veto all other validation routes. Side B's response that local pilots merely need to publish results either collapsed the benchmark requirement into a toothless formality or conceded that beneficial tools would be excluded, undermining their own safety-floor framework. Side A's pressure questions hit these structural weaknesses directly, and Side B's answers, while articulate, did not neutralize the overbreadth problem. Side A's rebuttals were sharper in narrowing the clash and exposing internal tensions in Side B's case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 dismantling of Side B's documentation-pilot response, demonstrating that treating any published local result as a "benchmark" eliminates meaningful thresholds while an external benchmark unjustly blocks beneficial, governed tools.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won on the logic of "only if." It kept pressure on bundled low-risk documentation vs high-risk triage, metric conflict among bias definitions, and the publication-vs-threshold dilemma: if any pilot counts once published the floor is toothless, if a preset public test is required it blocks a supervised tool that beats manual notes locally. PRO's floor-not-ceiling reply and pharma analogy didn't solve necessity, and the claim that reporting a pilot is trivial concedes CON's point. Despite the clipped R2, CON offered a clearer, enforceable alternative governance path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.5 / A2.2: turning automation bias against benchmarks and exposing that meeting a benchmark is not the same as merely publishing pilot results, collapsing PRO's attempt to make the requirement trivial.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A successfully dismantled the "only if" condition of the proposition, proving that making a publicly reported benchmark the exclusive gateway to reimbursement is either toothless (if any published local pilot counts as a benchmark) or actively harmful (if it blocks locally validated, supervised tools that do not fit a standardized external dataset). Side B struggled to resolve this dilemma, relying on pharmaceutical analogies that failed to account for the context-dependency and workflow integration of AI models. Side A's risk-tiered alternative was more clinically realistic, and their logical trapping of Side B's definitions in the later rounds decisively won the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they trapped Side B on the definition of a "benchmark." By forcing Side B to concede that publishing a successful local pilot could count as meeting a benchmark, Side A showed the standard lacked a meaningful threshold; conversely, if an external standardized threshold was required, it would arbitrarily block safe, locally proven tools.

### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins by successfully reframing the proposal as a flexible, risk-tiered evidentiary floor rather than a rigid one-size-fits-all gate. CON’s central attack—that mandatory benchmarks paralyze under-resourced hospitals—lost force once PRO showed that benchmarks can be locally validated, lighter for supervised tools, and funded by vendors. CON never squarely answered why validated local results could not simply be reported, instead retreating to speculative claims of crippling overhead. PRO’s weighing that bounded compliance costs are preferable to dispersing systematic errors onto patients proved decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s second rebuttal (B4) collapsing CON’s alleged contradiction between local validation and objective standards, showing that common safety endpoints with population-specific testing and prespecified thresholds are coherent, proportional, and place the burden of proof on vendors seeking public reimbursement.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO won the core definitional fight. CON's best push was the dilemma - flexible local tests aren't comparable, strict national tests freeze safety-net use. PRO in B2/B4 resolved it with risk-tiered common outcomes, local population testing, vendor-borne costs, and reuse of evidence, keeping the proposition as a necessary floor not a warranty. CON never answered B3 Q1 well - if local validation already exists, why can't it be publicly reported before reimbursement - and its A4 answers relied on overhead labels rather than comparative weighing of scaled systematic error vs bounded compliance cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 A1/A2 - PRO shows comparability via shared safety endpoints without identical datasets and offers prompt proportionate validation, collapsing CON's rigidity vs meaninglessness dilemma and answering the resource-strap clinic objection.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully defended the proposition by clarifying that "publicly reported benchmarks" does not require a single, rigid national exam, but can involve local testing against shared safety outcomes. This neutralized CON's primary argument about bureaucratic paralysis and "teaching to the test." CON struggled to explain why publishing the results of necessary local validation would be a crippling burden, leaving PRO with the stronger, more adaptable case for evidentiary floors before reimbursement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2, where it clearly answered the pressure questions by explaining how common safety outcomes can be measured on local populations without requiring identical national test sets, effectively collapsing CON's supposed dilemma between rigid centralization and meaningless local discretion.
