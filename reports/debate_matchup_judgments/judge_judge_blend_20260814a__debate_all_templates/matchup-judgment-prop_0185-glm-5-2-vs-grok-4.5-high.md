- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases, but PRO controlled the decisive clash. CON's answer conceded "no mechanism fully prevents waiver," relying instead on "corrigibility" (future correction via elections/markets). PRO seized on this concession in rebuttal and closing, converting it into a clean asymmetry frame—"costly evasion beats free evasion"—that CON never squarely dismantled; CON's final closing pivoted to a related but distinct point (that rule-compliance costs hit legitimate spending too) rather than directly refuting the asymmetry itself. CON's case remained strong on judicial-capacity concerns and the false-choice problem for multi-year investment, and it never fully collapsed, but it left the central pressure point standing. PRO's closing was clipped for exceeding the word limit, which is a real execution cost, but the decisive argument had already been fully stated beforehand, so the substantive damage to CON's position stands.

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built and sustained the decisive dilemma of the debate: any enforceable constitutional balance rule collapses into judicial fiscal policy, while any unenforceable version is just a norm indistinguishable from legislative discretion. PRO never escaped this trap. When PRO answered the pressure questions by invoking "voters and markets" as the enforcement backstop for constitutional sunsets, CON landed the decisive rebuttal: PRO's opening case rested on voters failing to discipline deficits under discretion, so invoking voter/market enforcement for the constitutional version is self-undermining — if voters/markets can enforce one, they can enforce the other. PRO's closing reasserted that constitutions make tools "binding defaults" but never resolved this specific contradiction, instead repeating the focal-point framing already shown to reduce to the same mechanisms CON described. CON also pressed unanswered points about entrenchment for novel structural shocks (climate, demographic change) that PRO's "certified temporary windows" never squarely addressed. PRO's opening and early rebuttals were solid and the deficit-bias mechanism was plausible, but the case never survived the enforcement-dichotomy pressure, making CON's path to victory cleaner and more resolved by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1) catching PRO's answer to Q1 in an internal contradiction: PRO relies on "voters and bond markets" to enforce constitutional sunsets after claiming pure discretion fails precisely because voters won't discipline deficits — a contradiction PRO never resolved in Rebuttal 2 or Closing.

When Side A conceded that constitutional rules ultimately rely on voters and markets for residual enforcement, Side B effectively pointed out that these are the exact same mechanisms available under legislative discretion, leaving rigidity as the only unique addition of Side A's proposal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 8 (Side B Rebuttal 2) decisively used Side A's own answers about multi-year plans and market enforcement to prove that Side A's constitutional rule is either redundant to legislative discretion or functionally forces judges to dictate economic policy.
