- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (A) built and sustained a single clean decision rule—"marginal impact per dollar"—and used it to dismantle PRO's harms one by one: statutory bans on lunch-shaming and invisible swipe-card payment kill stigma without universal provision, direct certification plus community eligibility catch most missing-middle cases, and a sliding-scale copay eliminates the cliff PRO kept pointing to. PRO's closing recapped its framing dichotomy without confronting the two live drops (the excludability rebuttal and "what does the public gain from feeding the already-fed"), while CON's closing crystallized exactly why those drops matter. CON therefore made the clash easier to resolve and left fewer open threads.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's excludability point in Rebuttal 1 ("you cannot heat one seat, but you can feed one child")—directly answering PRO's bus/library/heating analogy—which PRO never adequately rebutted in Rebuttal 2 or Closing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON kept the debate on marginal impact per dollar and answered PRO's strongest harms directly: cheese-sandwich shaming banned by statute, cliff fixed by taper/free reduced-price, debt as rounding error vs billions for universal, and excludability distinction that undercuts bus/heating analogy. - **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A built a much stronger comparative case by demonstrating that the harms of means-testing (stigma, administrative burden, the "missing middle") are implementation flaws that can be solved with cheap, targeted policy fixes (e.g., no-shame laws, making reduced-price meals free, direct certification). Side A successfully defended the opportunity cost argument, showing that the billions required for universality would divert crucial funds from other educational needs, while Side B failed to prove that universality was the only way to ensure no child goes hungry. Side A's excludability distinction regarding the heating/bus analogies was particularly sharp and decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they introduced the specific policy of making reduced-price meals free to eliminate the "missing middle" cliff and stigma at a fraction of the cost of universality, completely neutralizing Side B's core equity arguments.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): B built a coherent, adaptive case: stigma/uptake failure, "programs for the poor become poor programs," and the recurring "a meal only feeds a child if the child eats it" frame gave a clear decision rule. Crucially, B's pressure questions (cliff, lunch debt, forgone benefits) exposed that every fix A offered (raise the threshold, forbid denial, Direct Certification) still leaves a structural gap, and B's closing explicitly turned each of A's patches into a concession that the "sorting machine" still injures some children. A's opportunity-cost and quality-dilution arguments were substantive and repeatedly pressed, but A's answers to B's pressure questions were partly evasive (dismissing the cliff as merely "not a crisis" rather than resolving it), and A's final closing reasserted Direct Certification without rebutting B's direct counter that mismatched records still leave kids uncovered — a missed opportunity with the last speech. B's closing therefore landed more decisively unanswered, giving it the cleaner, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that A's fixes (raised threshold, no-denial policy, Direct Certification) each still leave a residual gap — "a quieter motor on the same sorting machine" — landed hardest, especially since A's closing failed to directly answer B's specific "records mismatch" rebuttal to Direct Certification.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) keeps the decisive mechanism alive: stigma/uptake is not just at the register but in applications, cliffs, and lunch debt, so targeted systems fail to feed by design. Side A's PIN and Direct Certification answers reduce but do not erase that failure mode, and its zero-sum budget claim was met by reimbursement scaling and bulk-purchase logic. With the effectiveness clash favoring B and the cost clash only partially won by A, PRO's weighing — a meal only feeds if eaten — provides the cleaner vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO Rebuttal 1 [B2.1-B2.2]: PIN solves last 5 seconds not first 5 months; debt shaming moves shaming from ticket to cold cheese sandwich/tray dump, exposing that CON's tech fix does not end sorting.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by maintaining a relentless focus on the mechanical and practical failures of means-testing (stigma, lunch debt, application friction) and effectively dismantling Side A's proposed patches. While Side A relied on strong macro-economic arguments about opportunity costs and regressive subsidies, Side B successfully reframed school meals as foundational educational infrastructure and exposed the flaws in Side A's "tech fixes" and Direct Certification proposals. Side B's rhetoric—particularly the "sorting machine" analogy and the contrast between a complex bureaucracy and "one moving part: the tray"—made their comparative case much cleaner and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they systematically dismantled Side A's patches (billing parents for lunch debt, auto-enrollment via Medicaid), demonstrating that every administrative requirement in a targeted system is a point of failure where a hungry child can slip through, whereas a universal system guarantees the meal reaches the child.
