### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a tighter, more consistent case around asymmetry: flexibility is exercised immediately and irreversibly by employers, while universal protection is a delayed, uncertain claim on state capacity. This "power now vs. claim later" framing was sustained and sharpened across every round. CON's pressure question forcing PRO to name concrete replacement rates/duration was well-aimed, and PRO's answer ("budget-design choices, not reasons to reject the proposition") effectively conceded that the second half of the bargain remains unpriced — a concession CON exploited decisively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing. CON also landed a substantive hit by noting that PRO's own safeguard (grandfathering existing workers while putting new hires on the flexible track) reproduces exactly the two-tier insider/outsider system PRO's opening condemned. PRO fought back competently — distinguishing regulated fixed-term work from off-book informality, reframing bargaining power as portability, and offering sequencing/sunset/anti-churn mechanisms — but these remained largely aspirational and never fully answered the irreversibility problem or the unpriced-floor critique. Both sides followed Q&A format correctly and avoided fake citations, but CON's line of attack proved more durable and was never squarely neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.6), pointing out that PRO's grandfathering safeguard entrenches the exact two-tier bargain PRO's opening claimed to abolish, combined with CON's pressure question forcing PRO to concede the protection "floor" remains an unnamed, unpriced budget choice.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) kept the stronger live route: security should follow the worker, conditionality and reversibility answer sequencing, and a written fixed-term contract with notice, severance and portable benefits beats off-book precarity. Side A (CON) landed sharp hits on irreversibility and everyday power vs delayed claim, but never answered what rigid attachment does for the informal majority and asserted the net could be built without changing hiring incentives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's baseline reversal in R1 that informal workers are already replaceable without records, so regulated FTC with evidence and portable benefits improves voice and enforceability compared to status quo.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully defending the core trade-off of the proposition and winning the comparative framing. While Side B mounted a strong practical attack on the sequencing and implementation of universal social protection, Side A effectively neutralized this by proposing conditional activation, statutory sunsets, and reversibility mechanisms. More decisively, Side A exposed the flaw in Side B's defense of "job attachment": it implicitly defends a two-tier system that leaves the vast informal majority with zero protection and zero bargaining power. Side A convincingly argued that portable benefits and regulated flexibility offer enforceable security to the broader workforce, whereas Side B's insistence on building the net without the exchange ignored the causal link Side A established between rigid exit costs and the incentive to hire off-book.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered Side B's pressure questions with concrete legislative mechanisms (sunsets, automatic suspension, audited timeliness) to ensure the "exchange" is not a one-way giveaway, effectively blunting Side B's primary implementation attack.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON built and sustained the single load-bearing argument the whole debate turned on: dismissal flexibility is immediately self-executing while universal protection is administratively and fiscally contingent, so the "exchange" is structurally irreversible on one side and speculative on the other. PRO never closed this gap with a concrete mechanism; its "simultaneous trigger" answer to Q1 was exposed in A4.3 as facing an unresolved dilemma (too narrow to protect any individual worker, or too sweeping to be administrable), and PRO's closing pivoted away from defending the trigger toward attacking CON's Q1 answer as an unreachable "veto" rather than repairing its own mechanism. CON's answers to pressure questions (specific enrollment/entitlement/appeals/funding conditions, plus a predictable-downsizing alternative with notice, severance, and adjudication) were concrete and arguably themselves a form of flexibility, blunting the "veto" charge. PRO's core causal claim — that easier firing converts informal into formal employment — remained asserted ("conversion, not invention," "cheaper than the cycle") rather than demonstrated, and CON's demand-driven-headcount rebuttal was never squarely answered. PRO's rhetoric and reframe ("protection should travel with the person") were vivid and memorable, but rhetoric alone did not substitute for closing the causal and sequencing gaps CON kept pressing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.1/A4.3 sequencing critique — dismissal law is self-executing while benefit delivery is contingent, and any "simultaneous trigger" is either too narrow to protect individuals or too sweeping to be workable — was never repaired by PRO, whose closing shifted to attacking CON's own standard instead of defending the trigger mechanism.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON wins the central clash. PRO's case needs two links: flexibility causes informal-to-formal conversion, and simultaneous reform avoids sequencing risk. CON shows dismissal liberalization is self-executing while universal benefits need enrollment, payment, portability; PRO's trigger (B4.1) is torn apart in A4.3 as either too narrow to restore a lost job or too sweeping to give firms predictability. CON also presses demand-determines-headcount and tax/safety evasion, making conversion speculative, while churn of stable jobs is definite. PRO never rehabilitates the causal transfer and retreats from more jobs to conversion. The floor is thus separable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A in Rebuttal 2 dismantling PRO's simultaneous trigger - showing an individual trigger can't restore a job and a national trigger destroys the predictability PRO says drives formalization - plus the definite churn vs speculative conversion weighing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully dismantling the causal link between contract flexibility and formalization, and by exposing the practical unworkability of Side A's "legal trigger" safeguard. Side B effectively argued that the exchange was asymmetrical—granting immediate, definite firing rights to employers in exchange for a complex, administratively fragile safety net for workers. While Side A's rhetorical framing of the dual labor market was strong, Side B's logical rigor on the mechanics of the exchange and the multi-causal nature of informality secured the victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 dismantling Side A's "legal trigger" mechanism, showing that an individual trigger cannot restore a lost job and a nationwide trigger would create chaotic legal uncertainty, thereby neutralizing Side A's main defense against the sequencing problem.
