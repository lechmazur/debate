### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but PRO holds the stronger live route to victory after the full exchange. PRO's core burden—that civilian institutions cannot function in cartel-controlled territory without some coercive backing—was never squarely answered by CON. CON's "build from the outside in through financial trails" answer in Rebuttal 2 describes how federal prosecutors ideally operate but does not address the physical problem of witnesses who cannot move through cartel checkpoints, local police chiefs who are murdered for cooperating, or prosecutors who cannot execute warrants in contested zones. CON's best arguments were the 2006 historical precedent and the budget-cannibalization mechanism; these are genuinely strong, and PRO's "sequencing" response is somewhat asserted rather than demonstrated. But PRO's Q2 pressure question exposed a real gap in CON's position: if commander capture risks fragmentation and urban enforcement risks civilian harm, no threshold would ever justify force against an armed group capable of engineering standoffs. CON's answer—force justified only for imminent massacre—conceded the principle while struggling to distinguish it from the operational context PRO was describing. PRO also landed the cleaner closing crystallization: "the stronger the criminal army, the more cautious the state must be—that is how sovereignty is lost" and "civilian justice needs a shield before it can be a remedy" are memorable framings that sharpened the decisive clash. CON's closing was somewhat repetitive, returning to the "house burned down" line without adding new comparative pressure. PRO's clipped turns impose a real execution cost, but the visible content was strong enough to sustain the edge. Margin is clear but competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's reframing of the fragmentation argument as a permanent immunity doctrine (A2.2)—"if every capture is avoided because successors may fight, then criminal commanders effectively become untouchable"—combined with Q2 in the pressure round (what trigger would ever justify force if commander capture risks fragmentation and urban enforcement risks civilian harm?) was the most decisive exchange. CON's answer conceding force only for imminent massacre, while trying to exclude standing strategy, highlighted the logical tension in CON's position without resolving it.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A won by effectively arguing that Side B's proposed solutions (prosecution, financial intelligence) fundamentally require the physical security that only a harder approach can provide. Side B made strong historical points about the 2006 strategy, but struggled to explain how the state can execute warrants or extract witnesses in territories physically controlled by cartels.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument that Side B's fear of fragmentation and urban casualties effectively grants cartels a permanent immunity doctrine and veto over the law.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B wins by successfully turning Side A's "security first" framing into a liability, demonstrating through clear causal mechanisms and historical empirics that militarization cannibalizes institutional budgets, triggers cartel fragmentation, and fails to produce convictions. Side B's rebuttals more effectively dismantled the operational feasibility of Side A's case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 directly answering Side A's pressure questions by explaining how outside-in financial investigations and relocated witnesses function without military occupation, while simultaneously turning Side A's "holding territory" plan into a proof of budget cannibalization and political failure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): PRO wins narrowly by maintaining a cleaner path through the core institutional clash. The most important exchange is about whether compromised institutions undercut PRO's strategy more than CON's own. PRO turns this effectively: if local police, prosecutors, and mayors are too corrupted to sustain PRO's campaign, they are equally too corrupted to carry out CON's "targeted enforcement and institutional consolidation." CON's response—that their approach relies on vetted capacity at narrow chokepoints rather than a sweeping campaign—is a real distinction, but it is abstract and never resolves the same dependency problem PRO exposes. PRO's causal argument is also the stronger one: cartel intimidation is itself what prevents institutional reform, so security breathing room is the mandatory precondition rather than a rival to institution-building. That argument is live throughout and CON never squarely neutralizes it. CON's strongest move—the "transfer" problem—lands real pressure and PRO's answer ("federally overseen civilian administrators") is somewhat vague and reasonably tagged by CON as emergency governance. That gap costs PRO some credibility. But CON's own alternative carries the same unanswered question about who protects vetted prosecutors in contested territory, and CON never makes that distinction sharp enough to flip the comparison. The "unfalsifiability" point about half-measures is clever but also cuts against CON: if no level of force can be certified as sufficient, that applies equally to CON's graduated enforcement. On the core proposition—has avoidance failed and is a harder approach now justified—PRO establishes failure clearly (CON largely concedes this) and provides a plausible causal mechanism for justification that CON does not fully defeat. The margin is real but the debate is genuinely competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2.3–A2.5 turning the institutional corruption argument back on CON: the very logic CON uses to say force cannot work (institutions are too compromised) applies with equal force to CON's own "targeted enforcement plus institutional consolidation" plan, since that plan also depends on reliable prosecutors and mayors. CON's response—emphasizing vetted, narrow, and gradual capacity rather than sweeping campaigns—is defensible but never escapes the underlying dependency, which remains a live vulnerability for CON throughout.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Both sides presented exceptional arguments and memorable rhetoric. Side B wins by successfully trapping Side A on the "transfer" mechanism, demonstrating that Side A's plan ultimately leads to indefinite military occupation. Side A's attempt to catch Side B in a contradiction over corrupt institutions was strong, but Side B cleanly escaped by distinguishing between sweeping offensives and gradual "vetted federal units," a distinction Side A ignored in its closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 attack on Side A's limiting principle, pointing out that if the army stays until the cartel stops collecting tolls, the cartel will simply wait out the rotations—forcing Side A into either permanent occupation or the very "half-measure" they condemned.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): CON wins by consistently isolating the fatal bottleneck in PRO's clear-hold-transfer model: the transfer phase. PRO effectively argues that institutions cannot be rebuilt under cartel coercion, but CON demonstrates that PRO's mechanism relies on an unsubstantiated leap from military control to institutional viability. CON successfully argues that PRO's hold-until-the-state-collects-tolls standard either leads to indefinite military governance or recreates the exact vacuum and half-measure dynamic PRO criticizes. CON also maintains a clear distinction between bounded, capacity-tied enforcement and all-out confrontation, preventing PRO from collapsing the debate. CON's closing metric, force subordinated to lawful capacity versus force asked to substitute for it, provides the cleaner comparative reason to reject the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 dismantling of PRO's transfer mechanism and limiting principle. By showing that federally overseen administrators is just a label for emergency rule and that PRO's exit criteria trap them in either indefinite occupation or the same half-measures they condemn, CON neutralized PRO's core sequencing argument and framed the decisive clash.
