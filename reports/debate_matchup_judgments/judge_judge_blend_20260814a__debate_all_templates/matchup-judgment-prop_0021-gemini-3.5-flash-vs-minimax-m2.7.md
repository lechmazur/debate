### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. A built a strong ecological case around trophic cascades, the regulatory void left by predators, and the inadequacy of permanent human culling. But A leaned heavily on Yellowstone and sometimes overstated collapse and self-regulation. B more effectively attacked the generalizability of that model to fragmented working landscapes, showed that predator reintroduction still requires ongoing state intervention, and made the social-license argument central to the motion’s “even when rural communities object” wording. A’s “local objection cannot be a veto” response was strong, but B better explained why forced carnivore reintroduction specifically risks poaching, resentment, and loss of cooperation across broader conservation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s “permanent treadmill” argument was the decisive exchange: B argued that reintroduction does not remove management burdens but shifts them into compensation, fencing, and controversial predator culls, weakening A’s claim that predators uniquely solve the problem.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): CON won by squarely neutralizing PRO’s Yellowstone-style self-regulation claim with European conflict evidence and by making social license the decisive burden that PRO never fully discharged despite direct pressure-question answers. PRO’s case remained stronger on long-term regulatory mechanisms but weaker on applicability to working landscapes and consent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 exchange, reframing predator management as a permanent conflict treadmill rather than escape from one, directly answering both pressure questions and sharpening the cooperation-vs-engineering comparison.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully contextualizing the motion to fragmented, human-dominated working lands and demonstrating that forced reintroduction does not eliminate human management but merely shifts it to controversial lethal controls and conflict mitigation. CON’s argument that overriding local vetoes “exports trust” and destroys the social license necessary for broader conservation initiatives provided a decisive comparative weighing mechanism that PRO never fully neutralized. While PRO offered a compelling ecological case for trophic cascades and cleverly distinguished predator “tuning” from prey culling, CON more effectively addressed the practical and political realities of the motion’s “even when object” clause, showing how top-down imposition institutionalizes conflict and undermines the cooperative stewardship required for sustainable ecosystem restoration.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over the “permanent treadmill” of management was decisive. PRO argued predators self-regulate, ending the need for constant human culling. CON effectively countered by pointing to European examples where predator reintroduction simply shifted state intervention from deer culling to controversial, taxpayer-funded lethal predator controls and conflict mitigation, proving that forced reintroduction institutionalizes conflict rather than escaping management. This successfully blunted PRO’s core comparative advantage.
