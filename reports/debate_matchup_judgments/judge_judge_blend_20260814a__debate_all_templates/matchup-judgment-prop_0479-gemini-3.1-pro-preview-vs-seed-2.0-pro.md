### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A built a more durable causal case across the full debate. Its three core claims — (1) the physical asymmetry between permanent fossil emissions and impermanent biological storage, (2) the profit-motive trap that makes cheap offsets a permanent escape hatch without a legal ceiling, and (3) the structural transformation argument that forcing in-chain investment drives learning curves and reduces the green premium — each held up through the exchange. Side B's most effective move was the marginal abatement cost argument (10x efficiency per dollar), but Side A neutralised it adequately by distinguishing static cost-per-ton from the dynamic value of deploying clean technology at scale. Side B's main defensive response to the permanence point — buffer pools supplemented by DAC scaling to 2050 — was the weakest moment in their case epistemically, leaning on speculative future technology while attacking PRO for relying on "unproven hypothetical progress." Side A's Q1 (what mechanism forces airlines to invest offset savings in R&D?) exposed a real gap: Side B's best answer in B4.1 essentially conceded there is no such mechanism and suggested writing separate targeted rules, which is a partial concession to the policy need PRO identifies. Side B's hard-to-abate sector concern was handled adequately by PRO pointing out that a cap reserves offsets precisely for those residual emissions, so the concern applies more forcefully to a zero-offset ban than to the actual proposition. Side A's closing successfully crystallised the paradox (CON needs breakthrough tech but defends a system that routes capital away from it) without introducing late new material. Side B's closing repeated the 1-ton-vs-10-tons frame without resolving the profit-motive gap or the permanence objection, leaving those clashes live in PRO's favour.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Pressure Question 2 — asking how replacing a burned offset tree with another temporary tree from a buffer pool permanently neutralises fossil carbon released for millennia — was the most decisive single exchange. Side B's answer in B4.2 pivoted to DAC scaling by 2050, which was speculative and internally inconsistent with their broader critique of PRO's reliance on unproven future technology. This left the core permanence asymmetry unresolved and standing in PRO's favour for the rest of the debate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): B built the stronger path by showing caps force structural change and that buffer pools do not resolve impermanence, while directly answering pressure questions; A’s near-term efficiency framing was never fully reconciled with the long-term transformation burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3–B2.4 reframing CON’s static cost comparison as a dynamic lock-in trap that stalls learning curves.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by establishing that without a legal cap, profit-maximizing firms will perpetually substitute cheap offsets for structural decarbonization, destroying the market signal needed to commercialize clean technology. Side B's marginal-abatement-cost and tipping-point arguments were competitive, but Side A's pressure questions exposed that Side B had no credible mechanism to redirect offset savings into R&D rather than shareholder returns. Side A also maintained a cleaner physical distinction between permanent fossil emissions and temporary biological storage, while showing that a residual offset allowance already accommodates hard-to-abate sectors. Side B's closing reliance on speculative future direct air capture to solve impermanence was less grounded than Side A's carbon-cycle reasoning.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), which directly answered Side B's pressure questions by distinguishing residual emissions from systemic loopholes, and sharply rebutted buffer pools by noting that setting aside more temporary trees does not alter the fundamental physical asymmetry between millennial fossil carbon and decades-long biological storage.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) secured the win by establishing a clearer comparative framework centered on near-term climate tipping points and cost-effectiveness (tons reduced per dollar). CON successfully neutralized PRO's structural transformation argument by demonstrating that a blunt cap forces capital into low-impact marginal upgrades rather than breakthrough R&D, while offering superior alternative mechanisms like strict verification standards and targeted innovation mandates. PRO delivered strong rhetoric on corporate incentives and the physical mismatch of fossil versus biological carbon, but consistently struggled to answer CON's policy design critiques and the urgency of the 25-year mitigation window. CON's direct, substantive answers to pressure questions and tighter weighing made the decision clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.1 cleanly severed PRO's main causal link by arguing that an offset cap does not guarantee R&D investment but instead forces expensive, low-yield internal tweaks, whereas targeted R&D mandates would achieve PRO's stated goal without sacrificing near-term emission volumes. This directly answered PRO's pressure question and reframed the capital allocation clash decisively in CON's favor.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): CON built a cleaner comparative path through consistent exploitation of PRO's most damaging concession. When PRO stated at [A2.3] that losing offset-reliant pledges is "no loss," CON correctly identified this as a structural vulnerability and returned to it throughout—pointing out that these pledges currently finance solar grids and coal retirements in the developing world, so if companies abandon them, the atmosphere gets nothing and those real climate projects disappear entirely. This reversal was never squarely neutralized by PRO. CON also deployed the "trickle-down climate economics" frame effectively in response to PRO's cost-spillover argument, and the "immediate math" argument (we cannot wait decades for Western R&D while a coal plant burns today) proved more persistently damaging than PRO's lock-in thesis. PRO's structural case—that offsets allow permanent fossil fuel dependence by removing the incentive to scale zero-emission technology—is genuinely sound and was never fully answered by CON, but execution was weak. PRO consistently used well under the word cap across every round, leaving key burdens underdeveloped: the EU ETS response to CON's "who defines unavoidable" pressure was brief and partially satisfying but not decisive, and the Global South funding response was undermined by CON's pointed observation that limiting offsets to a "small share" mathematically guts the majority of the demand financing those projects. Both sides followed the required A1/A2 format cleanly. CON's marginally stronger rebuttal discipline and more memorable framing across the later rounds tips the balance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 hammering PRO's "no loss" admission [A2.3] as a "staggering admission"—paired with the clarification that high-integrity offsets increasingly fund permanent systemic progress (solar arrays, coal retirements, methane capture) rather than just fragile forestry credits—materially damaged PRO's clean dismissal of the offset abandonment scenario and established the "atmosphere gets nothing" throughline CON rode to the close.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by more convincingly showing that caps risk slashing Global South funding and causing pledge abandonment, with sharper direct answers to pressure questions on credibility and regulation; B's systemic-change emphasis was undercut by A's math and equity framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2, flipping B's "no loss" claim into concrete loss of immediate reductions.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A won by neutralizing CON's key feasibility objections with concrete regulatory examples and maintaining a live systemic lock-in argument. PRO's rebuttal citing existing sector-specific benchmarks deflated CON's "bureaucratic nightmare" claim, which was largely dropped in later speeches. PRO also handled pressure questions more directly, showing that the cap preserves near-term integrity while forcing sector-specific R&D, whereas CON's closing repeated frames without resolving how hard-to-abate sectors would decarbonize without value-chain pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answer to B's Pressure Question 2, citing existing EU ETS and EPA benchmark frameworks to demonstrate that defining "commercially unavoidable" emissions is administratively feasible and already practiced. This decisively undermined CON's "paralyzing litigation" objection and left it largely unanswered in the closing.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON wins by decisively framing the core tradeoff as immediate, verifiable atmospheric impact versus a speculative, delayed technological transition. CON's "trickle-down climate economics" label successfully undercut PRO's tech-spillover defense, and CON's mathematical point about gutting offset demand directly challenged PRO's claim that Global South climate finance would survive a strict cap. PRO offered strong structural arguments about fossil lock-in and handled the regulatory pressure well with concrete benchmark examples, but ultimately could not resolve the weighing disadvantage on time-sensitive climate action or fully answer the charge that forced in-chain spending prices hard-to-abate sectors out of voluntary mitigation entirely. CON's comparative weighing on urgency and funding certainty provided the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2, which directly answered PRO's pressure questions while deploying the "trickle-down climate economics" frame and a sharp mathematical challenge to PRO's claim that a capped offset market would still sufficiently fund developing-world clean energy. This exchange crystallized the time and equity tradeoffs that ultimately decided the round.
