### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): B won by making the anonymity clash decisive. A had a plausible case that payment could reduce overburden and improve accountability, but B more convincingly argued that public attributed reviews would chill honest criticism by junior scholars because retaliation is quiet and structural. A’s answer that transparency would expose retaliation was less grounded and did not solve the career-risk problem. B’s payment critique was more contestable, but combined with the largely unanswered access and market-pressure concerns, it gave CON the clearer overall reason the proposal would work worse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s repeated answer to A’s transparency argument was the key exchange: public reports do not prevent retaliation because academic punishment happens in grants, hiring, and tenure, while anonymity at least preserves plausible deniability for vulnerable reviewers.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): A wins because its defense of anonymity as essential protection for juniors and its exposure of how payment creates publisher-driven speed pressures were more convincingly sustained and less neutralized across rebuttals and closings. B's transparency and incentive claims never fully overcame the structural power dynamics A highlighted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.2 and A4.3 on silent retaliation in tenure/grants vs B4.3's community-sanction hope, which A effectively showed as unrealistic given academic hierarchies.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): Side B won by more convincingly defending the comparative stakes. While Side A effectively showed the current system is strained, it never squarely answered Side B's harm that payment would balloon article-processing costs and tier access to wealthy labs. More decisively, Side B's retaliation argument proved more robust: Side A claimed public reviews would let the community sanction bias, but Side B demonstrated that academic retaliation is structural and silent (tenure, grants, hiring), making transparency a trap that eliminates plausible deniability without preventing backroom punishment. This materially damaged Side A's route to victory on accountability and junior-researcher safety, leaving Side B's core harms largely live while Side A's benefits remained contested.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8) answering Side A's pressure questions by distinguishing capacity problems from compensation distortions and pressing that transparency eliminates plausible deniability while doing nothing to stop silent, structural retaliation in hiring and funding decisions.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): Side B wins by grounding its objections in the structural realities of academia and academic publishing. While Side A offers a coherent idealistic model where payment buys time and transparency enables accountability, Side B effectively dismantles this by highlighting that academic retaliation is quiet and structural (occurring in closed grant/tenure committees), not public, making transparency a liability for junior scholars rather than a shield. Side B also convincingly argues that profit-driven publishers would inevitably tie payment to throughput, degrading review quality. Side A’s responses relied on normative hopes about community sanctions and journal credibility that failed to overcome Side B’s descriptive reality checks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 1 and 2 on power dynamics. By explaining that retaliation happens silently in closed-door committees rather than publicly, Side B neutralized Side A’s core claim that transparency protects junior reviewers, turning Side A’s accountability mechanism into a demonstrated chilling effect.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (PRO); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON)): B wins narrowly. A made a strong case that unpaid, anonymous review creates burnout and opacity, and its “professionalization plus accountability” frame was coherent. But B’s objections to public reports chilling junior reviewers and to costs being passed onto authors remained insufficiently answered. A’s contract-quality answer helped on payment, but its transparency defense relied too much on “public scrutiny” without solving private retaliation or self-censorship. B gave the clearer reason the proposed cure could be worse than the disease.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal on transparency was most decisive: it directly answered A’s “paper trail” claim by explaining that retaliation can occur privately through grants, collaborations, and career channels, so publication may chill candid criticism rather than protect it.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (PRO)): Side B wins by establishing that the unpaid anonymous model already produces shallow rushed reviews and unchecked gatekeeping, then showing payment plus transparency supplies contractual leverage and public accountability without the claimed corruption or chilling effects; Side A repeatedly returns to romanticized duty without neutralizing those concrete mechanisms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1/A2 answers in Rebuttal 2, directly contracting the throughput incentive and retaliation fear with existing contractual norms and community scrutiny.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (PRO); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON)): Side B successfully demonstrated that the proposed model would introduce perverse incentives worse than the status quo's flaws. B's argument that payment rewards completion rather than rigor—because journals prioritize throughput—and that public reports would chill necessary criticism through fear of covert retaliation, remained materially unresolved by A. A's profit-redirection and contractual-quality mechanisms were plausible but insufficiently grounded to overcome B's concrete incentive analysis. B's pressure questions were sharper, its answers to A's questions were more direct, and its closing crystallized the commodification risk more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 argument that transparency cannot prevent private retaliation—since senior researchers can covertly block grants or collaborations despite a public paper trail—decisively undermined A's accountability mechanism and exposed a lasting structural flaw in the proposal.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (PRO); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON)): Side B wins by more convincingly dismantling PRO's causal mechanisms. While PRO argued that payment and contracts would professionalize review, CON effectively demonstrated that tying compensation to reports aligns reviewer incentives with publishers' desire for throughput, inherently rewarding speed and completion over rigor. CON also grounded the transparency clash in realistic academic power dynamics, showing how public reports would chill junior scholars' criticism and how covert retaliation (e.g., grant blocking) easily bypasses PRO's proposed public paper trail. PRO's reliance on idealistic contractual enforcement and smooth profit redirection failed to overcome CON's structural critique of perverse incentives, financial barriers, and the chilling effect on frank critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's contractual leverage claim, arguing that journals' financial interest in throughput creates an irreconcilable conflict where payment per report inherently rewards completion over depth, effectively neutralizing PRO's core mechanism for ensuring quality.
