### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that Side A repeatedly treated successful diversification as requiring fiscal independence to be substantially complete already. B plausibly distinguished oil-funded investment from mere relabeling: ports, aviation, tourism assets, legal reforms, and new revenue channels can constitute substantive conversion even while build-out keeps fiscal breakevens high. A exposed real subsidy and labor-market risks, but leaned too heavily on unsupported Gulf-wide breakeven figures and an excessive test that one non-oil export should cover imports at low oil prices. B therefore offered the clearer path from present reforms to likely success, though its erroneous reference IDs were an execution blemish.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer that a high fiscal breakeven can reflect accelerated transition spending rather than failed conversion. This directly weakened A’s central inference from current oil dependence to mere public relations, while B’s concession that no single sector presently replaces oil made the transition-versus-arrival distinction more credible.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully exposing the circular nature of Gulf non-oil economies, proving that because the state subsidizes both the inputs and the demand, the new infrastructure remains wholly dependent on oil rents. Side A's "effort vs. arrival" framing was excellent, but Side B overcame it by establishing a reasonable mid-transition test (assets covering costs at market prices without circular state demand) that Side A could not meet.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 dismantling Side A's defense of public risk-sharing by pointing out that in the Gulf, the sovereign fund is uniquely the owner, client, demand-buyer, and lender of last resort.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON won by establishing a falsifiable, intuitive standard for real diversification—fiscal independence, falling breakeven oil prices, and non-oil earnings that survive without state subsidy—and repeatedly showing PRO's model fails it. PRO's admissions that no non-oil line covers imports at $40 oil and that fiscal breakevens remain high despite rising non-oil share crippled the 'likely to succeed' claim, leaving PRO reliant on a transition narrative that CON effectively framed as oil-funded storage rather than conversion. CON's use of the 2014/2020 demand shocks demonstrated that non-oil activity still collapses when oil revenue falls, providing concrete evidence that the diversification is not yet decoupled. PRO's 'arrival versus effort' reframe was eloquent but could not overcome the burden that the motion requires likelihood, not merely activity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer that if oil had truly been converted into fee-earning capacity, the fiscal breakeven would fall as non-oil share rose, yet it has not—crystallizing the storage-versus-conversion distinction and leveraging PRO's own concessions.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that oil-funded origins do not make diversification mere public relations: durable infrastructure, taxation, tradable services, and revenue streams less tied to crude can be substantive even while exploiting energy, capital, and location. Side A sharply exposed continued fiscal dependence and weak citizen employment, but its requirement that industries survive removal of cheap energy, sovereign capital, and imported labor became an excessively pure test that discounted ordinary comparative advantages. B did not fully prove independence across every Gulf state, yet the proposition required substantive efforts likely to succeed, not complete post-oil autonomy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s most decisive response was that Side A’s test required Gulf economies to abandon their advantages rather than diversify them. Reframing the comparison around reduced exposure to oil-price swings directly weakened A’s repeated claim that any activity initially financed by oil remained only “rent in a new wrapper.”
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the standard for diversification. A established that real diversification means utilizing existing advantages (cheap energy, capital) to create revenues that do not crash with oil, rather than abandoning those advantages entirely. A's analogy ("America didn't abandon land to industrialize") decisively exposed B's burden as an unrealistic purity test.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Closing rebuttal exposing CON's demand that Gulf states strip away cheap energy and capital as tantamount to asking them to abandon their core comparative advantages, perfectly crystallized with the "America didn't abandon land" analogy.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by defining the standard for diversification in a way that is falsifiable and historically grounded, then meeting it with concrete fiscal shifts, employment trends, and the 2020 stress test. Side A (CON) insisted on a purity test—industries must survive without cheap energy, sovereign capital, or imported labor—which PRO showed would condemn virtually all state-led development as public relations. CON’s “sovereign put” and “adjacency trap” arguments were sharp, but they never fully neutralized PRO’s evidence that non-oil fiscal intake and GDP proved more resilient than oil during a price collapse, or that VAT and private-national payrolls are directional changes that markets and balance sheets already reflect. PRO’s answers to the pressure questions turned the unfalsifiability charge back on CON, and their closing crystallized the clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where B answers Side A’s pressure questions by citing the 2020 oil-price collapse as an observable natural experiment—showing VAT hikes stuck, non-oil GDP recovered faster, plants kept producing, and logistics earnings continued—while reframing CON’s definition of independence as an impossible purity test that no energy-rich state could meet.
