### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): A won because its rebuttals cleanly exposed the surcharge's design flaws (redundancy with Platform Directive, offshoring leakage, and leverage inversion on SMEs) while offering targeted alternatives that directly addressed abuse without taxing legitimate commerce. B failed to neutralize these live routes and conceded key points on non-local work and cost-shifting mechanics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 answers to pressure questions plus weighing on offshoring concession and SME squeeze inversion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing a structural incoherence in the surcharge: it keys on the *structure* of outsourcing rather than the *conduct* of exploitation. Side B cleanly separated misclassified platform workers (already addressed by the Platform Work Directive) from legitimately employed subcontractor staff (whose actual employer already pays contributions), showing the surcharge is either redundant or a blunt tax on lawful commerce. Side A could not resolve this overinclusivity. Side B further demonstrated that the surcharge would accelerate offshoring of tradeable services—a concession Side A effectively made by retreating to "physically rooted" labor—and that raising contracting costs would strengthen large firms' leverage to squeeze SMEs, undermining Side A's stated goal. Side B's alternative toolkit (reclassification, joint liability, chain-length caps, inspection) provided a more precise path, and its "scalpel vs. surcharge" framing crystallized the debate decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 were decisive in carving apart the redundancy problem: if workers are misclassified, the Platform Work Directive already addresses them; if they are legitimate employees of a subcontractor SME, contributions are already paid and the lead firm is merely purchasing a service. This forced Side A into the weak position of defending a tax on legal outsourcing, which Side B then compounded with the offshoring and SME-squeeze turns that Side A never fully neutralized.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A won by decisively exposing the proposal's core design flaw: it taxes the structure of outsourcing rather than the conduct of abuse. A successfully argued this makes the policy redundant where misclassification exists, punitive toward lawful SME commerce, vulnerable to offshoring leakage (which B effectively conceded for tradeable services), and perversely incentivized to increase lead-firm leverage over subcontractors. A also provided targeted alternatives (joint liability, chain caps) that better address the harm. B's responses were rhetorically smooth but failed to resolve the leverage inversion or the offshoring problem, leaving CON with a clear, unanswered path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's dismantling of the SME squeeze point, showing that raising the cost of contracting actually increases the lead firm's leverage to demand lower base prices from subcontractors, combined with catching B's effective concession that white-collar work would simply offshore to avoid the surcharge.
