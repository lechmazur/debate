Its backbone—the Coogan Law—directly defeats Side B's core premise that families are inherently cohesive economic units that naturally protect children's finances. Side B never credibly distinguished influencer parents from the stage parents the Coogan Law was enacted to stop; it simply reasserted the cohesiveness assumption the law was designed to distrust. Side A's pressure Q2 exposed "interpersonal healing" as hollow leverage-free advice, and Side B's A2 response (pointing to existing fraud laws) failed to address the specific gap between general fraud remedies and the targeted harm of commercial digital exploitation. Side B never rebutted those analogies on the merits, only repeating that the removal right "cannot erase archived content," which A had already directly conceded and contextualised. Side A also handled the line-drawing pressure question competently by pointing to existing regulatory distinctions in tax and labor law. Side A's Rebuttal 1 clipping is a real execution cost that modestly constrains its rebuttal score, but the visible content was still sharp and the overall trajectory of the debate favored A. Side B's strongest angle—liquidity harm from mandatory trust-fund carve-outs—had genuine force but was underdeveloped and never seriously weighed against the exploitation harm it was supposed to outrank.
