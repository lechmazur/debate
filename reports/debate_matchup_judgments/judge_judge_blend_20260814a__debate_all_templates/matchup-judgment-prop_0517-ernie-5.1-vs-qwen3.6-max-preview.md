### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) won on the strength of two sustained, accurate attacks that PRO never neutralized. First, the ISO-21001 takedown in Rebuttal 1 was both factually correct—that standard governs institutional management processes, not graduate competence—and PRO never recovered a credible alternative accreditation argument. Second, and decisively, the ICC/UNCITRAL arbitration accessibility critique: Side A correctly identified that international commercial arbitration is expensive and structurally designed for corporate parties, not individual workers facing wage theft. PRO's closing simply restated that the mechanism exists without addressing affordability, which Side A had made the live burden. The structural-incentive argument—that making credentials explicitly easier than residency creates systematic incentives for employers to route hires through the faster track to shed local wage floors, union agreements, and malpractice liability—was also live at the end and PRO's "registration ledger" answer was plausible but too speculative to close the gap. PRO ran a genuinely strong case: the equity argument about who can afford relocation costs, the visa-limbo point (workers in immigration queues also lack rights), and political feasibility all had real force and were never fully extinguished. Side B's opening and framing were crisp. But PRO's enforcement architecture—the pivot point of the whole debate—rested on ICC arbitration, and the credible, accurate attack on its individual accessibility was the decisive blow that shifted the balance to CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 attack on ISO-21001 was the sharpest single exchange. PRO had cited it as evidence that emerging global standards make credential verification tantamount to validation. Side A correctly identified that ISO-21001 is a management-system standard for educational institutions, not a competency guarantee—it certifies that an organization has administrative processes, not that its graduates can intubate patients or seal bridges. PRO never regained credible ground on the accreditation question after this, and it set up Side A's sustained accountability theme for the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): B won by making the decisive clash about enforceable accountability rather than mere credential speed. A had a plausible equity and efficiency story, but over-relied on speculative digital contracts, ledgers, and international arbitration as substitutes for local jurisdiction. B’s verification-versus-validation distinction, regulated-profession examples, and worker-protection framing were more grounded and better connected to the motion’s incentive problem: making credentials easier than residency may push employers toward weaker, less accountable pathways.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s arbitration and tax-ledger answers was the key exchange: B argued that ICC or UNCITRAL-style enforcement is impractical for ordinary workers and that a digital ledger cannot replace local courts, inspectors, unions, or malpractice systems.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side B won decisively by systematically dismantling Side A's proposed mechanisms with strong, reality-based pressure. B effectively exposed the flaws in A's reliance on ISO standards (identifying it as a management standard, not a competency guarantee) and dealt a fatal blow by pointing out that A's proposed cross-border accountability mechanism (ICC arbitration) is prohibitively expensive for individual workers, leaving them with no practical recourse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing dismantled Side A's reliance on ICC arbitration by pointing out the prohibitive filing fees for individual workers.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): CON built and maintained a cleaner comparative path throughout. The "two-tier system" argument—credential-holders get market access, residency-holders get legal protection—was introduced early, survived sustained pressure, and was crystallized memorably in closing: "employable but unprotected." PRO's clever category-error reframe (the motion is about ease of recognition, not replacement of legal functions) was a genuine high point, but CON correctly identified the asymmetry as precisely the problem: lowering the credential gate while keeping the residency gate creates a structural vulnerability for workers. PRO's best counter—that labor rights attach to employment law and payment rails rather than physical presence—is technically non-trivial but CON correctly labeled it "practically hollow," since cross-border workers typically lack legal standing to invoke those rights without residency status. PRO's "most professions aren't nursing" argument had real force and CON leaned too hard on regulated exceptions, but it never dislodged the two-tier vulnerability. CON's pressure questions were sharper, landing a clean concession from PRO in A1 (the local clinical exam remains the actual gate) and exposing the hollowness of A2. PRO's rhetorical moves were stylistically confident—the "wealth filter vs. merit filter" framing is memorable—but they substituted assertion for resolution on the live clash. CON's closing was tighter, more grounded, and tied the stakes to a concrete image (the Nairobi analyst who is legally employable but legally vulnerable) that PRO never displaced.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A1 response after the pressure questions was the most decisive exchange. PRO had argued that "easier" recognition simply removes the residency prerequisite to access local exams. CON turned this into a concession: PRO's own answer confirmed that for every regulated profession, the residency-linked exam remains the actual gateway, meaning the motion's promised speed gain evaporates precisely where it matters most. That forced PRO to retreat to "most digital professions have no local exam"—a partial concession that left regulated professions undefended and tacitly acknowledged CON's two-tier framing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): A wins a clear but competitive debate. B had a strong rights-based frame, arguing that residency bundles legal protection, tax, and accountability. But A more convincingly narrowed the motion to recognition thresholds rather than abolition of labor law, and repeatedly exposed B's overgeneralization from clinical and regulated professions to all online credentials. A also pressed the decisive low-risk remote-work question, which B largely failed to answer in the required response round, instead replying to A's answers to B's own questions. B's claim that workers cannot meaningfully contract or be protected without residency was rhetorically strong but overbroad and insufficiently grounded, while A's speed, access, merit, and anti-wealth-filter case remained live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was the category-error rebuttal and follow-up pressure: credential recognition can be easier without erasing local exams or labor protections, and B never squarely explained why software engineers or other low-risk remote workers should face residency-style barriers.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing residency requirements as an exclusionary "wealth filter" and showing that Side B's defense of residency as a "protection" practically results in prohibiting skilled workers from accessing the market. Side B failed to adequately weigh the cost of this exclusion against the alleged lack of protections for remote workers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.3 and A2.4 decisively reframed B's core "protection" argument as "prohibition," pointing out that denying a Nairobi analyst a job to save them from a lack of Munich labor rights simply leaves them excluded.
