### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully framing the debate around implementation versus inherent harms. B convincingly argued that the negative outcomes Side A described are choices made by schools rather than inevitable properties of the devices themselves.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 response to A's question about non-verbal cues was decisive. B pointed out that students co-editing a document in a physical classroom can still talk, gesture, and negotiate face-to-face, directly neutralizing A's claim that 1:1 devices inherently eliminate unscripted human interaction.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by converting the clash into a clean burden PRO never met: PRO needed the harms (fragmented attention, lost non-verbal social practice, screen defaults) to be inherent and near-inevitable properties of one-to-one hardware itself rather than controllable via scheduling and pedagogy. CON repeatedly showed those mechanisms are design variables, used PRO's own accommodation concession to prove the effects are implementation-dependent, and made the equity alternative (shared labs/loaners) look two-tier and inferior. PRO's later speeches recycled "path of least resistance" and "gravitational pull" assertions without new comparative force, so the coexistence/design path still stood as the cleaner reason to reject forced prioritization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.5): defaults are choices of curriculum and habit, not hardware law, plus both/and digital + face-to-face social practice, collapsing PRO's categorical claim after the accommodation concession.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully dismantling Side A's core premise that one-to-one devices inevitably become the "default" medium of learning. Side B effectively argues that device usage is a function of school policy, scheduling, and curriculum design rather than an inescapable property of the hardware. Furthermore, Side B wins the equity clash decisively by highlighting the real-world logistical failures of "shared labs" (e.g., waitlists, broken chargers, leftover hardware), a practical burden Side A dismisses as "overstated" without adequate defense. Side B also capitalizes sharply on Side A's concession regarding accommodations to prove that device impacts are implementation-dependent, undermining Side A's claim of categorical developmental erosion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it cleanly separates hardware properties from school scheduling choices to answer the "default" pressure question, and uses Side A's concession on student accommodations to collapse the "categorical erosion" claim.
