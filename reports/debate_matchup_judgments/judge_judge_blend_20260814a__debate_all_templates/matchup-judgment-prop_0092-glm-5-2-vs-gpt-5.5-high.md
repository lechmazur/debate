- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): PRO won on the decisive clash because its best move—catching that CON's own preferred alternative ("charge for two spaces") is itself a size-based fee—was never squarely answered. CON kept insisting that categorical registration surcharges are blunt ownership taxes rather than use-based charges, but PRO countered that commercial truck weight fees operate on exactly the same logic and are universally accepted. PRO also held the stronger line on the safety externality: time-based curb pricing, CON's preferred substitute, treats a compact and an oversized SUV identically despite different sightline and crash-severity impacts, and CON never resolved this gap. CON's strongest live argument—that categorical fees charge owners regardless of actual street presence, and that the exemptions PRO endorsed prove the fee is selective rather than neutral—was real and not fully neutralized, but PRO's analogy to disability placards and commercial plates as working precedents for structured exemptions was adequate. CON's threshold question landed a hit (PRO's "roughly 6,000 pounds" answer was admittedly approximate), but PRO deflected credibly by pointing out that every fee tier draws lines where marginal costs shift. CON was also hurt by three clipped speeches (opening, second rebuttal, closing) that cut off finishing points, while PRO's argumentation was more contained and efficient throughout. The core comparative remained in PRO's favor: CON's alternatives either silently accept size-based logic or leave the safety externality entirely unpriced.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 catch that CON's "charge for two spaces when two spaces are used" is functionally a size fee—since the only thing that determines whether a vehicle occupies two spaces is its physical dimensions—was the sharpest single exchange. CON's response in Rebuttal 2 (distinguishing a use-triggered curb charge from a categorical registration surcharge) was coherent but did not escape the underlying concession that physical size is the operative variable driving both tools.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2 answers on thresholds/exemptions) cleanly neutralized CON's fairness and precision attacks while reframing CON's own tools as partial acceptance of size-based charges.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): CON wins by successfully distinguishing between use-based pricing and categorical ownership taxation. PRO built a strong case that vehicle mass and footprint are constant physical proxies for externalities and cleverly turned CON's "charge for two spaces" alternative into a concession of size-based pricing. However, CON consistently demonstrated that registration or blanket curb surcharges apply regardless of actual street use, creating a fundamental policy mismatch. CON's critique that exemptions for "legitimate hauling" collapse PRO's cost-internalization principle was particularly damaging; PRO's shift to framing exemptions as standard "policy trade-offs" effectively conceded the pure economic justification. CON also offered cleaner, more targeted alternatives (demand-based curb pricing, direct safety design, enforcement) that address the exact harms PRO named without overcharging low-use owners. Both sides incurred execution costs from clipped turns, but CON's tighter focus on the mechanism mismatch, sharper handling of the exemption logic, and cleaner weighing of proxy versus direct measurement provided the more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over "charge for two spaces" versus registration surcharges. PRO argued this alternative conceded the size-fee principle, but CON sharply distinguished a use fee (applied at the curb only when extra space is actually consumed) from a blanket ownership tax (paid regardless of use or location). This distinction preserved CON's core mismatch critique, neutralized PRO's turn, and kept the debate focused on whether the proposition's fee mechanism actually tracks the harms it claims to price.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): PRO maintained a coherent, economically grounded path to victory throughout: size imposes predictable, measurable externalities (space displacement, sightline obstruction, crash severity), and equal fees for unequal use constitute a subsidy from everyone else to the largest users. That core claim was never squarely neutralized. CON's strongest moves—the ownership-vs.-behavior distinction and the universality trap—were real and made this genuinely competitive. When PRO answered Q2 by embracing universal dimension-based pricing, CON effectively turned it into a liability (hitting EVs, wheelchair vans, minivans), and PRO's "implementation details" dismissal was the debate's weakest moment. However, CON's central counter—that existing tools (speed cameras, congestion pricing, fuel taxes) already cover the problem—never addressed PRO's specific externality: spatial displacement and sightline obstruction are not covered by speed enforcement, and PRO correctly returned to this gap throughout. The "equal fees for unequal use is the unfair position" framing held up as the cleaner reason to vote PRO. CON's rebuttal work was sharper and more rhetorically memorable (the "taller people on buses" analogy, the "charging dimensions not burdens" closing crystallization), and CON's speeches were unclipped while PRO's Opening, Rebuttal 1, and Rebuttal 2 were all truncated—a real execution cost. Those factors keep the margin narrow, but PRO's core externality argument and the cumulative logic of layered policy tools survived the exchange intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The universality trap exchange was the most decisive clash. CON's Q2 forced PRO to choose between admitting cultural targeting of disfavored body styles or endorsing a universal measured-size fee. PRO answered boldly (yes, apply it to all vehicles based on dimensions), but CON's counter in Rebuttal 2—that this sweeps in heavy EVs, wheelchair-accessible vans, and minivans, and that PRO calling these "implementation details" misunderstands that they are the policy—was the sharpest individual hit of the debate. It didn't ultimately defeat PRO because CON still lacked an answer to spatial displacement externalities, but it was the exchange that most visibly damaged PRO's position.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A built the stronger case by consistently showing the fee taxes ownership rather than actual harm or behavior, exposing line-drawing and regressivity problems, while fully completing every turn. Side B's core speeches were clipped, weakening development of their externality argument despite solid pressure responses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal 1 on ownership vs. behavior targeting, directly answered by B's later A1/A2 but never fully neutralized.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing that municipal pricing routinely relies on predictable proxies for spatial and safety impacts, rather than waiting for actual harm. A effectively neutralizes B's "taxes ownership, not behavior" argument by explaining how registration and curb fees function as access pricing, and delivers a decisive rebuttal on safety with "sensors do not repeal physics." While B raises strong practical concerns about overbreadth (EVs, accessibility vehicles), A successfully frames these as manageable implementation details within a sound principle, whereas B's demand for perfect behavioral targeting ignores the independent spatial burdens large vehicles impose simply by occupying public streets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to B's safety argument ("sensors do not repeal physics or geometry") cleanly separates crash avoidance from crash severity, dismantling B's claim that technology negates size-based risk. A also effectively turns B's "plumber with an F-150" example into a proof point for pricing business input costs rather than subsidizing them.
