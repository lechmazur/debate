### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON wins by demonstrating that the financial structures keeping coal alive (must-run contracts, cross-subsidies) actively block clean energy deployment and raise manufacturing costs. When PRO attempts to escape this by proposing contract reforms to turn coal into a paid reserve, CON decisively argues that removing a hard deadline creates the very execution delays PRO fears.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly endogenized PRO's execution risk argument, pointing out that infrastructure projects arrive late precisely because a condition-based retirement policy removes the cost of deferral.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): B won a close, high-quality debate by converting A's successive concessions into the decisive comparison. A built a coherent reliability-first sequencing case and answered pressure with a technical trigger plus contract reform, but that refinement left the motion resting mainly on a residual "keep coal if replacement is late" priority. B made clearer why that priority is costly: legacy PPAs, must-run status, curtailment, and blocked open access mean delay is not free insurance—it actively slows the clean firm power manufacturing needs, while export carbon screens and industrial tariffs also cut against A. A's risk-asymmetry point remained live, but B better explained why dates create replacement and conditions enable deferral. Net: B made the motion easier to reject.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 concession collapse: once A strips must-run/legacy capacity charges and pays only competitive reserve, ageing coal exits on roughly B's timetable—so the motion's remaining "even if delayed" priority is mainly a harmful signal that encourages deferral.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side A won by masterfully dissecting the political economy of India's power sector, distinguishing between the physical existence of coal plants and the contractual obligations (must-run status, long-term PPAs) that artificially keep them running. By forcing Side B to concede that these market distortions could be removed while retaining physical plants as backup, Side A demonstrated that without the protective contracts, the aging plants would naturally retire. This reduced the proposition to a harmful political signal that incentivizes utilities to defer clean energy and grid investments. Side A's pressure questions were exceptionally sharp, and their exploitation of Side B's concessions in the later speeches decisively sealed the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they capitalized on Side B's concession regarding contract reform to argue that Side B had effectively adopted Side A's timeline, exposing the motion as a dangerous priority signal that encourages grid and storage deferral.
