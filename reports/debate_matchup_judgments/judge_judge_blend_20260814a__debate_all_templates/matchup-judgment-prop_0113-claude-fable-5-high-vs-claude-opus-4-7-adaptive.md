### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by keeping the motion focused on who should bear waste costs and, crucially, who can change packaging design. Side B's best route was regressivity and pass-through, but A turned that into a pricing-and-incentive argument: costs are already paid by someone, and putting them into the producer supply chain creates eco-modulated pressure that municipal taxes cannot. B's capture and essential-goods objections were serious, but A plausibly answered that public collection can remain intact, producers can reimburse municipalities, and implementation flaws do not defeat the underlying funding principle. B was rhetorically sharp, but relied too heavily on calling the policy a grocery tax without fully answering the design-incentive advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was answering the "flat surcharge plus bureaucracy" attack by distinguishing eco-modulated producer fees from flat taxes and explaining that municipal trucks can remain public while the cost signal moves to the firms choosing the packaging design.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a live incentive comparison that Side A never neutralized, directly answering pressure questions with A1/A2 labels and eco-modulation framing, and crystallizing that the status quo gives designers zero skin in the game while B's mechanism varies fees by design.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 exchange on eco-modulation vs flat surcharge and pouch "losing" directly damaged A's core regressivity and inelastic-demand route.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): CON wins by successfully grounding the debate in economic and implementation reality. PRO built a clean theoretical case around design incentives and internalizing costs, but CON consistently exposed the pass-through tension: on inelastic staples, the fee functions as a regressive consumer surcharge, and EPR schemes face structural capture risks that PRO dismissed too easily as fixable design flaws. CON's rebuttals sharpened this clash, and their closing effectively neutralized PRO's "both horns of the dilemma help us" spin by showing why the consumer burden on essentials undermines PRO's fairness claim. PRO was polished but relied on an idealized mechanism that CON successfully weighed against real-world distributional impacts and industry self-regulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 dismantling PRO's pass-through defense. By forcing PRO into a dilemma—either the fee bites margins (making it a corporate tax) or passes to consumers (making it a regressive grocery tax)—CON exposed the economic sleight of hand in PRO's case, then used the inelasticity of staples to show why the consumer share dominates, decisively winning the regressivity and feasibility clash.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins narrowly because it kept the debate tied to the motion’s core: collection and recycling costs should be attached to the producers’ packaging decisions rather than left in general municipal budgets. Side B landed strong attacks on pass-through, governance, and diluted pooled fees, but too often assumed a narrow industry-consortium model rather than the broader proposition. A’s responses that pass-through does not erase price signals, that eco-modulated fees can solve pooling, and that B’s direct-design alternatives still fail to address who funds collection left A with the cleaner live route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s rebuttal that pass-through would invalidate nearly every pollution rule if accepted, and that the relevant question is where the invoice and incentive sit, was the most important exchange; it blunted B’s central distributional objection enough for A’s polluter-pays case to survive.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by forcing concessions on alternatives being producer-pays tools, directly answering pressure questions on pass-through and modulation, and maintaining the core comparison that cost attachment to producers is fairer and more effective than taxpayer status quo.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers plus closing reframing of CON's scalpel as aligned with the motion.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO)): CON wins by successfully severing the link between the motion's financing shift and its promised upstream design benefits. By turning PRO's eco-modulation answer into a concession that regulatory rules—not cost transfers—drive packaging redesign, and by sharply distinguishing consumer-funded deposits from producer-paid collection, CON demonstrated the motion is unnecessary for its stated benefits while leaving its costs on regressivity and opaque governance unrebutted. PRO built a coherent fairness and incentive case but relied on conflating consumer price signals with producer burden, a vulnerability CON consistently exploited across rebuttals and crystallized cleanly in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turn on eco-modulation, demonstrating that modulated fees are functionally just recyclability standards expressed as a price, thereby proving the design incentive comes from direct regulation rather than the wholesale financing transfer the motion mandates.
