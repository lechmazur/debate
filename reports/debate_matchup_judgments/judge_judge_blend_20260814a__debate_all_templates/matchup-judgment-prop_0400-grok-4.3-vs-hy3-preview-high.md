- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON held two live routes to victory across the full exchange. First, the bias/auditability argument: CON consistently argued that human bias is unconscious, unmonitorable, and adds unconstrained prejudice that disproportionately harms marginalized applicants, while algorithmic flaws can be audited and corrected before deployment. PRO's counter—that algorithms inherit bias from training data—was valid but never neutralized the auditability asymmetry, which is the more important comparative point. Second, the scale/delay argument: CON correctly noted that even under PRO's "optional right" reframing, a small invocation rate across tens of millions of annual cases creates massive backlogs that strand the vulnerable CON claims to protect. PRO's A2 response ("targeted use," "agencies focus resources on flagged cases") was the weakest moment in the debate—it was asserted without answering the underlying math. PRO's strongest move was the optional-right reframing in Rebuttal 2, which genuinely constrained CON's catastrophic-delay framing. CON responded competently with the 3% rate argument but did not fully close the door on PRO's claim that selectivity limits systemic burden. PRO also raised a valid downstream-costs point (erroneous denials → homelessness → larger public expenditure) that CON largely ignored. These unanswered PRO points keep this competitive rather than dominant, but CON's two live arguments—bias auditability and scale math—were stronger and more consistently grounded across all rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's optional-right reframing in Rebuttal 2 [A4.2]: even a 3% request rate across tens of millions of cases adds hundreds of thousands of reviews to strained agencies, stranding all applicants while staff are diverted. This directly engaged PRO's attempt to escape the scale objection and showed the math doesn't disappear just because the right is invocable rather than universal. PRO never answered the quantitative logic in the closing, leaving CON's scale argument live at the end of the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO won the debate's decisive clash. CON's core strategy—scale and backlog—depended on treating "tens of millions" of annual government decisions as subject to the right, but PRO's Q1 in the pressure round cleanly exposed that the proposition is scoped to high-stakes, life-altering decisions only, not routine administrative checks. CON's A4.1 attempted to salvage the scale concern by pointing to hundreds of thousands of asylum and benefits claims at the high-stakes threshold, but this substantially undercuts the original framing and never recovers the rhetorical or logical force it had before the scope clarification landed. PRO's auditability distinction—human inconsistency is visible and correctable; algorithmic bias is hidden in proprietary code—was the stronger second-tier argument and was never fully neutralized. CON's strongest counter, that automated denials already carry mandatory written explanations and appeal rights under governing law, was asserted with some confidence but unevenly grounded; it is plausible for some systems but PRO correctly noted that risk-score-driven or opaque AI tools often provide no usable rationale, particularly for asylum and pretrial decisions. CON's "existing appeals suffice" argument also carried a real burden problem: PRO consistently pressed that marginalized applicants are least able to navigate opaque post-denial processes, and CON never answered why the burden of correcting an erroneously automated denial should fall on the person who was wronged rather than be built into the initial decision. PRO's two clipped speeches are real execution costs but the substantive content survived intact in both cases. CON's closing and rebuttals were clean and consistent but largely repeated the same efficiency framing without adapting to the scope concession it had effectively made. PRO's case grew stronger across the exchange; CON's shrank.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 in the pressure questions round was the debate's most decisive move. By explicitly flagging that the proposition covers only decisions that significantly affect life outcomes—not the routine low-impact checks CON used to project overwhelming scale—PRO forced CON into a partial concession that substantially deflated the backlog argument CON had built its entire case around. CON's response in A4.1 shifted to "hundreds of thousands" rather than "tens of millions," a real but unacknowledged retreat that PRO effectively crystallized in closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's two primary mechanisms for victory: that scale concerns were inflated, and that automated decisions are unappealable. Side A effectively showed that high-stakes cases alone generate massive volume, and accurately explained that administrative appeals do not require a named initial decision-maker to function.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.2 directly neutralized Side B's core structural complaint by explaining that administrative appeals target the substantive outcome rather than requiring a named human target for service of process.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by demonstrating that mandating front-end human review for all high-stakes decisions would create severe resource bottlenecks and delays that harm more applicants than they help, without neutralizing the existing appeal mechanisms that already channel contested cases to humans. PRO successfully established the moral weight of unaccountable algorithmic harm and the auditability of human error, but never squarely answered the scale objection—conceding implicitly that even brief human sign-offs for hundreds of thousands of annual high-stakes claims require staffing levels that do not exist and would produce backlogs. CON's direct answers to PRO's pressure questions, particularly the explanation that administrative appeals operate on substantive grounds without requiring a named individual decision-maker, blunted PRO's "unappealable black box" narrative. PRO's clipped rebuttals and reliance on repeated assertions that delay is "negligible" weakened their ability to close the feasibility gap, leaving CON's comparative case—targeted appeals concentrate scarce human judgment where it is actually needed—as the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4) answering PRO's Pressure Questions: A2 undercut PRO's claim that automated denials are unchallengeable by explaining that governing statutes already mandate written explanations and substantive administrative appeals without requiring a named official, while A1 pressed the unaddressed resource constraint that front-end review would divert limited staff from the appeals stage.
