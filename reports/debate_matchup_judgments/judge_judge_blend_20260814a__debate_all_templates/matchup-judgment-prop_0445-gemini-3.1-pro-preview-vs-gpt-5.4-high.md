### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): CON won on the two most decisive clashes. First, the sequencing asymmetry: PRO's answer that "simultaneous statutory enactment" resolves the timing problem was directly and correctly rebutted — a dismissal rule changes on day one, while unemployment support requires verified work histories, processed claims, adequate payments, and usable healthcare. PRO never neutralized this gap. Second, the false binary: CON established that India can build tax-funded universal protection without making individual contracts more disposable, and PRO never answered why the exchange structure is necessary rather than just expanding the welfare side alone. PRO's strongest live point — that 80%+ of Indian workers are already unprotected — was real and maintained pressure throughout, but CON correctly redirected the benchmark from "today's informality vs. the ideal package" to "liberalize now vs. reform without trading away dismissal protections." CON's final comparative frame (certain, immediate harm to existing formal workers vs. conjectural, conditional welfare gains) was the cleanest resolution of the clash and was not answered in PRO's closing. PRO had superior rhetorical clarity and memorable framing ("labor aristocracy," "elite regulatory fortress"), which kept competitive pressure alive, but those phrases did not substitute for answering the sequencing problem or the false binary. CON's two clipped speeches impose a modest execution cost but the key substantive lines still survived. The margin is real but competitive because PRO's 80% exclusion frame and compliance-arithmetic argument were genuine contributions to the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.4 response to PRO's sequencing answer: "Simultaneous statutory enactment is not simultaneous protection. A dismissal rule changes on day one. Unemployment support only protects when work histories are verified, claims are processed, exclusions are low, and payments are enough to matter." This was the sharpest single exchange — PRO's A1 answer had appeared to resolve the sequencing problem with a concrete mechanism (India's DBT infrastructure), and CON's reply correctly distinguished digital payment delivery from genuine unemployment insurance, directly deflating PRO's most important rebuttal without overreaching.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): CON won by showing the proposed exchange was asymmetric and speculative. PRO never proved that easier firing would overcome multifactorial informality and weak enforcement to trigger mass formalization, and its answer to sequencing—"simultaneous statutory enactment"—collapsed against CON's point that statutes are not usable healthcare or processed unemployment claims. CON demonstrated concrete, immediate harm to formal workers against a speculative chain of benefits. CON's case was weakened by two clipped speeches and missing A1:/A2: labels, keeping the margin competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that "simultaneous statutory enactment" does not equal simultaneous protection—dismissal rules change immediately, but usable welfare requires verification, capacity, and time that PRO never proved existed, exposing the trade as one-sided.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON wins by decisively breaking PRO's causal chain and exposing the asymmetric risk of the proposed exchange. While PRO effectively framed the status quo as exclusionary, CON consistently demonstrated that legal flexibility arrives immediately while complex welfare delivery lags, making the "exchange" speculative for workers. CON's rebuttal that firms evade multiple obligations (taxes, safety, recordkeeping) under weak enforcement successfully neutralized PRO's claim that easier firing alone would trigger mass formalization. CON's closing crystallization—"statutes are not protection"—provided the cleaner comparative reason to reject the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's sequencing defense in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, sharply distinguishing between "simultaneous statutory enactment" and actual administrative delivery, effectively collapsed PRO's core mechanism and secured the risk calculus.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins on the most decisive live clash: the state-capacity asymmetry. PRO exposed a genuine contradiction in CON's case—CON simultaneously argues the Indian state is too weak to deliver portable universal protection yet strong enough to enforce severance, safety, and joint liability against millions of evasive small firms, contractors, and informal employers who already evade the law. CON's answer in Rebuttal 2 ("auditing identifiable corporate capital is cheaper than a welfare apparatus") was partially plausible but underdeveloped and introduced "joint liability" fairly late without grounding it in the earlier case architecture. It did not resolve why a state that cannot collect taxes from most firms could effectively audit them all. PRO also maintained a sharper baseline comparison throughout: not an idealized permanent job vs. flexibility, but the actual informal worker today—dropped off the books with nothing—vs. a formally contracted worker with recorded employment, portable benefits, and legal claims. That comparison held up under pressure. CON's best pressure (the funding question and sequencing problem) was real and created genuine cost for PRO, but PRO answered it plausibly by reframing the obligation structure as a shared risk pool financed in part by employer contributions rather than pure state spending, and by pointing out that formalizing more work itself broadens the fiscal base. The clipping of PRO's Rebuttal 2 is a minor execution cost, but the visible content remained strong. Side B's rhetorical framing ("bait-and-switch," "corporate subsidy masquerading as reform") was punchy but became repetitive and did not adapt sufficiently to the evolving clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question 1 and its subsequent development: if the state is too weak to deliver portable social protection, it is certainly not strong enough to enforce severance, benefits, and safety obligations against millions of evasive employers who already circumvent the law. This asymmetry was the sharpest point in the debate, genuinely damaged CON's case, and CON never fully neutralized it—their "targeted regulatory audit" answer assumed enforcement capacity they had denied existed.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO won by offering a more coherent and empirically grounded causal story about India's actual labor market. PRO successfully framed the status quo as a rigid dualism that protects a formal minority while pushing the vast informal majority into unprotected churn, and argued persuasively that portable social protection is better suited to Indian realities than employer-tied job security. CON landed real pressure on sequencing and state capacity, but undermined its own route to victory by repeatedly straw-manning PRO's model as "zero-liability" instant dismissal even after PRO explicitly clarified that flexibility still includes notice, severance, anti-abuse rules, and employer contributions. CON's "trillion-dollar fantasy" also relied on brittle fake precision. While CON's joint-liability alternative was concrete, it did not adequately explain why rigid dismissal rules specifically would not continue to drive informality. PRO's clipped Rebuttal 2 was an execution cost, but the core comparative frame—imperfect portable security for the many versus theoretical job security for the few—survived the exchange as the cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 dismantling of CON's "zero liability" caricature, clarifying that flexible formal contracts under the motion still include notice, predictable severance, anti-abuse rules, and mandatory contributions, and distinguishing recorded benefit-linked employment from off-the-books daily wage labor. This rebuttal was decisive because CON's "race to the bottom" and "state-sanctioned precarity" narratives depended entirely on the zero-liability framing that PRO exposed as a straw man.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by successfully turning CON's state-capacity argument against them. While CON effectively pressed the funding and sequencing risks of the proposed exchange, PRO cleanly demonstrated that CON's alternative (strict enforcement and joint liability on supply chains) relies on the exact same regulatory capacity CON claims is too weak to deliver portable benefits. PRO's clarification that flexibility does not mean zero liability, combined with the comparative framing of security attached to people rather than rare contracts, provided a more coherent and scalable path for India's dualistic labor market. CON's closing was rhetorically sharp but never resolved the enforcement contradiction or explained how to practically cover the informal majority without the formalization incentives PRO outlined. The minor clipping cost on PRO's second rebuttal did not materially damage an otherwise complete answer sequence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 directly answered CON's pressure on funding and the nature of flexible contracts, clarifying that shared contributions and an expanded formal base address the fiscal gap, while notice periods and anti-abuse rules prevent a race to the bottom. PRO then turned the clash by highlighting CON's asymmetry: demanding heavy state enforcement against millions of evasive firms while simultaneously claiming the state is too weak to administer a portable benefit floor. This comparison decisively narrowed the debate to which model actually scales security.
