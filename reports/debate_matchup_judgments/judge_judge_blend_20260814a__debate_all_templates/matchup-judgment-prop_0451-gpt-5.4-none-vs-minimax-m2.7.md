### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins a clear but competitive debate. Three factors tip the balance. First, the asymmetry-of-consequences argument — climate damage is partially irreversible; industrial job losses are recoverable — was never genuinely answered by PRO. PRO's attempted reframe ("the more essential EVs are, the less rational to accept dependence") does not actually explain why the threat lens and its tools are superior to CON's alternative; it just restates that hardware matters, which CON already conceded. Second, PRO's most damaging concession came in Rebuttal 2 (A1): "Yes, that can raise some near-term prices." CON exploited this repeatedly and cleanly — PRO's own acknowledged mechanism for building resilience is to make the transition slower and costlier, which is precisely the trade-off CON said was unacceptable given the harder climate problem. Third, CON's affirmative mechanism (integration plus JV conditions, competition law, data rules, and subsidy requirements) was specific, grounded in real examples (BYD Szeged, CATL Erfurt), and plausibly achieves the resilience PRO demands without the price penalty. PRO never convincingly refuted it; the "integration on whose terms / IP stays external" rejoinder is real, but CON correctly answered that control is a spectrum and that a European-footprint plant is meaningfully different from pure finished-goods imports. PRO did land genuine blows — "brittle versus resilient decarbonization" is a memorable and substantively serious frame, and the Q2 pressure on assembly-without-autonomy was sharp — but PRO never closed the gap on the cost concession or offered a mechanism that avoids it. CON's "the lens shapes the policy" point also held up: calling it "threat first" defaults to exclusion, which slams the door before the integration CON cited can occur. On grounding, CON's specific plant examples gave its case more empirical purchase than PRO's more theoretical resilience narrative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 counter to the flood/coach analogy: "the flood is not coming — BYD in Szeged, CATL in Erfurt — this is integration, not annihilation." By naming specific real-world investments and reframing the threat as messy integration rather than annihilation, CON materially damaged PRO's core factual premise while simultaneously setting up the mechanism argument that carried through to the closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by showing that the industrial-threat lens is self-defeating: it raises prices and slows adoption, which directly undermines the more urgent, partially irreversible goal of decarbonization. CON landed the factual point that European industry is not being annihilated—citing BYD and CATL plants in Europe, scaling battery gigafactories, and forced restructuring—as evidence that integration is happening and competition is spurring adaptation. PRO never overcame the asymmetry of consequences, nor did PRO explain why targeted industrial tools (JV rules, competition law, subsidy conditions) could only work under a threat-first framing rather than a decarbonization-first one. PRO’s admission that tariffs raise near-term costs conceded CON’s central comparative claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s response to PRO’s “flood” analogy with concrete integration examples (BYD in Szeged, CATL in Erfurt) and the subsequent argument that EU-based plants operate under European courts, tax, and employment law, directly undermining PRO’s factual premise of imminent industrial annihilation and showing control is a matter of degrees rather than binary ownership.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully turning Side A's core weighing mechanism. While A effectively pressed the short-term price/adoption tradeoff and cited concrete integration examples (BYD, CATL), B neutralized the "climate stakes are higher" argument by framing industrial capacity as a prerequisite for durable decarbonization, not a distraction from it. B's distinction between cosmetic assembly and strategic control/IP, combined with the "brittle vs resilient decarbonization" frame, provided a cleaner long-term pathway that A never fully resolved. B also handled the pressure questions more directly, accepting the near-term cost tradeoff and weighing it convincingly against structural vulnerability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to the asymmetry of stakes claim: arguing that precisely because decarbonization is a massive, decades-long systems transformation, relying on a single external supplier for its hardware creates a brittle procurement strategy that risks the entire transition. This turn effectively converted A's strongest offensive into a reason to vote PRO.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but CON (Side A) held the cleaner path to victory on the motion's specific word. By consistently anchoring on "primarily," CON forced PRO to justify why industrial defensiveness should come before climate delivery — and PRO never squarely answered that burden. CON's best move was distinguishing targeted safeguards from making threat the primary frame: the scalpel-versus-wall formulation, and especially the seatbelt analogy in the closing, held that the existence of safety rules does not define the governed activity as primarily dangerous. CON also landed the capital-good-versus-metered-choke-point distinction on dependency, which PRO rebutted with the "industrial tissue / 2040 vulnerability" argument but never fully reversed. PRO's sharpest moment was the convergence argument — "your entire policy toolkit is borrowed from the defensive playbook" — which was the single most damaging pressure in the debate. CON's response (every serious trade regime polices unfair practices without treating all imports as a threat) was adequate but not decisive; the seatbelt analogy carried more rhetorical weight than argumentative resolution. PRO's "industrial capacity is the precondition for decarbonization" closing line was strong, but CON's response — "helpful, not strictly prior" — was plausible enough to survive. Ultimately, CON had a complete, well-crystallized closing while PRO's was clipped, and CON's climate-urgency argument (making cheap clean-tech suspect raises prices, slows adoption, prolongs fossil dependence) was never met with a comparative weighing by PRO. The margin is narrow because PRO's convergence argument, subsidy-displacement framing, and future-dependency case were all live and partially unanswered, but CON maintained the cleaner warrant for what "primarily" should mean in policy terms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's convergence argument in Rebuttal 2 — "CON's entire framework is opportunity first, but if it becomes threatening, handle it; that is not opportunity first, it is threat-management dressed in optimistic language" — was the most decisive single exchange. CON's scalpel-versus-wall and seatbelt analogies answered it acceptably but did not fully neutralize it, leaving it as a live burden that constrained CON's margin of victory.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by more effectively narrowing the debate to the meaning of "primarily." B argued that structural industrial displacement was already underway and that CON's own proposed safeguards—tariffs, anti-subsidy tools, procurement rules—were defensive threat-management instruments, making "opportunity first" an optimistic label rather than a coherent frame. B maintained that only a threat-first lens generates the urgency and proportionality needed to preserve the industrial base that underpins long-term decarbonization. While CON made a strong case for rapid decarbonization and the distinction between capital goods and fuel dependency, they never fully resolved B's challenge that their framework functionally collapses into threat-management, nor did they offer a clear limiting principle for when their opportunity frame would shift. B's closing crystallized this clash effectively, leaving the stronger live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal and closing pressed the framework collapse argument: that CON's "opportunity first" stance necessarily relies on defensive tools (anti-subsidy, procurement, resilience rules), proving the industrial threat is the operative reality. Combined with the point that displaced production lines do not reopen in the next policy cycle, this created decisive pressure that CON's scalpel-versus-wall and seatbelt metaphors could not fully answer.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by cleanly separating primary framing from targeted safeguards, successfully arguing that a threat-first default predictably overcorrects into protectionism, higher prices, and prolonged fossil dependence. CON's capital-good vs. continuous-flow dependency distinction and the closing analogies (seatbelts, food safety) decisively neutralized PRO's claim that using defensive tools proves a threat-primary frame. While PRO effectively pressed on industrial displacement and the necessity of safeguards, CON consistently won the weighing on policy defaults and the irreversible climate costs of slowing adoption, making the path to victory clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing dismantled PRO's central trap that relying on anti-subsidy tools and screening concedes the threat frame. By distinguishing a starting presumption from proportional safeguards (scalpel vs. wall) and deploying the seatbelt/food safety analogies, CON showed that managing risk does not make risk the primary lens, preserving the decarbonization urgency while answering PRO's policy-reality pressure.
