### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): PRO wins a razor-thin but exceptional debate by successfully framing the core clash around the definition of taxable capacity. While CON brilliantly exposes the administrative nightmares of annual valuation, PRO's argument that "CON confuses the lack of a cash realization with the lack of capacity" decisively corners CON into admitting that a purely transaction-based system leaves compounding dynastic wealth largely untouched until death.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing argument perfectly distilling the debate: "CON confuses the lack of a cash realization with the lack of capacity. Capacity is the portfolio itself."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that an annual wealth tax turns the most contestable asset estimates into a permanent battlefield of avoidance, litigation, and formula gaming, while cleaner transaction-based reforms can target the same fortunes at enforceable moments. PRO successfully identified a gap in the status quo, but never overcame the administrative dilemma: their own safeguards—safe harbors, liquidity options, and clawbacks—recreate the very deferral and timing games the wealth tax was meant to eliminate. CON's distinction between episodic estate valuation and recurring annual appraisal sharpened the clash decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that PRO's valuation safeguards—formulaic safe harbors and liquidity-sensitive payment options—recreate the very timing games, deferral, and avoidance the wealth tax was supposed to end.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by establishing a clearer and more compelling comparative framework. PRO successfully framed the core clash as "administrative friction versus permanent exemption," arguing that transaction-based repairs (Side A's alternative) inevitably allow ultra-wealthy households to opt out of the tax base for decades through perpetual deferral. Side A made strong points about the valuation difficulties of an annual wealth tax, but Side B effectively neutralized this by pointing out that valuation disputes already occur for estates and gifts, and concentrating them on a few thousand ultra-wealthy households is manageable. Side B's pressure question regarding fortunes that are never sold or borrowed against exposed a critical gap in Side A's transaction-based alternative, which Side A could only answer by relying on estate taxes—a point Side B deftly countered in the closing by noting that death is not on a fiscal schedule. Additionally, Side A suffered execution costs due to multiple clipped turns, whereas Side B maintained disciplined, impactful rhetoric throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to Side A's valuation concerns, arguing that concentrating existing estate and gift valuation machinery on a narrow, high-threshold base makes the administrative friction acceptable compared to the permanent exemption of the status quo.
