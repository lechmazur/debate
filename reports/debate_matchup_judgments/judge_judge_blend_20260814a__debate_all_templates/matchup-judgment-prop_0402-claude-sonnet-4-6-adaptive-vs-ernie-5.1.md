### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by making the central distinction clearer: rejecting voucher participation can be about the attached inspections, rent caps, paperwork, and three-party government contract, not simply animus toward poor tenants. Side A’s claim that small landlords could simply exit the rental market was a damaging concession that the burden is real. Side B had minor execution costs from clipped turns, but still controlled the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s rebuttal to the credit card analogy was decisive: Visa does not impose inspections, rent caps, or government co-principal obligations, so vouchers are not merely another payment method.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's analogies and logical links. Side A successfully framed the issue as compelled government contracting, highlighting concrete programmatic burdens like inspections and rent caps. Side B's credit card analogy was crushed when Side A pointed out credit cards do not enforce rent controls or property inspections. Furthermore, Side B's argument that a landlord's willingness to accept the same tenant paying cash proved discrimination backfired; Side A correctly argued this proves the rejection is based on the voucher's regulatory package, not the human being.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 destruction of the credit card analogy, paired with their Rebuttal 2 answer explaining that landlords cannot simply raise the rent to cover voucher compliance costs because the programs themselves impose rent caps.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON wins by successfully distinguishing between rejecting a tenant and rejecting a burdensome government contract structure. CON dismantled PRO's credit card analogy by highlighting real program mechanics (inspections, rent caps, three-party leases) and directly answered PRO's pressure questions by noting rent caps prevent cost recovery. PRO consistently dismissed these structural burdens as stigma or pretext without substantively answering why small landlords should be forced to absorb them, and PRO's suggestion that burdened landlords could simply exit the market conceded the reality of the costs. CON's framing of compelled contracting versus anti-discrimination provided a cleaner, more grounded path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 dismantling of the credit card analogy, correctly noting that unlike payment processors, voucher programs impose inspections, price caps, and co-principal legal status, which directly neutralized PRO's central comparative frame and shifted the debate to program mechanics.
