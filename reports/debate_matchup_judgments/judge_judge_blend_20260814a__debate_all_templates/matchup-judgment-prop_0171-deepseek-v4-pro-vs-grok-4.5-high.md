### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): PRO built a genuinely compelling consequentialist frame (the "real experiment" happens uncontrolled in the field anyway) and pressed CON hard on the malaria-challenge concession as a fatal admission against absolutism. But PRO's execution was repeatedly undermined by clipping in four of five speeches, most damagingly in Rebuttal 1 where the promised rebuttal to CON's "alternatives exist" argument was cut off and never meaningfully revisited, leaving that CON line live and unanswered. CON's case stayed disciplined throughout: it drew a more precise, medically grounded line (near-complete reversibility plus certain rescue, as in malaria) and specifically exploited PRO's weakest spot—TB latency and incomplete influenza rescue—showing PRO's "manageable risk" standard doesn't actually resolve those cases. PRO's closing asserted TB/flu challenge models "already operate safely" without engaging CON's latency/incomplete-clearance point, leaving CON's sharpest rebuttal standing. CON's category-error framing (manufactured illness vs. passive outbreak harm) was never fully dismantled either, since PRO's ledger argument, while rhetorically strong, didn't establish why alternatives (adaptive designs, bridging strategies) couldn't substitute when speed is needed. Combined with PRO's recurring clipping cost, CON ends the debate with the cleaner, more consistently defended line and the less-answered pressure point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that TB risks latency and incomplete clearance while influenza risks severe pneumonia even with antivirals directly undercut PRO's claim that "manageable risk" already justifies broadening beyond malaria, and PRO's Closing never specifically answered the latency objection.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that deliberate infection and outbreak harm need not be morally identical to be weighed together, then limiting broader permission through consent, manageable risk, scientific necessity, and case-specific review. Side B offered a serious rescue-based boundary, but its near-absolute commission argument sat uneasily with its malaria exception, and it never established why informed volunteers under strict safeguards must categorically be treated as mere instruments. A's repeated clipping reduced the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In Rebuttal 2, A directly answered the category-error charge by distinguishing equivalence from proportional weighing and clarified that animal-model inadequacy is necessary, not sufficient, for permission. This materially weakened B's claim that A's framework automatically authorizes challenges for every hard pathogen.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON wins the key precedent clash. PRO used malaria to collapse an absolute prohibition, but CON repaired to a non-arbitrary medical line - vanishing irreversible harm plus near-certain complete rescue - which explains why malaria is narrow and TB latency / influenza severe pneumonia fail it. PRO's counter line "manageable risk" stayed elastic and was reduced by repeated word-limit clipping. CON also held the commission vs omission distinction against the body-count ledger, leaving its boundary as the cleaner reason to keep "more broadly" exceptional.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 in Rebuttal 2 distinguishing malaria's full clearance at clear thresholds from TB latency and influenza incomplete rescue, which neutralized PRO's best Q2 precedent attack and turned PRO's "manageable risk" into an open invitation.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): A built a consistent, complete case: animal-model failure creates a real fidelity gap, autonomous informed consent under layered safeguards (stop-rules, rescue therapy, independent boards, insurance) closes that gap without licensing drift, and the scale of unchosen preventable death outweighs residual risk voluntarily accepted by screened volunteers. A directly answered both pressure questions with A1/A2 and used them to reinforce the "narrow, non-scalable, consent-anchored" limiting principle. B's core objection—that deliberate infection is categorically different from Phase I uncertainty and thus voids consent—was a serious and repeatedly asserted claim, but it was never fully defended against A's parallel to ordinary non-therapeutic trials, and B's organ-harvesting analogy was actually disanalogous (no consent there, consent here), a vulnerability A's closing implicitly exploited. Crucially, B's Opening, Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2, and Closing were all clipped for exceeding word limits, meaning every major B speech except the Pressure Questions round lost its concluding force—including the closing, which cuts off mid-sentence. Per the judging framework, this is a real, repeated execution cost that undermines B's ability to crystallize its case, while A delivered every speech intact and progressively sharpened its consent/scale framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point that CON's "unknowable harm negates consent" standard would also ban ordinary Phase I trials, forcing B into a strained intent-based distinction that was asserted but never fully substantiated, especially given B's own clipped, incomplete follow-through.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): A won through the clearer comparison between voluntary, tightly controlled risk and the larger unchosen harm of delayed medical progress. B never established why uncertainty makes consent impossible rather than merely demanding stricter safeguards; its distinction based on deliberate infection showed moral concern but did not negate adult agency. B also conceded its alternatives were currently incomplete, while repeated clipping imposed an execution cost. A did overstate how fully safeguards can prevent severe events or trust loss, keeping the debate competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Phase I comparison was decisive: it exposed that incomplete knowledge of delayed harms does not automatically invalidate consent. B answered by emphasizing intent, but that established ethical distinctiveness rather than proving that voluntary consent cannot exist, and its unwilling-organ-donor analogy failed because challenge-study volunteers opt in.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central consent clash. CON's live route was that unknown harms after failed animal models make true consent impossible. B's Phase I parity [B3.1] exposed that standard as overbroad, and A's intent-based reply [A4.1] never solved residual-unknown consent in non-therapeutic healthy-volunteer research. B then gave clean, non-scalable limits and concrete safeguards [B4.1-B4.2] that directly answered CON's drift and trust-collapse pressures, while CON's categorical A2 (no threshold) refused trade-off weighing required by the proposition. Repeated clipping of A turns was an execution cost that left autonomy-vs-scale comparison to B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1/A1 sequence on Phase I trials – showing CON's impossibility-of-consent standard proves too much and collapsing CON's core justification for absolute refusal.
