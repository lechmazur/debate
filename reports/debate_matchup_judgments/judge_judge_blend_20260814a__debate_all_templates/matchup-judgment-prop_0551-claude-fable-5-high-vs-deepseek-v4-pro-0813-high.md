### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically trapping Side B in a dilemma between redundant regulation and impossible litigation. By forcing Side B to concede that judicial enforcement in the developing world would have to be "progressively sequenced" based on budgets, Side A successfully proved that Side B's proposed legal entitlement would ultimately reduce to the exact same discretionary funding Side B was trying to escape, just with added lawsuits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal and closing brilliantly weaponized Side B's concession about "progressive realization," pointing out that a legal right whose delivery date depends entirely on legislative budgets is indistinguishable from discretionary policy.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON set the central dilemma — enforceability is either redundant to existing regulation or creates connection-order litigation — and PRO never resolved it, conceding a citizen backstop to compel buildout while admitting judgments don't produce fiber tomorrow. The moving-target/vagueness and regressive cross-subsidy costs were largely dropped by PRO, leaving CON's outcome-focused comparison — adaptive targeted subsidies vs symbolic duty plus docket — as the cleaner voting path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's R1 dilemma [A2.1-A2.2] that PRO must be either redundant or ruinous litigation, later crystallized as redundant / litigious / symbolic, which forced PRO to concede standing to compel and progressive realization.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively trapping Side A on the practical mechanism of "legal enforceability." B forced A into a dilemma: either the enforceable right is merely regulatory oversight (which B proved already exists, making the motion redundant), or it is an individually justiciable right (which B showed would result in courts micromanaging infrastructure spending without the power to actually lay fiber). When A attempted to split the difference in Rebuttal 2 by claiming residents gain "standing" to compel agencies, B brilliantly dismantled this by pointing out it simply means judges reviewing procurement and subsidy decisions "valley by valley, docket by docket." B's closing crystallized the clash perfectly with the memorable and accurate framing: "Vote for the approach that lays fiber, not the one that files suit." B's argumentation, rebuttals, and rhetorical execution were sharper and more grounded in the realities of infrastructure deployment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they took Side A's specific answer to the pressure question (that residents gain "standing" to compel agencies) and demonstrated how this practically translates to judges micromanaging infrastructure procurement dockets, effectively neutralizing Side A's attempt to escape the redundancy versus litigation dilemma.
