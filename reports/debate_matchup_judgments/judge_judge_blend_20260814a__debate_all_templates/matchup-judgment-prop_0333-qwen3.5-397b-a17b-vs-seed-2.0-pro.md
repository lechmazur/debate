### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO (Side B) holds the stronger case across the full exchange. Its core mechanism — that early closures do not reduce total intoxication but merely redirect it to unregulated, higher-risk spaces with no bartender cut-offs, no security, and no transit access — is established in the opening and survives CON's responses throughout. CON's best counter (bars carry legal liability; home hosts do not) is a real point, but CON then argues that extending bar hours equals net-new consumption, which would be true with or without transit extensions and therefore does not isolate the policy variable under debate. The "moral hazard" argument (transit availability removes the psychological barrier to another drink) is plausible but speculative, and CON never adequately explains why the counterfactual — drinking at home and driving from a residential address — is safer. On the mass-exodus claim, PRO's synchronization point is mechanically coherent and supported by the 2020 curfew example; CON's rejoinder that transit creates "a later, drunker mass exodus" misses that PRO's mechanism specifically depends on synchronization overwhelming transport capacity, not merely on people leaving at all. CON's strongest uncontested point is geographic coverage — transit serves dense corridors, not suburban roads where drunk driving is prevalent — but PRO's response that nightlife disorder and DUI incidents are concentrated in exactly the entertainment districts transit serves is adequate, and CON never develops the suburban-origin share into a decisive comparative. CON's "prevention vs. mitigation" framing is rhetorically clean and the "stop the fuel source" metaphor is memorable, but PRO's "risk laundering" reframe correctly identifies that early closures do not actually prevent harm — they relocate it to places harder to monitor. Both sides answered the pressure questions with A1:/A2: labels as required. PRO's answers were marginally stronger: A1 correctly reframes the net-new-consumption claim, and A2 addresses transit-hub disorder by noting concentration makes security feasible. CON's answer to Q2 (the 2020 DUI spike) — dismissing it as enforcement artifact from a "unique crisis" — is defensible but incomplete, leaving PRO's synchronization evidence partially intact. The margin is clear but competitive; CON runs a coherent case and the geographic-coverage gap is a live unaddressed burden for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to CON's "prevention vs. mitigation" framing — recast as "risk laundering" — is the most decisive exchange. CON's entire case depends on early closures genuinely preventing impairment rather than merely hiding it. PRO's combination of the unregulated-spaces argument and the 2020 curfew DUI spike directly undercuts the assumption that a forced closure reduces total danger rather than displacing it. CON never produces a clean answer to how bartender cut-offs and commercial liability apply to the house parties and pre-gaming that early closures demonstrably encourage, leaving the "prevention" claim hollow at its foundation.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): Side A wins because it directly answered the pressure questions on unregulated shifts and 2020 spikes, maintained a live comparative advantage on mass-exodus peaks vs prevention, and crystallized the risk-laundering point in closing while B repeated the same prevention-mitigation frame without neutralizing the core shift evidence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1/A4.2 directly neutralized B3.1 and B3.2 on consumption and hubs, preserving the transit advantage on drunk-driving risk.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B wins because it built a more direct and robust comparative path to the motion. Its core mechanism—transit enabling gradual dispersal that eliminates synchronized mass-exodus peaks of impaired drivers and street disorder—was concrete, well-supported by the 2020 curfew example, and better grounded than Side A's prevention story. Side A's "net-new consumption" argument remained plausible but relied on an contested empirical assumption that Side B adequately undermined by showing drinking shifts to higher-risk, unregulated spaces. Side B also decisively neutralized Side A's last-mile and transit-hub disorder objections by demonstrating that a stumbling pedestrian is trivial compared to an impaired driver, and that fixed hubs can be policed whereas early closures force unmanageable dispersed disorder. Side A's dismissal of the 2020 curfew spike as mere enforcement targeting was speculative and failed to restore its mass-exodus argument, while its suburban coverage point was largely dropped after Side B noted that nightlife risk concentrates in dense districts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal neutralizing Side A's last-mile and transit-hub disorder points by showing a drunk walker is trivial compared to an impaired driver, and that fixed transit hubs allow targeted security while early closures create dangerous, unmanageable dispersed disorder across residential streets and sidewalks.
