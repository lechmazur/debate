### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's core mechanism, proving that a legal requirement for explicit consent functions merely as a form requirement, not a bargaining requirement. Side A successfully argued that studios could simply mandate specific AI boilerplate at onboarding, leaving vulnerable actors in the exact same power imbalance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, introducing the brilliant framing that 'specificity of language is not specificity of power' and showing that existing right-of-publicity laws already cover Side B's most extreme examples.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): CON wins by exposing two structural flaws in PRO's mandate that PRO never repaired. First, CON showed the correction-generation line remains irreducibly vague for the actual hard cases (de-aging within a role, contracted ADR), meaning the rule would create litigation risk without clear benefit. Second, and decisively, CON demonstrated that PRO's "explicit consent" requirement can be satisfied by the very boilerplate it claims to prevent—a studio can hand a performer a franchise-specific template at onboarding naming the tool and a flat fee, preserving the same power imbalance. PRO repeatedly asserted the consent must be "use-bound" and "separate" but never explained why an onboarding template wouldn't satisfy those words, leaving CON's "form requirement, not bargaining requirement" critique unanswered. PRO's principled distinction between correction and generation is intuitively appealing, but CON successfully showed the mandate is either redundant (for new-project misappropriation, covered by existing likeness law) or ineffective (for within-role alterations, gameable by boilerplate).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4) answer to A3.2, showing that PRO's "specific, separate, use-bound" consent requirement can be satisfied by an onboarding template naming the franchise, tool, and flat fee—proving the mandate is a form requirement that preserves the power imbalance, not a bargaining floor.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): PRO held the core: correction vs generation is a workable functional line that law routinely uses without numeric thresholds, and requiring separate, use-bound consent with compensation changes the default even if signed under pressure. CON's strongest push — that explicit consent collapses into boilerplate and that ads are already covered by publicity law — actually conceded the easy case and left the live middle case (de-aging/synthetic lines within a hired role) unprotected by existing law or union coverage for nonunion performers, which PRO's floor answers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 A2 — CON showed a franchise-named template at onboarding could meet PRO's own specificity test, crystallizing the form-vs-power clash that defined the debate.
