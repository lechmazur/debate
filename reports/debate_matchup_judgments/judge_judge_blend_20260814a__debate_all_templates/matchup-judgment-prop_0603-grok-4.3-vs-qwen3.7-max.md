### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but Side B held a real edge on the methodological core of the proposition. The decisive clash was over spatial controls: Side A introduced a creative and plausible move—designating Preservation Reference Zones alongside mined strips as concurrent baselines—but Side B's pressure question Q1 exposed its genuine weakness. Spatial equivalence confirmed at the moment of survey designation does not establish what either zone's natural variation history looked like, meaning regulators still cannot distinguish mining-driven change from background fluctuation without temporal data. Side A's rebuttal (A4.1) answered the snapshot mapping point but never engaged the natural variation rate problem, leaving the vulnerability live. Side B's irreversibility argument (B1.2) was also durable throughout—once the pre-mining state is gone, no retrospective reconstruction is possible, and Side A never directly neutralized this. Side A's strongest contribution was the terrestrial mining opportunity-cost argument, which had genuine force: a pause does shift demand to already-damaging terrestrial supply chains. Side B's counter (B4.2, B5.2) that the relative harm comparison is precisely what baseline data would establish is methodologically sound but somewhat circular as a policy answer. However, B correctly noted in B2.1 that baselines are needed for the specific extraction zones—not the whole ocean—deflating Side A's "permanent ban" reframe. Side B's definition of the pause endpoint (statistical bounds on natural variability in target zones, not a predetermined calendar) answered Side A's pressure question Q1 directly and competently. Overall, Side B's case was more tightly aligned with the proposition's actual terms—verification before approval—while Side A's strongest material operated at the level of downstream policy consequences rather than refuting the sequencing logic. The margin is real but the debate was competitive throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure question Q1 (B3.1) was the most decisive exchange: it forced Side A to defend whether spatial controls could actually confirm the mined and reference zones shared the same original conditions. Side A's answer in A4.1 claimed modern acoustic and visual surveys "statistically match" both zones before extraction begins, but this only established contemporaneous equivalence at designation—not that the zones have equivalent natural variation histories. Side B pressed this residual gap in B4.3, and it remained unresolved, undermining the core adaptive-management rebuttal that Side A relied on throughout.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate tied to the motion: approvals should wait for independently verified baselines because otherwise adaptive management lacks a reliable pre-disturbance reference and irreversible harm becomes unmeasurable. Side A raised the strongest counterweight with green-transition and terrestrial-mining opportunity costs, but leaned on overconfident claims that the pause would become permanent or guarantee climate and terrestrial catastrophe. B adequately narrowed the pause to zone-specific, measurable baselines and made A's spatial-control alternative look insufficiently anchored.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive rebuttal was that spatial controls cannot replace robust pre-mining baselines, because regulators still need an anchor for natural variability and comparability before judging mining impacts. A's answer using reference zones was plausible, but did not fully solve the temporal and attribution problem.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side B won the comparative clash by demonstrating that Side A's demand for multi-year temporal baselines functions as a de facto ban that forces reliance on highly destructive terrestrial mining. Side B successfully distinguished between temporal and spatial baselines, explaining how the exploration phase provides adequate spatial equivalence before extraction, effectively neutralizing Side A's core claim that regulators would be flying blind.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1 and B4.2 effectively dismantled Side A's pressure questions by clarifying that spatial baselines are established with pre-extraction exploration data and explaining how a pause permanently kills the economic viability needed for any future substitution.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins on the stronger resolution of the central clash. The decisive issue is whether meaningful environmental protection is possible without independently verified baselines. Side A's adaptive management case depends on the ability to set valid trigger thresholds, but A never resolves how nodule-density gradients and sediment properties translate into ecosystem-level collapse thresholds when species inventories are admittedly incomplete. B's pressure question Q1 exposed this gap cleanly, and A's Rebuttal 2 answer quietly shifted the goal from "preventing irreversible harm" to "limiting plume spread to designated zones"—a significant goalpost move that B correctly identified as not the same thing. B also answered A's pressure questions more directly: independent oceanographic institutions can gather data without contractor logistics (A1), and terrestrial mining expansion reflects demand surge rather than seabed supply absence (A2). Side A's strongest live argument—that pausing shifts environmental pressure to weaker terrestrial jurisdictions—lands and is never fully neutralized, but B's distinction between remediable terrestrial damage and geologically permanent seabed destruction reduces its weight. A's closing attempts to reframe the pause as a de-facto permanent ban, which is a reasonable comparative point, but B had already maintained throughout that it is an operational prerequisite with a defined endpoint, and A never demonstrated why independent baseline collection is practically impossible absent extraction approvals. B's framing on conflict of interest (entities profiting from extraction setting ecological limits) also remains standing. The margin is clear but competitive: A fights effectively on comparative harm and the perfect-knowledge strawman, but the threshold-setting hole in the adaptive management argument and the conflict-of-interest concern are more damaging to A's case than anything A inflicts on B's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure question Q1 exposing that CON's "trigger thresholds" require knowing the baseline state CON simultaneously admits is incomplete. B's Rebuttal 2 (A1 answer) then paired this with the institutional solution—independent research vessels can collect pre-extraction data during a pause—making the exchange the most decisive in the debate by showing both that A's monitoring framework is internally contradictory and that B's operational alternative is viable.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate centered on the proposition’s key burden: approvals should not proceed before robust, independently verified baselines exist. Side A’s terrestrial-mining tradeoff was relevant and plausible, but it never fully proved that approving deep-sea mining without independent baselines is safer than pausing, and it repeatedly leaned on a “perfect knowledge” strawman. Side B more cleanly showed why adaptive management and trigger thresholds depend on reliable pre-extraction baselines, especially given irreversible nodule removal and conflicts in industry-generated data.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was against Side A’s adaptive-management claim: without an independently verified baseline, thresholds and monitoring cannot reliably distinguish manageable disturbance from irreversible ecological harm until after damage occurs.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A wins by effectively dismantling Side B's reliance on 'adaptive management' by repeatedly demonstrating that you cannot measure or mitigate deviations without an independently established baseline. Side A also provided superior weighing on the comparative harms of terrestrial versus deep-sea mining by contrasting their remediation potential.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the terrestrial mining alternative, pointing out that land-based mining occurs within established regulatory frameworks where remediation is possible, whereas deep-sea extraction destroys millions-of-years-old substrates permanently.
