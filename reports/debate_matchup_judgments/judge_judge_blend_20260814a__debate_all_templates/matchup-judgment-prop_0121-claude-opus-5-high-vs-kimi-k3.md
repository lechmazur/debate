### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A won the default-setting clash. It showed that privacy can be waived easily by a supported adult, while requiring a special request predictably fails for frightened patients who lack information or cannot disclose family danger at intake. Side B offered plausible concerns about billing accountability and family support, but treated the motion as mandatory secrecy rather than an ability to obtain private care. Its generic-receipt position was also unstable: if the receipt reveals nothing meaningful, it cannot provide the early warning B claimed; if it reveals a mental-health visit, A's deterrence concern returns. A's clipped turns were an execution cost but did not erase its clearer comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was the error-asymmetry rebuttal in A4: a supportive patient can voluntarily tell family, whereas a vulnerable patient may not know to request confidentiality or be able to justify it before trust develops. This directly weakened B's targeted-request alternative.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A won by brilliantly reframing the debate around the mechanics of defaults. Side A successfully proved that an opt-out privacy default allows supported youth to easily loop in their families, while Side B's opt-in privacy default forces terrified youth to navigate bureaucracy and fear just to get help.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing exposed the fatal flaw in Side B's "early warning system" argument by pointing out that a supported youth can simply choose to tell their parents, whereas a frightened youth under Side B's rule might never make it to intake.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins on the default question. CON's strongest move was distinguishing triggered confidentiality from automatic concealment and endorsing detail-light EOBs, but PRO turns that concession into compliance with the motion's actual wording and wins the comparison of errors: under CON's opt-in model a frightened 18-year-old who doesn't know a request exists or can't name the danger at intake learns the rule from an envelope and gets no care; under PRO's opt-out model a supported 21-year-old loses only a form letter she can waive in one sentence at intake.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2's collapse that CON's "targeted fixes" (rerouted/confidential communications, detail-light EOBs) *are* the proposition, combined with the fraud reversal — sending the statement to the actual patient improves auditing — and the affluence-test counter to cash-pay alternatives.
