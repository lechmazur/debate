### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): PRO built a durable "double burden" argument—operators receive tax breaks while tariffs spread grid upgrade costs to all ratepayers, so the community pays twice—and CON never fully neutralized it. CON's most important concession came in B4.1, where it acknowledged that Northern Virginia rates rose due to transmission buildouts socialized by design. PRO correctly crystallized this in closing: "by design" does not mean the public isn't being harmed; it just confirms the mechanism PRO identified. CON's strongest counter was the institutional authority argument (municipalities cannot certify a standard set by state utility commissions), and it was pressed sharply in B3.1. PRO's answer—that the cap conditions the incentive on the operator demonstrating it has covered its marginal costs, which is distinct from the municipality setting rates—was workable and consistent with common development-agreement practice. CON never dismantled this distinction in B4; it restated the authority problem without showing why the conditioning mechanism specifically fails. CON's capital-mobility argument had surface force but PRO's judo flip (if operators truly can't relocate, externalities are permanent, making the cap more rather than less necessary) was not answered. CON's "pipeline avoidance proves unworkability" reasoning was logically weak, and PRO's Q2 exposed it. PRO's opening was clipped—a real execution cost—but the case was largely reconstructed through rebuttals. CON's argument was technically competent but relied on repeating the "category error" frame without fully closing the gap PRO had opened on the double-burden mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.1–A4.2 response to CON's Q1 on institutional authority: PRO cleanly separated "conditioning a subsidy on demonstrated cost coverage" from "rewriting utility ratemaking," showing that state tariff authority and municipal incentive conditions can coexist. This deflated CON's sharpest institutional objection without overclaiming, and CON's B4 rejoinder did not show why that distinction fails in practice.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): B won by making the core fiscal fairness clash clearer: tax abatements are unjustified when grid and water costs are shifted onto ratepayers and communities. A’s strongest point was the category-error/admin objection, but B answered that the policy need not rewrite utility rates; it can condition subsidies on demonstrated cost recovery or contractual commitments. A’s capital-flight claim remained plausible but overstated, especially because the motion caps incentives rather than banning projects.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive rebuttal was against A’s “existing tariffs already internalize costs” claim: B showed that broad transmission and water burdens can still be socialized, so incentives create a double public subsidy unless capped.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A won by cleanly differentiating between state utility rate-setting and municipal tax policy, neutralizing Side B's main argument about regulatory preemption. Side A also effectively capitalized on Side B's admission that regional transmission costs are socialized, using it to prove that current frameworks fail to fully internalize costs without the proposed cap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively clarified that conditioning a local tax break is not equivalent to rewriting state utility tariffs, and brilliantly flipped Side B's latency argument by noting that if operators cannot relocate, communities have the leverage to demand cost internalization.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): PRO ran the cleaner path through the decisive clashes. First, the motion's conditionality is PRO's structural anchor: the cap lifts when costs are internalized, so efficient operators keep their incentives. CON kept framing this as punishing efficiency, but never escaped PRO's Q1 answer—if you meet the condition, no cap applies. CON's A1 response pivoted to compliance burden and vagueness, which was their best counter, but PRO had already pre-answered it by pointing to existing PUC long-run marginal cost benchmarks as the measurement standard. CON never squarely neutralized that mechanism. Second, PRO's Q2 exposed the core vulnerability in CON's surcharge-as-alternative theory: a utility rebate bundled into a development agreement can nullify a metered surcharge entirely, leaving the double subsidy intact. CON's reply was that a cap can also be waived, but PRO's distinction—statutory cap override requires explicit legislative action and public record, while utility rebates are buried in administrative agreements—was a meaningful structural difference CON never answered. Third, the capital-flight objection ran both ways and didn't clearly favor CON; PRO argued CON's light-touch approach is already producing the race to the bottom. CON's rejoinder that the cap relocates the problem rather than stopping it was plausible but unsubstantiated, while PRO's mechanism for why a hard legislative ceiling is harder to circumvent than a line-item surcharge was more concrete. CON's case was competent and raised genuine issues—especially definitional vagueness—but each key challenge was met with a substantive PRO answer that CON did not squarely rebut.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 (a municipality can bundle a tax holiday with a utility rebate to nullify a surcharge) and PRO's subsequent A2 (internalization is defined by existing PUC cost-of-service benchmarks, making a discounted rate below that benchmark an identifiable subsidy by definition) was the most decisive exchange. It simultaneously damaged CON's core alternative mechanism and gave PRO a concrete standard for the motion's "fully internalized" condition—two burdens answered in one move that CON never recovered from.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): B won by keeping the debate centered on the motion's conditional cap as an enforcement mechanism, not a ban on incentives. A had a plausible alternative in targeted surcharges, but never convincingly answered B's point that those fees can be neutralized through utility rebates and bargaining. A also repeatedly overstated the cap as blanket punishment despite the "unless costs are internalized" condition. B gave the cleaner comparative story: tax incentives can continue, but only after public grid and water costs are covered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that the cap flips the legal default was decisive: surcharges may be priced correctly on paper, but without a statutory ceiling municipalities can rebate or waive them in bidding wars, recreating the hidden subsidy.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A consistently outmaneuvered Side B on the enforcement mechanism, successfully proving that a statutory cap flips the legal default and prevents cities from offering hidden rebates. Side A also effectively neutralized B's claim that the cap punishes efficiency by emphasizing the motion's conditionality and using existing Public Utility Commission metrics to answer B's pressure on vagueness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively answered B's pressure questions by pointing out that "fully internalized" uses existing PUC cost-of-service benchmarks, exposing that B's alternative of precise surcharges requires the exact same accounting B just claimed was too vague.
