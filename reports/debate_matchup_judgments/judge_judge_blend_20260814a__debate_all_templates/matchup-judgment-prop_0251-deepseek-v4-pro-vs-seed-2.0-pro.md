### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON's three-pronged attack (wage loss to informal/vulnerable workers, heterogeneous acclimatization defeating a universal threshold, and displacement of work to unsupervised night shifts) was pressed consistently and never fully neutralized. PRO's strongest counter—that a WBGT bright-line makes enforcement objectively verifiable rather than a wage dispute—was a good move, but it never squarely answered why undocumented or informal workers would actually receive that pay or file that claim, and PRO's rebuttal that night work "already happens" sidestepped the volume-shift concern rather than refuting it. CON's closing crisply crystallized the decisive clash ("this mandate hides the choice, it doesn't remove it"), while PRO's opening, second rebuttal, and closing were all clipped for length, costing it completed arguments at exactly the moments it needed to land its strongest framing (especially the closing, cut off mid-sentence). That execution failure, combined with CON's sharper, more consistent rebuttals across rounds, gives CON a clear but not overwhelming win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 response to PRO's WBGT/pay-enforcement claims—pointing out that a "drafting fix" doesn't make an employer who already evades minimum wage suddenly pay for a stopped shift, and that acclimatization can't be reduced to a single index—forced PRO onto the defensive for the rest of the debate about whether the mandate actually reaches the most vulnerable workers it claims to protect.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won by framing exposure to lethal heat as employer coercion rather than meaningful worker choice, then showing how an objective, adjustable threshold creates a clearer and more enforceable right. Side A exposed a genuine weakness around payment enforcement, but relied too heavily on asserting that work would simply move underground without proving that cooler night work would be comparably dangerous. Side B’s repeated clipping reduced its margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B most decisively answered the displacement argument: moving work to cooler hours does not preserve the acute heat hazard, while lighting and supervision can be separately regulated. This made Side A’s claimed tradeoff look speculative beside the immediate danger of extreme heat.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO kept a live rights-based route: heat like fall protection needs a clear yes/no duty to cure power imbalance. CON's three prongs—lost income, no threshold, enforcement failure/underground shift—were directly answered: paid stop with verifiable WBGT trigger changes the legal terrain, WBGT already handles humidity/workload, and a bright line makes enforcement easier, not harder. CON never solved its own paradox that fixing enforcement without a standard is empty, and its strongest moral point about informal wages was mitigated by PRO's verifiable-trigger + reporting-pay logic. Clipping penalizes A for word-limit execution but does not erase the comparative win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2/A4 flip that vague water/rest rules are unenforceable precisely because they lack a measurable violation, so CON's enforcement-failure objection becomes the reason to adopt a clear WBGT stop-work threshold with a public weather trigger.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): B built a clean, consistent case around employer conflict of interest and forced A to abandon its strongest opening claim (that stop-work means unpaid stop-work) by correctly noting mandated stops are paid, like existing lightning holds. B's pressure questions were sharper and better answered by B's own A1/A2, especially the closing move that a bright-line threshold gives a worker self-enforcement power a flexible plan never can. A had genuinely strong counter-mechanisms (cost-driven job/hour reduction, humidity/acclimatization miscalibration, enforcement-not-design diagnosis), but never fully neutralized B's core "who decides, and who can self-enforce" framing, and repeatedly ran over the word limit—clipped in Opening, Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2, and crucially the Closing—costing it clarity and punch exactly when crystallization mattered most. That combination of B's cleaner rebuttal work and A's repeated execution failures gives B the clearer, more complete path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 correcting A's "unpaid stop-work" framing and reframing paid, time-bound stops as analogous to existing lightning-hold practice, which forced A to retreat to a weaker, more speculative "job elimination via idle-pay cost" argument for the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won a competitive debate by showing that heat risk varies across humidity, exertion, radiant load, and acclimatization, while Side B never adequately justified a rigid trigger over enforceable prevention plans. A also exposed the compliance problem: supervisors already violating heat rules may violate a stop line too. B offered a compelling worker-empowerment frame, but weakened it with unsupported absolutes about fatalities, compliant plans, and typical shutdown duration. A's repeated clipping was a meaningful execution cost, but not enough to erase its stronger policy comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange argued that a bright line does not itself create compliance and that the same retaliation and enforcement failures remain. Side B's response that workers could enforce a public threshold was rhetorically strong, but A directly answered that workers facing unlawful retaliation still lack practical power.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO wins the central clash on enforcement. CON argued flexible prevention plans fail only from weak enforcement and that a foreman who ignores water will ignore a thermometer. PRO turned that into their strongest live route: an objective, public stop line is the one rule a low-power worker can invoke without debating acclimatization or exertion. That empowerment claim, plus reframing stop-work as paid/shiftable 2-4 hour pauses (like lightning holds) rather than unpaid job destruction, survived CON's scale and complexity attacks. CON's valid heat-is-a-stew point was never fully answered, but CON's repeated word-limit clips and reliance on hypothetical hour-hollowing vs PRO's documented deaths left PRO with the cleaner comparative weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's R2/A2 and Closing rebuttal that every fatal heat death occurred under discretionary flexible plans, so the fix must remove the final call from the conflicted foreman and give workers a self-enforceable thermometer rule – directly answering CON's enforcement-failure argument.
