### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO built a cleaner path to victory through the structural contradiction it crystallized in [B4.5] and [B5.2]: CON's own concessions reveal that mega-events require strong local capacity to succeed (Athens, Rio fail without it), yet CON recommends them specifically for declining cities that lack that capacity. This means the tool is most dangerous where most needed and most redundant where least needed. CON's response—that "the hard deadline forces capacity into being"—is partially circular and ungrounded; the Barcelona example, CON's best case, is itself a city with pre-existing institutional depth, which PRO had already flagged. CON's survivorship bias point ([A2.4])—that Fringe and Mardi Gras are century-old outliers—had real force and was PRO's most underanswered burden. However, PRO's implicit counter (the motion asks what institutions should prefer, not which approach guarantees global fame; recurring events carry lower downside risk even if not all succeed) held up adequately. PRO's examples were more varied and mutually reinforcing (Edinburgh, New Orleans, Oaxaca as positives; Athens, Rio, Montreal as negatives), whereas CON's case rested heavily on Barcelona, making CON more exposed to cherry-picking charges. Both sides suffered clipped speeches, neutralizing that execution cost. CON's "maintenance vs. creation" and "overwrite not amplify" framings were genuine contributions that kept the debate competitive, and the closing was punchy, but they arrived too late to resolve the structural contradiction PRO had already landed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's [B4.5]—"the amplifier needs something to amplify"—directly connected CON's own admission that governance matters to its claim that mega-events benefit declining cities. This exposed a genuine paradox: if the event only works when local capacity is already high, it is not the catalytic circuit-breaker CON promised but a risky amplifier of existing conditions. CON's answer ("the deadline forces alignment") never explained why governance quality needed for a mega-event would materialize in cities that demonstrably lack institutional capacity, leaving this contradiction as the debate's most consequential live burden.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins because it exposes the structural risks and ownership deficits in mega-events more convincingly, answers the pressure questions directly, and crystallizes the paradox that CON's catalyst works best where least needed while leaving local recurring events as the durable path to place identity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Questions on governance and scripting, answered in B4, combined with the closing paradox, most decisively neutralized CON's deterministic transformation claim and forced weighing on capacity and sustainability.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by exposing a structural contradiction in CON's case: mega-events are pitched as catalysts for declining cities, yet CON concedes they fail without strong pre-existing governance and capacity—the very thing declining cities lack. While CON framed mega-events as circuit-breakers that manufacture alignment, they could not produce an example of a mega-event successfully manufacturing capacity in a struggling city, whereas PRO cited Athens, Rio, and Montreal as cautionary tales. PRO's alternative—recurring creator-led events that compound identity and can achieve global recognition authentically—emerged as the more coherent and institutionally prudent preference. Both sides clipped word limits, but the decisive clash resolved against CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's identification that CON's model is "most dangerous precisely where it is most needed, and only reliable where it is least necessary," using CON's own concessions about Barcelona's existing capacity and Athens/Rio's governance failures.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Both sides argued competently throughout, but Side A (CON) built a slightly stronger path to victory on the key clashes. First, CON's "safe compounding of invisibility still yields invisibility" line—deployed in Rebuttal 2 against the Brisbane case—was the debate's most decisive single move and was never squarely answered by PRO; it directly rebuts PRO's variance argument by showing that low-risk iteration can lock in obscurity for cities that most need transformation. Second, CON's insularity argument (recurring events serve existing communities; they affirm rather than compel outward projection) was maintained across all rounds, and PRO's responses—"iteration naturally expands," "authenticity travels"—were asserted rather than demonstrated. Third, CON's counter-standardization examples (Tokyo's architectural philosophy, London's East End industrial heritage, Parc Jean-Drapeau as resident-inhabited fabric) materially blunted PRO's strongest pressure question. Fourth, CON's closing crystallized a memorable distinction—"mega-events build identity, local events maintain atmosphere"—that sharpened the motion's "build" framing rather than evading it. PRO's philosophical core ("identity is endogenous," "identity is practice not monument") was genuinely coherent and the survivorship-bias attack landed, but PRO's answers to the recognition/insularity problem never rose above assertion. The margin is narrow because PRO rightly pressed the "default strategy" framing and variance asymmetry, and CON's case ultimately relied on a subset of transformationally-needy cities rather than a true default. Still, CON's unanswered "recognition-as-necessary-for-identity" argument and the memorable closing rhetoric give it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 A1—"safe compounding of invisibility still yields invisibility"—was the sharpest exchange. It directly reversed PRO's risk-management framing: PRO had argued recurring events are safer; CON turned that back to show safety that preserves obscurity is no virtue for the cities most in need of building identity. PRO never answered this reversal.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the stronger case by establishing a clearer comparative frame: identity requires external recognition and transformative rupture for stagnant cities, supported by plausible mechanisms like forced coalition-building and integrated infrastructure (Barcelona, Montreal, Brisbane). They directly answered B's pressure questions on variance and standardization with concrete adaptations (Tokyo, London) and execution distinctions. Side B (PRO) effectively stressed ownership and iteration but failed to neutralize the insularity and invisibility burdens, retreating to safety without resolving why local compounding suffices for places needing breakthrough recognition. Whole-debate progression favored A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.3) decisively handling B's pressure questions by reframing failures as governance, showing local adaptation of standards, and preserving the transformation vs. maintenance clash.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a clearer, more cohesive framework for what place identity actually is—endogenous, practice-based, and compounding—and by successfully pressing the institutional question at stake. Side B showed that as a default strategy, recurring creator-led events safely build cultural infrastructure and authentic distinctiveness, whereas mega-events are high-variance gambles that often homogenize cities and leave debt or white elephants. While Side A landed effective blows with Barcelona, Montreal, and Brisbane, and skillfully blurred the line between infrastructure and identity, it never fully escaped Side B's pressure on variance and standardization. Side A's response that institutions merely need to "strategize" did not resolve why a risky, externally dictated spectacle should be the preferred default over locally owned recurrence. Side B's rebuttals consistently tied the debate back to the motion's core metric—lasting place identity as lived by residents—and closed with the memorable, cohesive frame that identity is a practice that compounds, not a spectacle that can be leased.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answer to Side A's pressure questions in Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.3), where B reframed recurring events as engines of continuous democratic negotiation and authentic distinctiveness that travels, directly neutralizing A's charges of insularity and nostalgia while reinforcing that identity belongs to residents first.
