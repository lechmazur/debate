- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the cleaner path to victory across the full exchange. Their core framing—that tipping hides labor costs behind social pressure rather than competing on real total price—was never squarely neutralized. The most decisive moment came from CON's own A1 answer to B's pressure question: admitting the system "partitions" costs to preserve volume. PRO correctly identified this in the closing as the admission that tipping depends on an incomplete sticker price, which collapses CON's honesty defense. PRO also answered CON's two strongest arguments adequately: on service-charge leakiness, they clarified that the wage itself is payroll-protected regardless of how the charge is labelled, shifting the frame to clear employment terms rather than pass-through trust; on real-time incentives, "instant is not the same as accurate" was a sharp, concise counter that CON never genuinely refuted—tipping rewards check size, customer mood, and bias as much as actual performance. CON's strongest original contributions—the legal distinction between tips and service-charge revenue, and the demand-elasticity/cover-count argument—were real and kept this competitive, but both were undermined: the leakiness point was partially answered, and the cover-count argument inadvertently reinforced PRO's hidden-cost claim. CON's "double dependency" rebuttal—that management still controls sections and schedules—was never cleanly answered on the worker-control question, which further eroded CON's "directly in workers' hands" rhetoric. PRO's closing crystallized the live clash cleanly while CON's closing was largely defensive repetition. Across rounds, PRO consistently narrowed the dispute and resolved it, while CON widened without landing a decisive counter.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to CON's A1 in Rebuttal 2 [B4.5]: after CON answered the hidden-cost pressure question by calling tipping "partitioning" to preserve volume, PRO immediately turned that admission into a concession that the model depends on obscuring real labor costs—the very thing PRO was contesting. This was the sharpest pivot in the debate and the moment CON's honesty defense effectively collapsed.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Both sides were substantively competent, but PRO (B) held a consistent structural edge across the exchange. PRO's strongest unanswered point was the back-of-house equity problem: CON's only response was "fairer pooling where allowed," which implicitly concedes the legal constraint and leaves the structural exclusion intact. PRO's demographics-over-quality argument on tips was also never seriously rebutted—CON's "a noisy incentive beats none" defense was weak because it accepts the bias without explaining why that is preferable to a professional standard. CON's best argument—that service charges route every dollar through management first, giving bosses more control—was sharp and was reinforced in A4.4 when CON noted PRO had to add "performance bonuses and profit-sharing" to recreate upside (implying more boss discretion, not less). However, PRO partially neutralized this by pointing out that managers already control tipping outcomes through scheduling, section assignments, and tip-out manipulation, making the comparative power question roughly a wash rather than a win for CON. The "survival bias vs. rational choice" clash was close, but PRO's framing held: when the system offers no stable floor, calling the lottery a "preference" is not convincing. CON's clipped Rebuttal 1 (A2.8) was a real execution cost—losing the closing of that speech in the critical first exchange. CON's overbreadth argument (one-size-fits-all mandate removes worker choice) was live throughout and is a genuine burden on PRO that PRO never fully answered, but PRO's structural narrative was more coherent and the back-of-house equity gap remained open. On balance, PRO built a cleaner comparative path to the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's A4.4 noting that PRO had to supplement their model with "measurable performance bonuses and profit-sharing" to recreate the upside tipping provides—demonstrating that PRO's system requires more managerial discretion, not less. This was a genuine, original catch that exposed a real tension in PRO's case and was not addressed in PRO's closing. However, it was insufficient to flip the result because PRO's back-of-house equity argument and the demographics-not-quality point remained unanswered at the core.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively turning Side A's arguments about management control and worker exploitation. B successfully demonstrated that Side A's alternative (service charges) simply routes all compensation through the employer as house revenue, thereby centralizing rather than reducing management's power over worker pay.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal (B4.4) brilliantly pointed out that A had to rely on "performance bonuses" and "profit-sharing" to replace the lost upside of tips, proving B's point that A's model hands total control over earnings back to management discretion.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side A won by clearly establishing tipping as a structural wage subsidy enabling volatility, demographic bias, and exclusion of back-of-house workers, while showing existing managerial control over sections/schedules/tip-outs already undermines CON's "direct pay" advantage. This neutralized CON's preference and incentive claims as survival adaptations rather than true choice, and the auditable base-pay model answered pressure on safeguards better than CON's generic incremental fixes. CON made solid points on worker upside and overbreadth but suffered from a clipped rebuttal, repetition of volatility romanticization, and weaker response to equity across all staff. The decisive clash on payroll control favored A's structural fix over targeted patches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2, which directly invoked guaranteed base pay and professional bonuses to counter B's questions on dilution and lost upside, sharpening the control comparison that persisted into both closings.
