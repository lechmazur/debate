### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Both sides opened competently and the moral framing was genuinely contested throughout, but CON (A) built the stronger technical case across the full exchange. The decisive edge came in CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's Q1. PRO's pressure question—"if a company won't launch at an affordable price, how are patients worse off than having a registered but unaffordable drug?"—was sharp and temporarily destabilized CON's registration-withholding argument. But CON's A1 response drew a meaningful and legally accurate distinction: a registered-but-expensive drug still permits compulsory licensing, generic pathways, donation programs, and future negotiation because a patent and regulatory dossier exist to work with; a never-registered drug eliminates all of those options, leaving patients in absolute absence. PRO's closing never answered this distinction, only restating "unaffordable equals non-existent" without engaging the structural difference CON identified. CON also landed an unrebutted point about biologics manufacturing complexity—complex biologics cannot be reverse-engineered locally even with a license—that PRO left completely unaddressed across the entire debate. CON's royalty paradox was partially but adequately resolved by PRO's global-aggregate-funding response, and PRO's leverage argument (threshold creates incentive to price below it) is historically grounded and real. The automatism-versus-leverage contradiction CON raised in Q1 was partially answered by PRO ("absolute consequence is the ultimate incentive"), but the underlying tension was never cleanly resolved. On balance, CON's registration-withholding argument and biologics complexity created live routes PRO did not neutralize, while PRO's strongest counter—the leverage mechanism—was structurally undermined by the motion's "whenever" language that CON correctly exploited.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's Q1 was the most decisive exchange. PRO asked how patients are worse off when a company withholds registration versus having an unaffordable registered drug. CON responded that without registration there is no patent to seize, no clinical dossier to copy, no supply chain, and no donation or negotiation pathway—absolute absence rather than difficult access. PRO's closing never engaged this distinction, simply repeating "unaffordable equals non-existent," leaving CON's structural point standing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): B won by making the automatic nature of the threshold the decisive problem: it could deter registration, technology transfer, and investment, leaving patients with empty shelves rather than cheap medicines. A had a strong access/social-contract case and effectively argued localized thresholds, but relied too heavily on the claim that unaffordable drugs are equivalent to unavailable drugs and never fully solved B’s innovation and royalty paradox. B also offered plausible alternatives such as bargaining, value-based pricing, voluntary licensing, and surgical compulsory use, which made its position look less like accepting unlimited prices.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was the response to A’s pressure question: B argued that non-registration is materially worse because compulsory licensing may not create a usable generic pathway without data, supply chains, know-how, or an originator product. That undercut A’s core claim that automatic licensing guarantees access.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B wins by successfully grounding the clash in practical and regulatory realities that Side A's idealized economic model could not overcome. While PRO built a compelling moral case and argued that the threshold acts as pricing leverage, CON decisively demonstrated that an automatic trigger would rationally cause originators to withhold market registration entirely. CON's explanation that compulsory licensing legally and practically requires an existing patent and regulatory dossier to override directly dismantled PRO's claim that "patients are at zero anyway." PRO never adequately answered how generics could be rapidly approved and manufactured without that originator footprint, especially for complex biologics. CON also sustained effective pressure on the "royalty paradox" and the contradiction between an automatic mandate and voluntary leverage, leaving PRO's mechanism looking self-defeating in practice. Both sides were rhetorically sharp, but CON's technical precision and cleaner comparative weighing secured the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 answer to Q1, explaining that an unregistered drug cannot be compulsorily licensed because there is no patent to seize, no clinical dossier to copy, and no supply chain to repurpose. This turned PRO's "patients are already at zero" claim on its head by showing that automatic triggers guarantee regulatory absence rather than affordable access, effectively neutralizing PRO's core moral leverage.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): The debate was genuinely competitive, with PRO holding a rhetorical and framing edge throughout (social contract, "real lives now," HIV success story) while CON identified and sustained the more decisive argumentative vulnerability. CON's core move—that the motion is universal in scope and that Western health systems like the NHS routinely deny expensive biologics on systemic affordability grounds—was introduced in Rebuttal 1 and pressed consistently through closing. PRO's central defensive response was that "sustainable financing capacity" excludes wealthy nations because their institutions already absorb costly treatments. But this defense was empirically fragile: NHS coverage refusals for high-cost oncology drugs are precisely the kind of systemic unaffordability PRO's own threshold targets. PRO never convincingly explained why those denials would not trigger a compulsory license under their standard, and the closing simply restated that "the US, Germany, and Japan already reimburse staggeringly expensive treatments"—true for some drugs, but not the drugs their own systems regularly refuse. CON's pressure on this point across Rebuttals 1 and 2 and both closings was the live route to victory that PRO failed to neutralize. PRO also left the Phase III financing gap partially open: gesturing at public-private partnerships without explaining how they would scale to cover all essential drug pipelines. CON's innovation collapse argument was somewhat speculative, but PRO never provided a concrete mechanism to fill the funding void. The geographic quarantine was PRO's load-bearing claim; CON cracked it without PRO ever repairing it. That gap tips the verdict to CON despite PRO's superior rhetorical presence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to A1 (the NHS move): PRO had claimed wealthy-nation health systems sustainably absorb expensive treatments, but CON correctly pointed out that Western systems routinely deny cutting-edge biologics on cost-effectiveness grounds—which is systemic unaffordability by PRO's own definition. PRO's closing answer ("the US, Germany, and Japan already reimburse staggeringly expensive treatments") simply restated the claim without addressing why NHS-style denials wouldn't trigger the threshold. This was the most decisive unrectified exchange of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive debate by exposing the weakness in A's key limiting principle. A made a strong moral and access case, especially around HIV generics and the patent social contract, but relied heavily on the claim that wealthy-market profits would remain untouched. B repeatedly and effectively argued that the motion is universal and that expensive essential medicines can exceed affordability thresholds even in wealthy health systems, which undercuts A's innovation answer. A's systemic-capacity clarification helped, but B showed that systemic budget strain exists in rich countries too, making A's geographic quarantine uncertain.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's geographic-illusion rebuttal was decisive: if the affordability threshold applies whenever essential medicines strain a country's health system, then wealthy nations can also trigger compulsory licensing, threatening the profit engine A depended on to preserve innovation.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B wins by successfully dismantling Side A's core assurance that the affordability threshold would be geographically quarantined to low-income markets. When Side A pivoted to defining affordability by systemic financing capacity, Side B sharply countered that wealthy health systems already deny coverage for cutting-edge biologics due to budget strain, meaning PRO's own standard would trigger compulsory licensing in the very markets that fund global R&D. Side B's explanation of the pharmaceutical risk math (high trial failure rates requiring premiums to fund the pipeline) remained structurally intact, while Side A's suggestion that public grants could replace multi-billion-dollar private risk capital was underdeveloped. Side B made the decisive clash clearer, exposed a functional contradiction in Side A's economic model, and provided the cleaner comparative reason to reject the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 directly answering Side A's systemic capacity pivot by noting that Western systems like the NHS already deny expensive drugs due to budget limits, proving the threshold would still trigger in wealthy nations and collapse the R&D profit engine.
