### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-organized cases, but CON maintained a tighter and more consistent framework across rounds: later hours as an ordinary municipal function, externalities as shared urban services already funded by nightlife's own tax contributions, and a persistent, unrebutted tension in PRO's case—PRO insisted mitigation plans were simultaneously "virtually costless" for small venues yet substantively effective at preventing transit, sanitation, and noise harms, and effective enough to "disarm" community opposition. CON repeatedly exploited this tension (selection effects, compliance friction, paperwork-as-veto) and PRO's Rebuttal 2 answers ("standardized templates," "virtually nothing") read as assertion rather than resolution of that contradiction. PRO's strongest thread—political sustainability via avoiding backlash-driven rollbacks—remained live but was met by a credible counter-mechanism (enforcement plus scaled public services), leaving the clash genuinely contested rather than decisively won by PRO. CON's closing crystallized the comparative choice more cleanly than PRO's, which leaned on repeated assertion rather than fresh resolution of the friction/selection critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3) pointing out that PRO's own "standardized, virtually costless" template answer still concedes application friction, staffing mandates, and scrutiny that independents absorb first—directly undercutting PRO's simultaneous claim that these same plans meaningfully prevent transit/sanitation/noise harms and disarm opposition.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by showing that venue-level conditional plans are mismatched to city-scale externalities, especially transit, and operate as compliance theater that filters out independent venues. CON successfully turned PRO's neighborhood-backlash argument by demonstrating that conditional licensing hands opponents a procedural veto rather than disarming them. PRO's retreat from "robust mandatory plans" to "virtually nothing" costs in rebuttal underscored CON's critique and left the transit safety gap unresolved, since a rideshare app does not replace actual late-night service. CON's weighing on public capacity, shared urban services, and barrier-to-entry remained the cleaner live route after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2, where they argued that PRO's venue-level rideshare zones and sidewalk sweeps cannot close systemic transit and sanitation gaps, and turned PRO's anti-backlash claim by showing that conditional licensing creates a permanent political veto for opponents rather than preventing neighborhood rollbacks.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A wins by effectively framing the proposition's required "plans" as bureaucratic friction that disproportionately harms independent venues and empowers NIMBY opponents. A's argument that transit and sanitation are city-scale issues best solved by municipal services (funded by existing nightlife tax revenue and BIDs) rather than venue-by-venue compliance theater was highly persuasive and realistically grounded in urban planning. Side B's claim that these plans are "low-cost" and "proactive" failed to adequately answer A's sharp point that any bespoke licensing requirement creates a subjective veto point for neighborhood opposition, whereas objective municipal enforcement is harder to politicize.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal to B's claim that "proactive plans disarm NIMBYs," pointing out that requiring a subjective mitigation plan actually hands opponents a checklist to demand more paperwork and block approval, whereas objective municipal enforcement and shared corridor services prevent backlash without creating a permanent licensing veto.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly without fabricated evidence, but B built a more disciplined and adaptive case. B correctly anchored on the proposition's "some venues" scoping and offered a concrete scaled-duty mechanism (shared vans, staggered exits, proportional cleanup) that directly answered A's core "corporate monopoly" objection, and it consistently returned to a clear decision metric—durability versus the backlash-and-rollback cycle. A's opening was rhetorically strong and raised real equity concerns, but subsequent rebuttals mostly reasserted "devastatingly expensive" and "abdication of duty" without engaging B's scaling mechanism or explaining why proportional steps remain unaffordable. Under pressure questioning, B's answers (A1/A2) were squarely responsive, while A's answer to Q2 ("the solution is better governance") sidestepped the specific challenge of what happens when public budgets or political will fail, leaving a live hole in CON's public-funding model. Closings mostly crystallized existing clash, but B's closing tied directly back to the unresolved budget-failure gap, while A's closing repeated framing without closing that gap. This makes B's route to victory more complete and less exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Questions (B3.1/B3.2) and A4.2's answer: B forced CON to explain who covers incremental late-night costs if public budgets or political will falter, and A's reply ("better governance," reinvested taxes) never actually answered the failure-mode scenario, leaving B's durability argument standing as the more resilient case.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a clearer causal mechanism: pairing extended hours with scaled, venue-specific safeguards prevents the overload-backlash-ban cycle that actually destroys independent nightlife. CON's public-funding alternative remained speculative and failed to explain why scaled duties were infeasible rather than merely difficult, leaving B's durability frame as the cleaner path to the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal that unconditional late hours trigger neighborhood backlash and blanket early closes that kill independent venues first, while scaled duty requirements preserve them—directly neutralizing CON's central corporate-capture objection.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully defending the feasibility of scaled mitigation plans for small venues and presenting a more realistic causal story about the political durability of nightlife. Side B effectively turned Side A's "underground" and "backlash" arguments by showing that unconditional late hours trigger the very NIMBY rollbacks that kill independent venues first. Side A's reliance on general tax revenues to fund localized late-night spikes was less convincing than Side B's targeted, conditional approach, and Side A struggled to explain how uniform regulations could manage the localized externalities of specific venues getting extended hours.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's defense of "scaled duty" across Rebuttals 1 and 2, which dismantled Side A's core "corporate monopoly" claim by showing that small venues can implement proportional, affordable mitigations (like shared vans and basic soundproofing) to survive politically without needing corporate bankrolls.
