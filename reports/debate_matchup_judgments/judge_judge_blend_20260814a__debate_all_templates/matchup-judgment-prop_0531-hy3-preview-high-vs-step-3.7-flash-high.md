### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): CON (B) built a stronger path to victory through the full exchange. The sequencing argument—that shared civic accounts only earn legitimacy after distinct ethnic narratives are formally validated—was original, causally plausible, and PRO never fully neutralized it. The Iraq 2003 example was CON's most powerful piece of evidence and it held up through every round: when the state framed Saddam's rule as a generic national tragedy without centering the Anfal campaign or Shia suppression, marginalized groups read the shared frame as majoritarian erasure, dooming peacebuilding. PRO's response was essentially "that wasn't a true inclusive shared account," which is a reasonable clarification but leaves the systemic risk CON identified—that even well-intentioned shared accounts can subordinate specific harms—unanswered. CON's reinterpretation of the TRC was the most decisive rebuttal exchange: PRO used the TRC as a flagship example, and CON successfully reversed ownership by showing that TRC's success came FROM validating group-specific testimonies first, not from imposing a top-down shared narrative. PRO dismissed these testimonies as merely "procedural steps," which felt dismissive of what was actually foundational to the TRC process. PRO made a real and important contribution with the definitional clarification—the motion rejects "fully separate competing" narratives, not all acknowledgment of specific harms—and this kept PRO in the debate. But CON answered PRO's pressure questions with adequate labeled A1/A2 responses and the Rwanda addition in Rebuttal 2 was a narrow, appropriate example that directly answered a live point. PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions were weaker, particularly A1, which characterized TRC grassroots memorials as "transitional mechanisms" without engaging the deeper point about whether defunding them would have undermined reconciliation. CON's consistent framing—that prioritizing the shared account before distinct harms are validated reproduces the majoritarian erasure that delegitimizes the shared project—left a live route to victory that PRO's definitional work partially but not fully closed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 reinterpretation of the South African TRC: PRO's flagship example was turned against it by arguing that TRC reconciliation worked precisely because it first centered thousands of group-specific racial and community testimonies before building a shared national story—validating that the shared narrative felt like collective agreement rather than coercive imposition. PRO responded by calling those testimonies merely "procedural steps," but CON's framing that they were the foundation rather than the scaffolding was more persuasive and better matched the historical record. This exchange materially damaged PRO's strongest example and shifted ownership of it to CON for the remainder of the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A built the stronger case across the full exchange for three principal reasons. First, A consistently held the "contextualization vs. erasure" distinction against B's straw man framing, and B never definitively demolished it—B's most pointed response ("you cannot contextualize a genocide, only document it") is rhetorically memorable but analytically weak, since documentation of targeted intent is itself a form of contextualization. Second, A's evidence base was more specific and grounded throughout: Northern Ireland murals, Bosnia revisionism, Sierra Leone, Rwanda Gacaca, and Peru were all plausible and internally consistent, whereas B's most concrete counter-example—that Northern Ireland's shared civic memorials erased anti-Catholic state violence—was vague, contested by A's own earlier use of the same case, and never substantiated beyond assertion. Third, A landed a sharp evidentiary challenge in Q2 (name a single shared memorial that denied survivors dignity), and B's answer was the weakest moment in the debate. B's best structural move—distinguishing victim-controlled separate memorials (Bosniak/Srebrenica) from aggressor-controlled ones (Bosnian Serb)—actually conceded A's core point: the real question is who controls the design process, which plays directly into A's argument for cross-community negotiated civic projects over unilateral ethnic sites. B's asymmetry and elite-capture concerns were genuine live points but were answered with plausible structural counterexamples (cross-community negotiation as guardrail) that B never squarely refuted. The proposition's case remained standing at the close; B offered a framework of "separate truth first," but could not demonstrate that shared frameworks systematically fail on those terms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q2 pressure question—asking B to name a single post-conflict shared memorial that denied survivors dignity by refusing to honor their specific loss—combined with B's vague and contested Northern Ireland response in Rebuttal 2, was the most decisive exchange. It exposed that B's central fear (shared accounts erase specific trauma) was structurally asserted rather than evidenced, while A had multiple concrete examples of separate memorials producing revisionism and violence. This asymmetry in evidentiary grounding was the clearest reason A won.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully demonstrating that shared civic accounts do not require the erasure of specific trauma, using strong examples (Peru, Rwanda) to show how cross-community negotiation prevents elite capture. Side A also decisively proved that preserving 'competing' narratives inherently protects aggressor revisionism, a flaw Side B failed to resolve.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized Side B's core offense by using the Peru and Rwanda examples to prove shared projects have built-in guardrails against elite capture and top-down imposition.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won because it successfully demonstrated that shared civic memorials can honor specific trauma without creating false equivalence, provided they are built through participatory cross-community processes (Peru, phased Rwanda Gacaca). Side A (CON) failed to fully resolve the problem that preserving fully separate competing ethnic narratives necessarily includes perpetrator-group memorials that enshrine revisionism, as seen in Bosnian Serb sites. While CON's moral intuition about asymmetric atrocities was powerful, its characterization of shared accounts as inherent dilution remained largely assertive and did not adequately answer PRO's concrete examples of contextualization without erasure. PRO's pressure questions exposed a real burden on CON regarding perpetrator-controlled separate memorials, and PRO's closing effectively crystallized the circular trap of demanding separate acknowledgment before shared reconciliation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 answer to Side A's pressure question about elite capture, using Peru's co-designed memorials and Rwanda's phased Gacaca-to-shared process to show structural guardrails exist in civic projects but not in separate ethnic ones, while exposing that CON offered no comparable mechanism to prevent perpetrator groups from hijacking separate narratives.
