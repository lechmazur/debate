### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): A built a durable structural argument early ("any uniform profit-maximizing price must lie between the highest and lowest personalized quotes—banning personalization erases the low quote, not the high one") and defended it consistently across every round, reframing B's "vulnerable/captive" examples as local monopoly problems that a pricing ban cannot fix. B's case was rhetorically strong and emotionally resonant (captivity, invisibility, upward wealth transfer) but rested on the unproven assumption that algorithmic inelasticity signals equal affluence rather than time-poverty or lack of choice, and B never directly rebutted A's high-low quote mechanism. When pressed, B's attempt to supply a workable legal test (personal characteristics vs. transactional context) was cleanly exposed by A as self-contradictory, since B's own rule banned "profiles inferred from past behavior" while simultaneously exempting cart-abandonment discounts, which are past behavior. B's closing restated the vulnerability narrative without repairing this collapse, leaving A's core comparative claim live and unanswered. Both sides followed the Q&A format correctly, but A's answers and rebuttals did more actual argumentative work, making A's path to victory clearer and more resilient.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 showing that B's proposed personal-characteristics-vs-transactional-context legal test self-destructs, since cart-abandonment discounts (which B wants to protect) are themselves "past behavior," the exact category B's rule bans.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that a categorical ban also removes beneficial willingness-based discounts that can expand access. Side B plausibly identified captive vulnerable consumers, but never established that exploitation dominates the opposite distributional effect, and its proposed carveout for cart abandonment narrowed the motion and contradicted its own ban on profiles inferred from past behavior. Side A overstated that uniform pricing preserves every high quote, but Side B failed to exploit that weakness. Side B also incurred a modest cost from its clipped opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.3 was decisive: Side B banned pricing based on profiles inferred from past behavior while permitting cart abandonment, itself past behavior used to infer willingness to pay. That exposed both a line-drawing problem and a retreat from the proposition as written.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A built a significantly stronger and more economically rigorous case. A decisively dismantled Side B's core examples of vulnerability by distinguishing between structural/geographic monopolies (the rural shopper with one store) and algorithmic pricing. A's central economic argument—that a uniform profit-maximizing price eliminates the low personalized quotes for price-sensitive shoppers without lowering the high quotes for captive shoppers—was never successfully answered by B. Furthermore, A exploited B's proposed legal test and enforcement mechanisms effectively, proving that B's ban would either be unenforceable or require the exact surveillance apparatus B claimed to oppose.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's proposed legal test (pointing out that cart abandonment is itself past behavior) and reinforced the devastating economic reality that banning personalized pricing only deletes the low quote, leaving the captive shopper's high monopoly price intact.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B built a clearer, more enforceable line (transparent/opt-in group discounts vs. secret individualized markups) and pressed it relentlessly with the "coupon test" — if the goal is access, post a public discount; secrecy only serves to extract surplus from those who can't walk away. A never squarely answered why secrecy is necessary rather than a public coupon, repeatedly restating that coupons are "inefficient" without engaging the distinction. B also exposed a real internal tension in A's case: A claimed existing anti-discrimination law already handles proxy discrimination, while simultaneously arguing that willingness and demand signals are indistinguishable to auditors — but if they're indistinguishable, a shopper can never prove disparate treatment, gutting the "existing law already covers it" defense. A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.2) never resolved this contradiction, just reasserted it. B's paired-testing enforcement mechanism (output-based, same time/inventory, vary only personal traits) was a concrete answer to A's "auditors can't distinguish" pressure point, while A's rebuttal to that mechanism was just a complaint about chilling effects rather than a rebuttal of the method. B's closing crystallized these unresolved points sharply; A's closing was largely a recap that didn't re-engage the coupon test or the legal-gap contradiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to A's own pressure question (B4.1, "A1") introducing paired output-testing as an enforceable line, combined with B4.4 exposing the contradiction between A's "existing law covers it" and "signals are indistinguishable" claims — a tension A never resolved in Rebuttal 2 or Closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by clearly distinguishing permitted scarcity pricing and transparent discounts from secret individualized markups, then explaining why the latter undermine comparison shopping and conceal proxy discrimination. Side A had a plausible welfare case about targeted discounts, but repeatedly conflated aggregate dynamic pricing and public loyalty discounts with the narrower practice in the motion. Its reliance on existing discrimination law was also weakened by its own claim that legitimate demand signals and willingness signals are indistinguishable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s paired-testing answer was decisive: simultaneous price differences with time, inventory, and product held constant provide a workable way to identify individualized willingness pricing. B then turned A’s claimed enforcement ambiguity against A’s assertion that existing law already solves proxy discrimination.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B dominated the debate by introducing the "coupon test," which effectively neutralized Side A's primary efficiency argument. Side B also set a brilliant logical trap regarding signal overlap: if demand and willingness signals are truly indistinguishable to auditors (as Side A claimed to argue against the ban's feasibility), then shoppers can never prove proxy discrimination under existing laws, thereby proving the necessity of a per se ban. Side A repeatedly fell back on generic Econ 101 price discrimination theory without adequately addressing the specific harms of secret, algorithmic extraction from inelastic demand.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it trapped Side A in a contradiction: Side A argued that overlapping signals made enforcement of a ban impossible, but Side B pointed out that this exact overlap makes it impossible for shoppers to prove proxy discrimination, thus destroying Side A's reliance on existing anti-discrimination law and proving the need for the ban.
