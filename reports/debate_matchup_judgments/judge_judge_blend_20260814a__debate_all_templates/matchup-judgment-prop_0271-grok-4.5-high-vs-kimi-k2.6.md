### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued with skill and no format violations, but PRO better survived the central technical clash. CON's strongest structural move was claiming safety-demotion and engagement-ranking are inseparable, so parental toggles would blind protective systems, and that mandatory platform design should replace parental levers entirely. PRO's pressure question forced CON to specify which shared pipeline was at risk, and PRO's answer—that chronological feeds, hash-matching, and report-driven classifiers already run independently of personalization engines on major platforms—was concrete and plausible, while CON's reply mostly reasserted "shared models" without new grounding. PRO also successfully reframed the mandatory-design-vs-parental-power dichotomy as complementary rather than exclusive ("mandatory floors set the floor, family delay sets the threshold"), which CON never directly rebutted in the final rebuttal or closing, leaving CON's "replace parental power with corporate mandates" thesis structurally undercut. CON's manufactured-consent and liability-shift arguments were rhetorically sharp and landed some real points (platforms burying non-algorithmic options to coerce "choice"), but they attack implementation risk rather than squarely defeating the motion's core claim that parents deserve the stronger lever. Overall PRO gave the cleaner, more directly responsive path through the debate, though CON's rhetoric and liability framing kept this competitive rather than lopsided.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to Pressure Question Q1 (A1 in Rebuttal 2), showing that safety demotion already operates on chronological/search views via classifiers and hash lists without full personalization, directly undercut CON's inseparability claim and was never adequately re-grounded by CON afterward.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that parental control over engagement personalization can coexist with platform-wide safety standards and moderation. Side A offered a forceful accountability frame, but its central claims were under-supported: shared data or code does not establish that disabling personalized recommendations must disable safety filtering, and the proposition itself creates no liability waiver. B also answered circumvention proportionately and showed that imperfect controls can still provide meaningful leverage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s distinction between engagement ranking and safety enforcement was decisive. It explained that chronological or curated feeds can retain classifiers, age rules, spam controls, and content demotion while removing personalized cascades. Side A largely repeated that shared infrastructure makes separation impossible without demonstrating why separate functions cannot use shared inputs.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a very tight debate by successfully disaggregating the 'engagement engine' from basic safety moderation. While Side A powerfully argued that parental toggles are a liability shift and that safety algorithms rely on engagement data, Side B logically demonstrated that turning off the engagement engine removes the very escalation cascades Side A feared, making static moderation (hash lists, classifiers) sufficient for chronological feeds. Side B's framing that parental controls complement rather than replace platform safety baselines cleanly neutralized Side A's main offensive push.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 breakdown of how static safety demotion (hash lists, fixed classifiers) functions independently of personalization models, effectively dismantling Side A's technical inseparability claim.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): B built a sharper, more precise distinction between macro-level parental authority (device access, uninstall) and micro-level authority over the recommendation mechanism itself, and used this to repeatedly puncture A's central claim that existing blunt tools already suffice. B's pressure question forced A to defend why granular controls would cause migration when total uninstall (a strictly larger intervention) would not, and A's answer ("degraded product drives abandonment") never squarely resolved that asymmetry. B also offered a more coherent account of the market-failure objection: platforms bury granular controls because engagement monetization directly conflicts with friction, and diffuse/delayed harms mute consumer pressure—A's "market would surface demand" argument never engaged this incentive-misalignment point in rebuttal 2, instead repeating that most families don't want micromanagement. A's case leaned heavily on circular reasoning (authorizing use time implicitly authorizes the mechanism) and suffered an execution cost from a clipped rebuttal turn. B's memorable framing (scalpel vs axe, "burn down the arcade," hostage metaphor) also made the stakes easier to grasp without sacrificing substance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to A's market-demand question (A2 in Rebuttal 2), explaining that buried tools reflect incentive-driven market calculation rather than genuine lack of demand, directly undercut A's strongest structural argument and went largely unanswered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by clearly distinguishing blunt control over device access from granular control over recommendation architecture. Side A repeatedly asserted that permitting an app authorizes its whole design, but never justified why an all-or-nothing uninstall is preferable to an optional safety control. Its migration, overblocking, and market-demand claims remained speculative and did not outweigh B's incentive-mismatch analysis. Side A's clipped first rebuttal was also a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first pressure question exposed the central contradiction: if uninstalling the app is acceptable, disabling recommendations should not uniquely cause harmful migration. Side A answered that the partial restriction degrades the experience, but never established why this would cause more evasion than a total ban. B's scalpel-versus-axe framing therefore remained decisive.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by decisively refuting Side A's argument that existing "macro" tools (like uninstalling or ending a session) are sufficient. Side B successfully framed these blunt tools as 'burning down the arcade' rather than managing the risk, and effectively dismantled Side A's free-market argument by pointing out that platforms monetize engagement and hide granular controls by calculation, not due to lack of parental demand.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 explanation of why the market fails to deliver granular controls (monetization of engagement and network effects) cleanly neutralized Side A's strongest defense of the status quo.
