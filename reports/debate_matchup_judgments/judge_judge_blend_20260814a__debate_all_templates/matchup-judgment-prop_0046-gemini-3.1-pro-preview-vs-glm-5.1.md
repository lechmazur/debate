### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): CON built a structurally superior case around a single live wedge that PRO never fully neutralized: prohibition guarantees wealthy-only enhancement (via offshore access PRO explicitly conceded) while producing zero regulatory protections, whereas the caste-system fear motivating PRO's case materializes under prohibition as readily as under legalization. PRO's best late move—that anti-discrimination law is powerless against enhanced observable performance—was sharp, but CON had already pre-empted it: that same performance pressure exists under prohibition too, because the wealthy still enhance and still compete in the same labor market. PRO's distinction between "isolated offshore anomalies" and "domestic neighborhood clinics" was their most original counterpoint, and it has some intuitive force, but CON answered cleanly: the anomalies still occupy the same competitive positions, and prohibition offers zero structural response (no formularies, no privacy protections, no equitable access pathway). PRO's coercion and public-health arguments were reasonably developed, but the subsidized-access attack was undermined by CON's grounded observation that healthcare budgets draw lines constantly. Throughout, CON consistently turned PRO's own evidence—infrastructure requirements, competitive pressure, inequality risks—into reasons favoring regulation rather than bans, which is exactly the comparative work that wins debates. PRO performed competently but left its core structural vulnerability unaddressed across multiple rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's crystallization in B4.5–B4.7 (Rebuttal 2): PRO had already conceded wealthy individuals would enhance offshore regardless, so CON drove home that a genetic caste system emerges under both regimes—the only operative question is whether non-wealthy citizens get safe, regulated access or are locked out entirely. This turned PRO's flagship inequality argument into an affirmative reason to favor regulation over prohibition, and PRO's closing never closed that loop.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A built a stronger case by consistently showing that observable performance advantages from enhancement render anti-discrimination laws ineffective and create unavoidable coercive pressure for the majority, while prohibition limits widespread normalization. B's regulatory middle ground failed to neutralize these practical dynamics despite good analogies, leaving A's externality and arms-race arguments more decisive after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The A closing exchange on anti-discrimination laws and observable performance gaps (hiring the un-fatigued "bio-machine" without needing genetic disclosure), which exposed a key weakness in B's protections claim.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by decisively collapsing Side A's primary safeguard: anti-discrimination law. When CON argued that genetic privacy and anti-discrimination statutes would protect the unenhanced, PRO sharply countered that employers hire based on observable performance, not genetic records. If enhancements work, the performance gap is visible and legally actionable as merit, rendering anti-discrimination laws practically powerless against coerced participation. PRO also effectively distinguished between isolated offshore enhancements and mass commercialization, convincingly arguing that only the latter shifts societal baselines and creates a true caste system. While CON offered a competent harm-reduction and regulatory framework, they never fully resolved how regulation prevents an open-ended biological arms race or protects workers from performance-based coercion. PRO's clearer causal chain, sharper pressure responses, and more decisive clash resolution earn the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 and Closing exchange on anti-discrimination law. CON claimed genetic privacy and anti-discrimination statutes would protect the unenhanced, but PRO devastatingly noted that employers don't need genetic records to hire the candidate who objectively works longer hours or fits aesthetic standards better. By shifting the focus from genetic status to observable performance, PRO exposed a fatal practical gap in CON's regulatory model and crystallized why legalization inevitably coerces the unenhanced into a biological arms race.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): CON (Side B) wins a competitive but clear debate on the weight of three decisive exchanges. First, the somatic/germline distinction is CON's strongest scientific claim: PRO's "individual consent cannot authorize risks to descendants" [A1.6] depends on heritable modification, but somatic editing does not alter germline cells. PRO's counter—viral vector off-target integration—is speculative and, as CON correctly notes, is precisely an argument for regulating specific delivery mechanisms rather than a blanket ban. PRO never fully escaped this. Second, PRO's key distinguishing argument—that surgery is "temporary, reversible, and visible" [A2.4]—was directly falsified by CON's examples of permanent, irreversible, seamlessly invisible cosmetic procedures (jaw reconstruction, rhinoplasty, limb-lengthening) [B2.4]. This forced PRO to pivot late to "observability" as the real distinction, a move that carried some force but arrived too late to anchor the whole case. Third, CON's closing turn [B5.1–B5.2] on that very observability argument was sharp: if genetic edits are truly undetectable, prohibition is as unenforceable as PRO's "unauditable" regulatory framework—and regulators govern supply chains, not individuals, successfully doing so for pharmaceuticals and hormone therapies already. PRO's live threads—the collective-consequences principle and slippery slope to germline editing—remain genuinely interesting but were insufficiently grounded after CON showed society maintains adult-only consent boundaries for other extreme permanent modifications. CON's "prohibition guarantees elite offshore monopoly while regulation drives down costs" argument also held up better than PRO's "regulation accelerates stratification," which relied on general market pessimism without addressing CON's cosmetic-dentistry cost-reduction example. PRO argued well and maintained philosophical coherence, but CON answered the most critical empirical pillar (heritable risk) and made the decisive clash easier to resolve.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.4 directly falsified PRO's claim that surgery is "temporary, reversible, and visible"—the pivot on which PRO's entire surgery-versus-genetics distinction rested—by naming specific permanently irreversible and visually seamless cosmetic procedures. This forced PRO off their original framing and onto the late "observability" pivot, which CON then turned in closing by pointing out that prohibition cannot police what it cannot detect, while supply-chain regulation already governs internally invisible medical interventions every day.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins because it established genetic enhancement's unique features (invisibility, pleiotropic cascades, rapid baseline shifts) as defeating any regulatory regime, while individual consent cannot contain collective social coercion or heritable risks. Side B's cosmetic surgery analogy and autonomy appeals were undercut by A's direct comparisons on observability and scalability, and B never explained how regulation would handle undetectable procedures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.2) directly answering B's Q1 on method differences, showing why surgery oversight fails for concealable, scalable genetic edits.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A wins by dismantling Side B's key distinctions and landing a decisive logical turn. A correctly exposes B's claim that cosmetic surgery is "temporary and reversible" as factually weak, preserving the analogy to existing aesthetic practices. A also maintains a firmer grasp on the somatic vs. germline distinction, framing biological risks as manageable via clinical trials rather than grounds for a total ban. Most importantly, A's closing turns B's "concealability" pivot against itself: if edits are truly undetectable, prohibition is an unenforceable fantasy. A consistently contextualizes B's fears within existing regulatory and liberal frameworks, providing a cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing response to B's concealability argument, turning B's premise into proof that prohibition is unenforceable while clarifying that regulation targets the observable supply chain, not individual bodies.
