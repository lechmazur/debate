### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): CON won by turning PRO's own concession into the debate's decisive clash. The moment PRO admitted that a ban still requires permit exceptions for essential trips, CON crystallized a clean comparative: both sides need a sorting mechanism, so the real question is whether a market toll or a permit office is more equitable. CON's answer—"the toll booth never interrogates motive; the permit office does, and it is rarely friendly to the applicant lacking a lawyer"—was well-grounded, directly responsive, and PRO never neutralized it. PRO's closing simply reasserted that permit criteria already exist and are narrow, which is precisely what CON was contesting. PRO's moral framing ("pricing invoices injustice and calls it revenue") was rhetorically strong and the "trim is not eradicate" point in Rebuttal 2 was genuinely sharp, but these arguments stayed at the level of aspiration without answering the gatekeeping problem. CON's case progressed across rounds—each rebuttal tightened the permit-bureaucracy turn—while PRO's case was more static, repeating the disease/symptoms framing with diminishing returns. CON's pressure questions were also sharper and more targeted than PRO's, and CON's closing crystallized the comparative cleanly without introducing new material. The margin is competitive because PRO's equity framing is intuitive and the "auctioning public safety" line has real force, but the core clash was decided by CON's unmet permit-bureaucracy argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turn on the surveillance/bureaucracy question: after PRO raised pricing's billing infrastructure as a cost, CON inverted it by pointing out that automated cameras require minimal human discretion while a ban's permit office adjudicates individual cases—making it more arbitrary, more biased, and harder on those without lawyers. PRO never answered this in the closing, leaving CON's comparative standing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side A won by making the practical comparison clearer: both systems need exceptions, but pricing preserves access, generates transit revenue, and can tighten over time, while a ban depends on discretionary permit gatekeeping and risks premature backlash. Side B had the stronger moral rhetoric about not auctioning public safety, but it leaned too heavily on “eradication” while conceding exceptions and did not sufficiently answer the revenue, transition, and access burdens.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s best exchange was turning B’s permit concession: if essential trips remain, the choice is not pure ban versus pricing, but toll-based access with revenue versus bureaucratic gatekeeping without the same funding stream.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B won by decisively winning the comparative analysis of how both policies function in practice. While Side A had exceptional rhetoric framing pricing as a 'wealth-sorting' injustice, Side B successfully turned this by showing that Side A's alternative—a permit system—replaces a mechanical toll with an arbitrary human gatekeeper. Side B also neutralized Side A's transit arguments by demonstrating that pricing provides the actual revenue to fund transit improvements, whereas a ban relies on a hypothetical political catalyst.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 expertly contrasted the mechanical, non-judgmental nature of a toll camera with the bureaucratic, motive-interrogating nature of Side A's required permit office.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): CON (Side A) won on the two clashes that most directly tested the motion. First, the equity inversion: "equality of prohibition is not equity of access" [A2.1] was the sharpest rebuttal in the debate and PRO never neutralized it. PRO's "the nurse and surgeon face the same rule" framing was exposed as formal equality masking real inequality in housing costs, permit access, and night-bus coverage. Second, the funding-transition argument: CON's sustained claim that pricing generates the revenue that makes transit alternatives possible, and therefore any workable ban depends on pricing first, was never squarely answered. PRO's closing concession—calling pricing the "construction loan"—actually conceded this point while reframing it, but the motion says "ban… rather than rely on congestion pricing," and if pricing must precede the ban, the motion's framing fails on its own terms. PRO's permit-versus-price-exemption distinction (permits are necessity-based, price exemptions are politically negotiable) was genuinely interesting and kept the debate competitive, but it only drew a partial answer to the lobbying-corruption pressure CON applied and didn't resolve the deeper funding-sequencing clash. PRO also suffered two format clippings (opening and closing), with the closing visibly repeating itself, which further reduced its ability to crystallize the final comparison. CON's closing efficiently exploited PRO's "construction loan" concession to show that the motion demands skipping a necessary step. The margin is clear but not dominant because PRO's street-redesign framing and permit-versus-price distinction were real argumentative contributions that gave it genuine ground throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's "equality of prohibition is not equity of access" [A2.1] paired with "pricing funds mobility vouchers, night buses, and disability subsidies—targeted support a wall cannot provide" [A4.4]. PRO's universality claim was PRO's strongest equity move, and CON turned it by showing that identical formal rules produce unequal real outcomes when housing costs, permit lobbying capacity, and transit availability vary by income. PRO's response—that permits are auditable on grounds everyone accepts—did not engage the access-inequality mechanism and left the rebuttal standing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate. Side B had a strong framing that bans change the street default while pricing preserves a wealthy car privilege, but Side A did more damage on implementation: equality of prohibition is not equity of access, exemptions turn the ban into permit rationing, and congestion pricing both reduces traffic and funds the transit alternatives a ban would need. Side B never fully solved the funding and access burdens, and its closing concession that pricing is a construction loan let Side A crystallize the proposition as skipping the foundation for the desired endpoint.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's attack on Side B's permit carve-outs was the decisive exchange: once Side B conceded medical, accessibility, and commercial exceptions, Side A reframed the ban as opaque bureaucratic rationing rather than true car deletion, while pricing remained flexible, transparent, and revenue-generating.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won by consistently driving home the practical mechanisms of both policies, particularly the argument that congestion pricing provides the essential revenue needed to fund public transit. Side A made a fatal strategic error in the closing by conceding this funding point and framing pricing as a "construction loan," which Side B easily weaponized to show that implementing a ban rather than pricing would destroy the transition's foundation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing correctly identifying that Side A's concession on the "construction loan" meant a ban skips the necessary foundation for urban transit.
