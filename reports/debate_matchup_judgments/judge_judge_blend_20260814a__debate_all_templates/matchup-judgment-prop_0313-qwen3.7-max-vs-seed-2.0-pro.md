### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A wins a competitive but real margin. The decisive factor is the motion's threshold: it asks what governing bodies should do *when* heat and travel risks materially threaten welfare. A consistently framed human physiology as the immovable object at that threshold, and the biological-ceiling argument (cumulative load cannot be rearranged away when expansion itself is the problem) remained live through the close. A's Q1 pressure question was genuinely damaging: it forced B to either concede that mandated rest windows and travel caps implicitly reduce scheduling intensity (and thus concede the motion) or argue that clever rearrangement can eliminate material risk without cutting any matches. B's Rebuttal 2 answer—group regional fixtures, stagger opening rounds—was a real response, but it addressed below-threshold scheduling, not the scenario where the baseline volume itself is unsafe. B's strongest line was the perverse-incentive/cover-up argument, which was original and consistently maintained. A's reply ("federations already hide risk; independent bodies strip the financial veto") is plausible but leaves a gap: A never explains how independent medical oversight survives the governing bodies that fund and authorize it. That gap prevents A from a dominant win. Still, B never defined when cuts would ever be appropriate, and the motion's "material threat" framing already incorporates the threshold B wants—leaving B's mitigation-first case addressing a slightly different question than the one on the table. A's framing of the commercial model as treating athletes as depreciating assets and the "no amount of revenue justifies consuming its players" close were rhetorically sharper. Overall, A made the decisive clash cleaner and answered pressure more directly, earning a clear but competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1 pressure question ("If you mandate rest windows and cap travel, aren't you inherently reducing scheduling intensity and blocking tournament expansion?") was the most decisive exchange. It exposed the self-undermining quality of B's mitigation-first position: structural safeguards that actually protect athletes necessarily reduce total match volume, making B's promised "alternative to cuts" functionally indistinguishable from the cuts the motion requires. B's response—that staggering and regional grouping can absorb rest days without cancelling matches—was coherent but only addressed sub-threshold scheduling problems, not the scenario where aggregate physiological load is itself the hazard.

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO built the stronger path to victory by rooting the debate in physiological non-negotiability and exposing that CON’s mitigation alternatives either failed to resolve cumulative overload or tacitly required reducing match inventory anyway. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A built the stronger cumulative case. The decisive move came in Rebuttal 2, where PRO introduced the "marginal revenue" framing: core tournament revenue (which fully funds safety infrastructure) stays intact; only the final dangerous add-ons are cut. This directly neutralised CON's central argument that broadcast revenue funds athlete protection, because the motion only applies to that last, dangerous increment added after safety budgets are already secured. CON's response—that "marginal growth funds the next generation of sports science"—was too abstract and speculative to match the precision of PRO's framing. CON's strongest live arguments were (1) the weaponizable threshold problem and (2) collective bargaining consent. PRO answered (1) by noting independent tournament doctors already define material risk and only need their judgment to override the broadcast department—a clean institutional fix. On (2), PRO's coercion-versus-consent distinction was never fully rebutted; CON asserted that unions negotiate for expansion but never answered why individual athletes cannot unilaterally opt out without career consequences. CON's repeated "paternalistic outsider" framing was rhetorically active but never engaged with PRO's limiting principle: we are not deciding athletes cannot compete, only that one avoidable marginal slot should not proceed. CON also ended with a duplicated sentence in its closing, a small execution cost. PRO's closing crystallised the existing clash cleanly and memorably without introducing new material. CON's closing largely repeated Rebuttal 2 points without sharpening the comparative weighting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 "marginal revenue" answer to CON's funding pressure question. By distinguishing core tournament revenue (untouched, funds all safety infrastructure) from the final dangerous increment added solely for extra broadcast slots, PRO collapsed CON's all-or-nothing funding dichotomy and left CON's core argument without a live route to victory.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate tied to the motion’s threshold: when heat and travel risks materially threaten welfare, incremental expansion or intensity should give way. Side A’s best case was that broadcast revenue funds safety and athletes consent to tradeoffs, but B effectively answered that this cannot justify deliberately creating avoidable hazards for marginal revenue, and that consent is constrained by career pressure. B’s doctor-threshold and “not shutting down the core tournament” responses also blunted A’s vagueness and defunding objections, though B’s unsupported 10-15% framing was somewhat overprecise.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal was most decisive: it reframed A’s mitigation argument as creating a hazard and then spending a fraction of the proceeds on band-aids, which directly weakened the central CON route that revenue expansion is itself athlete protection.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won by piercing the central false dichotomy in B's funding defense. A showed that the proposition targets only marginal broadcast add-ons—extra rounds or dangerous kickoff times added after baseline safety budgets are already secured—so cutting them does not defund core medical infrastructure. A also directly answered B's pressure questions by proposing independent tournament doctors as the threshold deciders and by demonstrating that athletes' structural inability to refuse risky schedules without career penalty undermines claims of free consent. B never recovered from this narrowing; B kept asserting that all marginal revenue funds safety but could not explain why the specific incremental hazards were necessary for that funding rather than pure profit extraction. A's framing of exploitation versus safety, combined with cleaner progression across rounds, provided the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7) was decisive: A answered B's pressure questions directly with the independent-doctor threshold mechanism and the marginal-versus-core revenue distinction, showing that cutting avoidable scheduling risks leaves 100% of baseline safety funding intact while eliminating only the last exploitative dollars. This collapsed B's claim that welfare cuts must starve sports science and redefined the entire clash around a concrete, winnable tradeoff.
