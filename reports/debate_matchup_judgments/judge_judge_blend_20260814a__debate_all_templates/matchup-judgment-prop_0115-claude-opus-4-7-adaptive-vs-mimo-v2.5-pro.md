### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A wins because the motion only requires BNPL to be regulated much more like credit cards than checkout features, not copied identically from every credit-card statute. A made the stronger functional case that BNPL creates credit-like harms requiring dispute rights, fee transparency, reporting/visibility, and repayment checks, and effectively turned B's concessions that many of those protections are needed. B's bespoke-regulation framing was strong and made the debate competitive, but it often depended on treating any adaptation as a concession against the motion. A answered the pressure questions more directly, while B largely dodged A's request to name protections it would exclude and suffered minor execution costs from clipped turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal was the decisive exchange: it reframed B's “bespoke” package as mostly the credit-card protection set with the label removed, while showing that dispute rights and stacking visibility matter independently of revolving balances.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A won by consistently mapping BNPL's functional credit risks (stacking, disputes) onto the credit-card framework with minimal trigger adaptations, directly resolving pressure questions on identical rules like FCBA dispute mechanics; Side B conceded needed protections but relied on indivisibility claims and incomplete clipped speeches that failed to show why bespoke labeling protects better than established defaults.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers directly establishing that dispute rights transfer via auto-debit triggers without new statutes, undermining B's indivisibility argument and sharpening the default-to-framework comparison.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A won because it collapsed the distinction between bespoke and credit-card regulation, showing that the substantive protections CON endorsed—dispute rights, stacking visibility, fee caps, and proportionate ability-to-repay—are core features of the credit-card framework. Since BNPL is functionally credit and CON conceded it needs credit-style protections, the motion’s comparative framing favors A: the product should fall under the mature credit regime by default rather than be carved out. A also landed sharper pressure questions, exposing that CON could not name a single credit-card protection to exclude. CON further suffered real execution costs when both its Rebuttal 2 and Closing were clipped for exceeding word limits, leaving key arguments unfinished.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Pressure Question Q1 asking Side A to name one credit-card rule to apply identically without adaptation, and Side A’s answer that Fair Credit Billing Act dispute rights transfer cleanly—followed by Side B’s counter that changing statement triggers to installment dates constitutes drafting new regulation rather than applying existing rules. This exchange crystallized the central framework-versus-bespoke clash.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won by making the decisive distinction between regulating BNPL as credit and regulating it like credit cards. Side A showed real consumer risks and won that ordinary checkout treatment is inadequate, but B conceded targeted protections and repeatedly showed that shared features like disclosure, fee limits, dispute rights, and reporting do not prove the whole credit card template fits. B also turned A's installment-loan analogies into evidence for bespoke regulation and kept access and compliance-cost concerns live. A's clipped turns and tendency to collapse baseline credit protections into credit-card regulation weakened its path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal was decisive: it used A's payday, auto, and personal loan analogy to show that many credit products are not regulated like credit cards, then attacked the claim that deferred-payment risks are identical across product structures.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by sharply distinguishing BNPL's installment mechanics from credit cards' revolving structure and demonstrating that transplanting the full credit-card template would raise compliance costs that shrink access for thin-file users while pushing them toward payday loans. A never neutralized the access arithmetic and treated shared protections (bureau reporting, disclosures) as decisive proof the template fits, allowing B to frame the motion as a failed template claim rather than a protections question. B's responses adapted cleanly across rounds; A's later speeches repeated earlier points without fully answering pressure on risk differences or costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) exposing A's floor-vs-template concession and clarifying bureau reporting as non-unique.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): CON wins by isolating the motion's demand for the credit-card template specifically, not merely regulation-as-credit. CON's first rebuttal showed that auto loans, payday loans, and personal installment loans are all credit yet none are regulated like credit cards, neutralizing PRO's "BNPL is credit" framing. CON then demonstrated that the credit card package imports APR machinery, ability-to-repay underwriting, and fixed compliance costs misfit for small, zero-interest, short-duration installments—predictably contracting access and pushing marginal users toward worse alternatives. PRO never fully answered the arithmetic of how that template scales to $60 transactions without pricing out thin-file borrowers, and PRO had three speeches clipped by the word limit, leaving key comparative weighing unseen in the closing stages.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal [B2.1], which pointed out that payday loans, auto loans, and personal installment loans are regulated as credit but not like credit cards. This rebuttal destroyed PRO's central analogy, reframed the debate around template specificity rather than the binary "credit or not" question, and remained live through the final exchange without being fully recovered.
