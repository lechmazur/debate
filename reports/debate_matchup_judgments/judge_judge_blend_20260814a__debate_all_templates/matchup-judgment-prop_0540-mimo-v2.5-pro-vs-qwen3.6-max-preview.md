### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A won primarily because it identified and consistently pressed B's central conflation—B repeatedly argued as though the proposition required open-ended whole-genome sequencing filed in permanent government databases, when the proposition explicitly constrains scope to targeted panels for actionable conditions. A made this identification early [A2.1] and never let B escape it. B never offered a compelling account of why a narrow targeted panel with curated reporting criteria would generate the VUS cascades, permanent blueprints, and infinite replication risks B warned against. On the VUS pressure question, A gave a plausible and consistent answer: variants outside curated pathogenic criteria are not returned to families, consistent with how clinical genomic panels already operate [A4.1]. B's response—that even targeted panels produce hereditary records—is true but also true of biochemical screening that reveals CF carrier status or sickle cell trait, and B never cleanly distinguished the two after A raised this parallel [A2.3, A3.1]. A's child autonomy framing was the sharpest move of the debate: the infant at the center has no consent mechanism regardless of system design, and "protecting future autonomy to live without preventable disability" turns B's own autonomy language [A4.4]. B's adult testing analogy was interesting but arrived late in Rebuttal 2 and went unaddressed mainly because A was clipped; still, newborns are categorically different from adults capable of deliberate choice, limiting that analogy's force. B's strongest live argument—that outreach, not opt-out, is the proper remedy for coverage gaps—is reasonable but speculative about what outreach can actually achieve compared to the well-documented power of defaults. The clipping of two A speeches is a real execution cost, but the core arguments made before each clip were sufficient. B's rhetoric was good ("commodifies their genomes") but relied more on repetition than on directly answering A's targeted-panel response.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal identifying B's conflation of targeted genomic panels with whole-genome sequencing [A2.1–A2.3], combined with the parallel to bloodspot archiving already practiced in conventional screening, was the most decisive exchange. B never produced a convincing account of why a narrow targeted panel differs meaningfully from biochemical screening in privacy terms, which undercut B's strongest objections for the remainder of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): B won by making the motion narrower and easier to decide: opt-out for targeted, actionable newborn genomic screening prevents avoidable irreversible harm while preserving refusal. A had a serious privacy and consent case, but too much of it depended on treating the proposal as permanent whole-genome archiving, and A never fully answered B's point that VUS and secondary findings can be excluded by design. A also asserted that existing biochemical screening makes gains marginal without proving outreach would close the coverage gap. B better weighed child health and unequal opt-in failures against manageable governance risks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to the VUS/privacy attack was decisive: targeted panels can report only pathogenic variants for actionable conditions, so A's cascade argument applied more to untargeted sequencing than to the proposition as limited.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A wins by establishing that opt-out for narrowly scoped actionable conditions prevents real preventable harm from coverage gaps that opt-in produces, while successfully reframing opt-out as compensatory education rather than coercion and narrowing data risks via targeted panels. Side B lands effective distinctions on hereditary permanence but leaves the child's unprotected stake and opt-in's documented failures inadequately answered across the exchange. The clash stays focused after pressure rounds, with A adapting more directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal 2 opening with labeled A1/A2 answers to B's VUS and autonomy questions, explaining non-reporting protocols and child's interest in avoiding irreversible harm, which sharpened the consent-vs-harm comparison without widening scope.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): PRO maintained the cleaner comparative case throughout the exchange. The equity argument—that opt-in systematically filters out marginalized and exhausted families, producing a predictable tail of irreversible missed diagnoses—was introduced early, sustained consistently, and never squarely defeated. CON's counter ("well-resourced opt-in achieves high uptake") remained aspirational rather than demonstrated, and PRO correctly identified that aspirational alternatives shift the burden back onto overwhelmed parents, recreating the exact inequity problem. PRO also kept the motion's scope limitation—actionable conditions only—as an effective rhetorical anchor, forcing CON to argue against a narrowly bounded version of the policy. CON's strongest live argument was the infrastructure asymmetry: opt-in expansion requires re-engaging families, opt-out expansion requires only relaxing a bioinformatic filter, meaning opt-out removes the structural brake on scope creep. PRO's response (data minimization protocols, clinical governance) was plausible but not decisive—CON correctly noted that historical panel expansion occurred under exactly those oversight structures. The categorical distinction between genomic and metabolite data (permanent, familial, probabilistic) also had genuine force and PRO's filtering-suppresses-reporting-not-sequencing problem was never fully resolved. However, CON's opening was clipped (a real execution cost leaving its full third argument unseen), and PRO's rhetorical framing—"procedural friction versus preventable harm," "days determine developmental trajectories"—was more memorable and more directly tied to the motion's moral stakes. CON's consent-as-surveillance frame was sharp but relied on a future-state risk while PRO pointed to present, inequitable harms. On balance, PRO's equity argument and child-centered moral weight slightly outperformed CON's technically sophisticated but forward-looking infrastructure concerns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.1 infrastructure asymmetry rebuttal was the sharpest single exchange: distinguishing that expanding an opt-in panel structurally requires re-engaging families while expanding an opt-out panel requires only relaxing a filter—no re-consent, no population brake. PRO's data-minimization response was adequate but not decisive, and this asymmetry remained partially live through closing. It was the most original technical point of the debate, though it was ultimately insufficient to overcome PRO's sustained equity argument.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): B won by keeping the debate centered on the marginal children missed by opt-in friction and weighing irreversible, inequitable health harms against autonomy and data concerns. A had a strong consent and scope-creep case, especially the structural-brake argument, but it leaned too heavily on assuming permanent genomic databases and invisible implementation. B directly answered that with clinical reporting limits, governance, and data minimization, then made the cleaner comparison: opt-out can preserve a decline pathway while avoiding predictable missed treatment windows.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to scope creep was the decisive exchange: expansion is a governance question that would violate the motion if it added non-actionable conditions, and opt-out need not mean storing or reporting the whole genome. That undercut A's central infrastructure claim while B's missed-diagnosis weighing remained live.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side B won by more convincingly establishing genomic screening's unique permanent and familial data risks, the intrinsic value of consent beyond friction, and opt-out's expansion asymmetry, which Side A failed to fully neutralize despite strong equity/harm arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2's direct A1/A2 answers on infrastructure brake removal and consent as non-instrumental, reframing the clash away from A's false binary.
