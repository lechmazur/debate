### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won decisively by distinguishing standardized reporting from standardized valuation or payment. Side A repeatedly predicted homogenization, gaming, and lost cross-subsidies without establishing why a common disclosure schema would dictate business models. B directly answered both pressure questions, showed that platforms could retain bespoke definitions while disclosing them consistently, and demonstrated why opacity especially disadvantages independent creators.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 was decisive: it rejected the assumed universal definition of a view, explained how different values can be reported through shared fields, and turned A's data-advantage concern by showing that opacity already protects majors while standardized disclosure lowers the independent creator's auditing barrier.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won by locking the decisive clash: standardized reporting is not a standardized payout formula. CON repeatedly collapsed the two and never escaped the pressure that platforms already use precise engagement definitions to cut checks. PRO’s indie-songwriter burden and nutrition-label / GAAP framing made the comparison easier to decide; CON’s diversity and major-weaponization risks stayed speculative once platforms already hold the internal data.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R1/R2 distinction (reporting schema ≠ payment model), crystallized by the unanswered Q1 on definitions precise enough to pay but allegedly too arbitrary to disclose.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A's distinction between standardized reporting schemas and standardized payment models completely unraveled Side B's case. Side B repeatedly conflated the two, arguing that standardized reporting would force platforms to optimize for raw volume and destroy cross-subsidies. Side A effectively countered that platforms already use precise internal metrics to calculate payouts, so requiring them to report those exact metrics in a common format does not change how platforms value content. Side A also brilliantly flipped Side B's argument about major labels weaponizing data by pointing out that opacity is currently the majors' moat. Side B's failure to answer Side A's central pressure question—why metrics precise enough to calculate pay are too arbitrary to disclose—sealed the debate for Side A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where the "nutrition label" analogy perfectly clarified the difference between a standardized reporting schema and a standardized unit of value, dismantling Side B's core argument about incompatible formats.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that creators lacking verified data cannot reliably enforce payouts or bargain over future value, while private audits and collective bargaining remain unavailable to many weaker rights holders. Side A drew a sharp distinction between auditability and standardization, but its major harms depended on reading the motion as imposing a universal payout formula or publicly exposing cross-platform benchmarks. Sector-specific reporting standards need not do either. Claims that transparency would eliminate advances, dictate content, or prove fatal to startups were plausible risks but insufficiently established against B's direct accountability benefits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest response was that sector-specific, multidimensional standards need not equate songs, films, and livestreams, while the costly guild fight demonstrated why negotiated disclosure is not an adequate remedy for independent creators. This substantially weakened Side A's standardization straw man and voluntary-bargaining alternative.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won by locking the decisive distinction that auditability is not standardization and showing the motion uniquely adds the latter’s harms—Goodhart deformation, mid-tier downward benchmarking under monopsony buyers, and compliance moats—while every legitimate PRO grievance is already deliverable by contract-specific audits and bargaining. A’s black-box equity case stayed live but never showed why standardization, not mere audit rights, was necessary; A’s “gaming already exists” reply conceded rather than answered Goodhart, and answers on leverage repeatedly restated “data is leverage” without meeting the structural buyer-side point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 and separation of audit rights from mandated standardized metrics, which flipped A’s enforceability claim into the motion’s unique downside.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully distinguishing between auditability (which solves PRO's harms via private contracts) and standardization (which the motion strictly requires). Side B demonstrated that contracts already define their own metrics for audits, rendering a government mandate unnecessary while introducing unique harms like Goodhart's law, downward benchmarking by monopsony buyers, and compliance moats. Side A repeatedly conflated transparency with standardization, and B capitalized on this in the closing to prove that the motion's benefits are achievable without it, while its distinctive features are actively harmful. B's economic reasoning regarding risk-shifting and the long tail was also sharp and largely unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they cleanly separated audit rights from standardized metrics, demonstrating that a negotiated contract defines its own terms for an audit, effectively neutralizing PRO's claim that you cannot audit a black box without a government-mandated baseline.
