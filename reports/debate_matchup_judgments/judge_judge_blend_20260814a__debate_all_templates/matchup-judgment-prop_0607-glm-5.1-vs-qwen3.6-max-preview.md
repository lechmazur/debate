### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B built the stronger through-line across all rounds. The GSOMIA 2019 crisis was the pivotal clash, and Side B won it decisively: the channel nearly died by a single executive order overnight, with no legislative debate and no institutional friction—exactly proving that informality, not formality, is what makes arrangements dangerously fragile. Side A's counter—that Washington's emergency intervention "proved" the U.S. buffer works—was the weakest move in the debate. Depending on last-minute American scrambling after the arrangement has already nearly failed is precisely not a stable security design, and Side B landed this with "you don't have a system—you have a hope." Side B's institutional argument (formal structures require parliamentary majorities to dismantle; informal ones die by executive memo) was sharp, well-grounded, and never squarely neutralized. Side A's rebuttal that legislatures "amplify" backlash rather than contain it was asserted but not demonstrated—no mechanism was given for why legislative deliberation produces worse outcomes than executive impulsiveness. Side B's answers to the pressure questions (pre-negotiated ROE for escalation thresholds; legislative vs. executive termination asymmetry) were direct and structurally sound. Side A's "weaponizes the trust deficit" framing was rhetorically effective but lacked the mechanical specificity to actually rebut the sunk-cost argument. Side A's Rebuttal 2 was notably shorter than allowed, leaving some burdens underargued, and its closing largely recycled earlier framings. Side B's closing was clipped but had already secured the key comparative advantages. The U.S.-as-permanent-buffer alternative was never fully defended as a durable long-term solution, which left Side A's case structurally incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 on the GSOMIA pressure question: the distinction between executive-order termination (informal, no friction) versus legislatively-embedded structures (requiring sustained parliamentary majorities, committee hearings, military lobbying) was the debate's most decisive argumentative move. It directly turned Side A's "weaponization" framing against them—informal arrangements are the ones most easily weaponized precisely because they lack institutional sunk costs. Side A never recovered a clean answer to this structural asymmetry.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A won by using the GSOMIA near-collapse as concrete proof of informal fragility, directly answering pressure questions on escalation rules and legislative barriers with pre-negotiated structures and sunk costs, and showing complementarity from divergent perceptions, giving a clearer path to resilience than B's repeated trust-deficit warnings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2, clarifying sovereign veto in joint commands and why formal processes raise rupture costs versus executive memos.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won because it successfully framed the GSOMIA crisis as proof that informal arrangements are dangerously vulnerable to impulsive rupture, whereas formal, legislatively embedded structures create institutional friction and sunk costs that preserve cooperation through political shocks. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins a clear but competitive debate on the strength of three sustained lines that PRO never fully neutralized. First, CON turned the GSOMIA example more effectively than PRO did: PRO claimed it proved institutional resilience, but CON showed it survived only through U.S. intervention, demonstrating that political will overwhelmed institutional logic even against an active nuclear threat. PRO's Rebuttal 2 pivot—distinguishing shallow GSOMIA from deeper command integration—was a genuine move, but the claim that "deeper = operationally unthinkable to sever" rests on assertion rather than evidence and does not address CON's core empirical point. Second, CON's "hostage" argument in R2 was the most analytically precise move in the debate: making severance catastrophically costly does not prevent severance, it merely amplifies the damage when political will eventually prevails. PRO's closing restated survival incentives but did not explain why those incentives would work on joint command if they failed on GSOMIA. Third, CON's colonizer/colonized asymmetry response to the European analogy was never adequately rebutted; PRO's "missiles don't respect colonial legacy" counter correctly identifies the physics but ignores that domestic leaders and populations—who decide whether agreements survive—plainly do. CON's closing "two kinds of failure" framing was the cleanest comparative argument in the debate, cleanly distinguishing limited damage from informal-channel fraying versus catastrophic damage from formal-command rupture. PRO's pressure question on informal revocability was pointed, but CON's answer (working-level coordination survived every crisis, GSOMIA nearly didn't) was responsive and grounded. PRO's "circular trap" framing was rhetorically effective but did not resolve the core evidentiary problem: the one real precedent both sides cited supported CON's mechanism more than PRO's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 "hostage" argument is the most decisive exchange. PRO claimed that making withdrawal "operationally unthinkable" prevents failure; CON replied that making failure operationally catastrophic does not prevent politically-driven rupture—it only ensures the rupture is total. Combined with the observation that GSOMIA nearly collapsed despite an active nuclear threat and survived only through external rescue, this systematically undercuts PRO's central mechanism and was never squarely answered in PRO's closing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side B built the stronger overall case by showing formal structures amplify rather than contain political fractures (GSOMIA near-collapse under threat), while ad-hoc via US intermediaries proved more durable with lower stakes. PRO's institutional trust-generation and sunk-cost claims were not fully neutralized, as B's false-confidence versus limited-damage comparison held after exchanges.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Rebuttal 2 exchange on GSOMIA sunk costs vs. catastrophic rupture, where B exposed that deeper ties raise incentives for adversaries to trigger failure while A could not fully escape the circular reliance on pre-existing political will.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): CON won by demonstrating that formal joint structures without underlying trust do not prevent political rupture; they amplify its damage. It effectively used the GSOMIA crisis to show political will overwhelming institutional logic, and framed deeper integration as a hostage scenario rather than a safeguard. CON’s counter-model of resilient, low-visibility ad-hoc coordination through U.S. intermediaries survived PRO’s pressure on latency and offered a more realistic path to sustained cooperation. PRO’s case that operational interdependence would make withdrawal “unthinkable” remained speculative and never fully answered why entangled command structures would be immune to the domestic pressures that nearly collapsed a much shallower pact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 turn that deeper formalization converts political friction into catastrophic institutional failure rather than preventing it, contrasting limited damage from informal fray against total operational collapse from severed joint command.
