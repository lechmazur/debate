### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): A won by making the decisive clash about enforceability rather than visibility. B plausibly argued that interim legality with transparency is better than waiting, but leaned too hard on unsupported claims about current unregulated extraction and on the Outer Space Treaty creating practical deterrence without showing a priority rule, court, injunction, or binding dispute mechanism. A repeatedly and cleanly showed that transparency and debris control do not prevent monopolization of scarce sites or resolve overlapping licenses, and that legal recognition may surrender leverage before equitable rules exist.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was the answer that transparency is a receipt, not a restraint: B's claim that disclosure enables OST challenges was undercut by A's point that the treaty lacks adjudication, priority rules, and a way to force withdrawal once infrastructure is deployed.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A convincingly won the core clash on enforcement by proving that transparency without a dispute resolution mechanism merely documents monopolization rather than preventing it. Side A's argument that early legalization surrenders the international community's leverage for a future treaty remained standing, and they decisively neutralized Side B's reliance on the Outer Space Treaty.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, which brilliantly points out that relying on OST state responsibility to resolve disputes actually "nationalizes the conflict," escalating commercial rivalry into interstate brinkmanship.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): CON successfully established that the proposition’s two guardrails—transparency and debris control—cannot substitute for enforceable governance. The decisive clash centered on whether interim legalization prevents land grabs or merely documents them. CON’s argument that the Outer Space Treaty lacks an adjudicatory body, priority rules, or injunctive power, and that state responsibility nationalizes commercial disputes into interstate brinkmanship, remained largely intact after PRO’s responses. PRO’s reliance on state-level enforcement incentives and pre-operation disclosure creating negotiation space was plausible but idealistic; CON demonstrated that without multilateral dispute resolution, transparency becomes a starting pistol for physical occupation rather than a restraint. CON’s leverage argument—that pre-treaty legalization surrenders the international community’s only bargaining chip for equitable benefit-sharing—was also never fully neutralized. Side A’s clipped opening and failure to close the enforcement gap left CON with the cleaner, more durable route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s first rebuttal introducing the visibility-versus-enforceability distinction and the “receipt, not a restraint” framing, which PRO never overcame with a credible mechanism to prevent state-backed operators from achieving de facto monopolization through physical occupation.
