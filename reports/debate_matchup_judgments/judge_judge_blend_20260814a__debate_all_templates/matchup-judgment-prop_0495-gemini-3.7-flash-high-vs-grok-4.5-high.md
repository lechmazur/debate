### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing that critical software requires continuous specialized labor, that portability does not supply that labor, and that targeted public funding directly reduces burnout and neglect. Side A offered credible procurement, insurance, and consortium alternatives, but did not show they would reliably overcome the conceded free-rider problem or that imperfect public selection makes funding worse than persistent underinvestment. B's repeated nonexistent reference IDs and its weak closing response to A's concrete private-compulsion mechanisms narrowed the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer that dependency itself creates the security target, while a paycheck supplies capacity rather than creating vulnerability, sharply undercut A's XZ-based objection. Its broader point that an imperfectly selected funding program can still protect high-risk public goods also outweighed A's demand that every dependency be covered.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): CON won by the thinnest margin because it exposed unresolved structural flaws in the public-funding model: taxpayer pay would socialize maintenance costs while free-riding firms kept the upside, political selection of “critical” libraries would crowd out independent global maintainers, and funding a few gatekeepers would leave the deep dependency long tail exactly as exposed as before. PRO’s bridge analogy was resonant but never fully answered how the proposal compels corporations to internalize risk rather than simply shift the wage bill to the public, nor how an objective, non-political chooser could operate across a borderless stack. CON’s sharper weighing—that pressure on extractors scales while a politicized payroll does not—left the cleaner live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s repetition of “paid isolation is still isolation” and its sustained pressure that the long tail of dependencies would remain unfunded exposed a structural limit in PRO’s model that PRO never fully resolved.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by dismantling Side A's core physical infrastructure analogy and providing a more concrete, structural alternative to the free-rider problem. While Side A relied on the assumption that private markets would only ever act out of "benevolence," Side B successfully introduced private compulsion (liability, cyber-insurance, procurement rules) as a mechanism to force the internalization of risk. Side A failed to engage with this mechanism in the later rounds, improperly dismissing it as corporate self-policing. Side B also effectively highlighted the political and structural flaws of applying a public payroll to a borderless, forkable codebase, leaving Side A's case structurally compromised.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8), where it answered the pressure question by detailing how procurement rules and cyber-insurance compel free-riders to internalize risk, directly neutralizing Side A's claim that only public funding can solve the market failure. Side A's subsequent failure to address this, mislabeling it as "corporate benevolence" in the closing, sealed the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. It established the public-good and systemic-risk rationale for stable funding, then plausibly framed funding as a residual backstop delivered through grants or foundations rather than a nationalized payroll. Side A identified genuine oversight and moral-hazard risks, but repeatedly escalated them into unsupported absolutes: that public support necessarily requires security clearances and fragmentation, and that any backstop destroys private incentives. Side B preserved liability and procurement as complementary tools while explaining why they do not eliminate the present funding gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer to the claimed oversight dilemma was decisive: it separated compensation mechanisms from control of commit access and noted that cross-border public funding need not make every recipient a cleared civil servant. Side A’s response largely reasserted legal necessity without establishing why narrower grants, contracts, or foundation support are impossible.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won by establishing that public funding for critical open-source maintenance can be structured as stable, comparable support without the jurisdictional capture and civil-service accountability regime CON claimed were inevitable. PRO successfully distinguished funding labor from controlling commit keys, showed that cross-border research funding already operates on a similar separation, and demonstrated that CON's downstream-liability alternative had already failed to prevent systemic burnout. CON treated all public funding as direct state employment, which PRO disclaimed from the opening, so the governance objection remained an unresolved tension rather than a fatal contradiction. PRO's residual backstop frame held up better under pressure and provided a cleaner path to affirming the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's distinction between funding essential labor and commandeering repositories (A2.1-A2.2, A4.1), which directly answered CON's claim that public paychecks necessarily require security clearances, jurisdictional lockouts, and fractured global collaboration.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing critical open-source libraries as public goods that markets systematically underprovide, and by dismantling Side B's core objection. Side B argued that public funding legally requires strict state oversight and security clearances that would destroy borderless open-source collaboration. Side A effectively countered this by pointing to cross-border science and research grants, exposing Side B's legal and constitutional claims as brittle and overstated. Furthermore, Side A highlighted that Side B's alternative—downstream corporate liability—only punishes failures after they occur, failing to answer who proactively funds the maintenance needed to prevent systemic outages in the first place.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 to the pressure questions, specifically dismantling Side B's claim that public funding legally requires locking out foreign developers by citing existing international research funding models, and pointing out that Side B's liability model does not answer who pays to prevent the next outage.
