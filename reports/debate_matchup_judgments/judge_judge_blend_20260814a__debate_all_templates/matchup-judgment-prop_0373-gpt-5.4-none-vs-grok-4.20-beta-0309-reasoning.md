### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins on two connected threads that survived the full exchange. First, the burden argument: the motion required PRO to show the overall pattern has "gone too far," not merely that some decisions are imperfect. CON's concession that some removals "may be overbroad" was tactical, not a concession of the motion, and PRO never closed the gap—their "documented volume" claim in Rebuttal 2 was asserted without grounding in a closed-book format, which weakens it under epistemic discipline standards. Second, the retention-versus-removal weighing comparison introduced in CON's opening (A1.4) was never fully neutralized. PRO's equity counter—students without home access or supportive families—is genuinely powerful and the strongest live argument in PRO's favor, but CON's rejoinder (unequal access is a reason to build better collections, not to override suitability judgments in minor-serving spaces) was adequate, and the asymmetry between exposure risk and access inconvenience remained. CON also landed the sharpest rhetorical point of the debate: PRO's limiting principle is circular—when educators apply judgment to restrict books, PRO labels the outcome censorship regardless, making it "heads-I-win, tails-you-overreached." PRO's closing, at 132 words against a 220-word cap, left real burdens unanswered and failed to crystallize the equity thread that was its strongest route. CON's two clipped rebuttals are a real execution cost, but the closing (209 words) was complete and well-organized. On balance, CON's sustained burden argument and the surviving weighing comparison give it a clear but competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange is CON's retention-versus-removal comparison (A1.4) and PRO's equity rebuttal (B2.2), resolved by CON's rejoinder in A2.3. CON argued that a mistaken retention exposes all minors in a shared environment, while a mistaken removal limits convenience but not access in principle. PRO countered that library-dependent students have no fallback. CON replied that unequal access is an argument for richer collections, not for overriding suitability judgments—reframing equity as a collection-quality issue rather than an argument against removal. This exchange remained live through closing and consistently favored CON's institutional framing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's proposed alternatives (labels and opt-outs) using the practical realities of a school library, where browsing and peer-sharing bypass permission slips. Side A also provided a cleaner comparative framing: in a curated space for minors, the safer error is mistakenly restricting access rather than mistakenly exposing children to age-inappropriate material.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 cleanly answered Side B's pressure by explaining the mechanical reasons why labels and opt-outs fail in open, mixed-age school spaces, decisively neutralizing Side B's main alternative to removals.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): PRO showed that blanket removals sacrifice equitable access and the educational mission, while CON conceded overbroad removals exist but could not articulate a limiting principle to prevent indefinite expansion.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s A2.2 equity rebuttal, which showed that CON’s “alternative access” fallback writes off low-income students who depend on school libraries, undermining CON’s claim that mistaken removal merely limits convenience.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): CON wins by successfully pressing the burden of proof on systemic overreach versus isolated mistakes, and by delivering a decisive practical critique of PRO's proposed alternatives. PRO's reliance on labels and opt-outs never adequately answered CON's grounded point about the porosity of those tools in open, mixed-age library spaces where casual browsing and peer sharing occur. CON's weighing on the asymmetry of errors (mistaken retention exposes minors in a compulsory setting; mistaken removal merely limits convenience) provided a clearer, more institutionally sound reason to reject the motion. PRO's rhetoric was emotive but ultimately sidestepped the practical clash, leaving CON's limiting principle and stewardship frame intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 directly answers the pressure questions by defining a clear threshold for overreach and dismantling the practicality of PRO's opt-out/labeling alternative in open library environments, effectively cementing the asymmetry-of-errors weighing.
