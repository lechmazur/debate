### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the debate around consent, the irrevocable nature of biometric data, and the illusion of "buying elsewhere" for essential services. Side B made a highly effective technical push regarding the backend costs of closed-loop systems, but Side A's strategic maneuver to highlight Side B's concessions on captive monopolies (transport/airports) proved decisive. Side A's final risk calculus—weighing merchant backend costs against irrevocable bodily privacy—was highly persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 explanation of closed-loop biometric backend costs vs. PCI/EMV card rails was a fantastic answer to Side A's pressure question. However, Side A's Rebuttal 1 capture of the accessibility argument ("mine keeps both") was strategically dominant.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the live clash on irrevocable biometric credentials, real consent under captive or thin-choice venues, and the person who simply declines. B’s dual-rail cost and closed-loop points were real but never named a convincing “scanner but no tap” case, and B’s fallbacks kept presupposing enrollment or “buy elsewhere,” which A used to force a concession on metros/airports and collapse the rest into map-haggling. A’s risk weighing (business pennies vs unreissuable identity) decided the motion as written more cleanly than B’s call for only targeted rules.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure Q1 and follow-through: CON “fallbacks” assume prior enrollment, so a pure decliner still cannot pay—B’s “buy elsewhere” answer then concedes the captive case.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by proving the motion is overbroad. PRO showed real coercion harms, but almost exclusively in captive settings (metros, airports, last-shop-in-village) where CON explicitly endorsed targeted fallback rules. CON successfully argued the universal "always" mandate sweeps far wider, banning efficient closed-loop biometric designs at rural kiosks and festival stalls where consumers can choose elsewhere, and that compliance costs would prevent beneficial biometric access for disabled users. PRO never justified why a blanket legal restriction was needed instead of narrower monopoly-specific rules, and their closing effectively narrowed to the very captive venues CON had already conceded. CON also answered the Pressure Questions more directly and avoided the word-limit clipping that disrupted PRO's Rebuttal 2.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.5): the direct answers to PRO's pressure questions distinguishing captive monopolies from competitive markets, combined with the concrete closed-loop kiosk example (rural fuel point / seasonal self-service kiosk) showing the mandate bans viable low-cost designs rather than merely preserving existing rails. This exposed the gap between PRO's "cheap card reader" framing and the real backend cost burden of a parallel payment system.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully exposing a gap between Side A's stated harms and the motion's actual effects. By using Side A's own transit camera example, Side B proved that the motion regulates the checkout lane while leaving the underlying biometric databases untouched. Side B also effectively flipped Side A's accessibility argument, framing the restriction on low-margin deployments as a loss for vulnerable consumers who rely on biometrics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal, where they capitalized on Side A's concession regarding the transit camera. Side B powerfully argued that distinguishing between 'surveillance' and 'payment' surrendered Side A's core opening harm of irreplaceable data sitting in a breachable database.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): B won a close, high-quality clash by showing the motion regulates the doorway rather than the irrevocable-data harm A opened on. B repeatedly forced the point that a convenient card/ticket lane lets bad storage practices and even face enrollment walk through compliance, and A’s Rebuttal 2 admission that the paradigm transit camera still “complies” while being labeled a separate surveillance problem undercut A’s core breach/permanence story. B also better weighed live effects: the rule mainly bites thin-margin deployments and can deny access to users for whom biometrics work when cards/PINs fail, while offering cleaner, objective alternatives (no biometric surcharge; template/retention rules). A remained strong on consent-before-collection and two-way accessibility, and extracted a useful essential-access concession, but never recovered a decisive reason the lane mandate is the better instrument once B separated payment modality from database harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B turning A's own transit-camera paradigm (A2.4) into proof the motion fails its central harm—lane compliance leaves central enrollment and database risk untouched (B2.1–B2.2, sealed in B4.5/B5.2).
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing the motion targets the wrong locus of harm. PRO opened with the danger of irrevocable biometric data in breachable databases, but CON demonstrated that a shop or transit operator can collect and store raw biometric images while remaining fully compliant as long as a card reader exists. PRO's concession that a gateline camera enrolling every face is "a surveillance problem, not a biometric-payment problem" gutted the before/after framing that underpinned their closing. CON offered a more direct alternative—hard rules on template-only storage, retention limits, and breach liability—that actually reaches the database harm. Meanwhile, CON showed PRO's "don't deploy yet" answer for low-margin operators means vulnerable users (arthritis sufferers, domestic-abuse victims who need palm-pay) lose a working access method without gaining real privacy protection. The motion became a hammer for a phantom problem and a scalpel that cut the wrong patient.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4.1–A4.8), where they exposed that PRO's own "paradigm danger"—a transit camera centrally enrolling faces—walks straight through the rule so long as a ticket machine is technically available. This collapsed PRO's claim that the motion protects "before" collection, since the camera records regardless of checkout lane, and paired it with a sharp accessibility turnaround showing that PRO's rule denies working payment options to those who need them most.
