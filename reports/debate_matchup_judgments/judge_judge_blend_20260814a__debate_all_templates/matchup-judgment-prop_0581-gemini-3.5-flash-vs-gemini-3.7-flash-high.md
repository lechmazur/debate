### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Both sides built genuine, well-grounded economic cases without fabricated citations, and both handled the pressure-question format cleanly with proper A1/A2 labeling. CON (A) had strong material on economies of scale, double marginalization, and the VC exit mechanism, and its "five inefficient firms vs. three scaled firms" closing image was a solid comparative frame. But PRO (B) sustained the more decisive throughline: that cost efficiencies mean nothing to consumers absent competitive pressure to pass them on, since a merged firm captures savings as profit once rivals are removed. CON's central rebuttal to this ("basic microeconomics disproves the premise... unless a firm possesses an absolute, insurmountable monopoly") asserted pass-through rather than actually engaging with B's point that merger itself removes the competitive discipline that forces pass-through — this left B's core mechanism functionally unrefuted. B also answered pressure questions more substantively (offering JV/licensing/mid-tier alternatives and forced internal R&D) and kept a crisp, consistent "build vs. buy" frame across rounds that tied its horizontal, vertical, and innovation arguments together. The result is a close, competitive debate but one where B's rebuttal chain did slightly more damage to CON's central causal claim than CON's rebuttals did to PRO's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing framing that "paper-thin cost efficiencies mean absolutely nothing to consumers without the competitive pressure to pass them on" (crystallizing B2.2/B4.3) was the most decisive exchange, since A's answer (A4.1) asserted pass-through via "basic microeconomics" without directly refuting that merger-driven loss of rivals removes the very pressure needed for pass-through.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON won the decisive cost vs competition trade: PRO never neutralized double marginalization elimination and minimum efficient scale logic, and its claim that savings are pocketed was met by A4's micro response that lower marginal cost lowers price absent absolute monopoly. The five-at-$10 vs three-at-$5 cost-floor example crystallized that more competitors at higher costs still means higher consumer prices. On innovation, PRO's killer-acquisition pivot was answered by the specialization bridge - startups need big-firm capital for trials/manufacturing - and PRO's mid-tier/JV fix did not solve the valley-of-death funding problem that CON pressed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 opening with A1/A2: profit-maximizing firms pass through lower marginal costs even with imperfect competition, plus the 5 sub-scale at $10 vs 3 scaled at $5 illustration, which flipped PRO's core pocketing argument and made the cost-floor weighing decisive.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B wins by providing a more rigorous and concrete economic framework for why blocking mergers harms consumers and innovation. Side B effectively used the concepts of minimum efficient scale, double marginalization, and the venture capital valley of death (specifically in biopharma) to show that strict enforcement artificially inflates the cost floor and starves early-stage innovation. Side A relied heavily on the intuitive but less rigorously defended claim that merged firms simply pocket efficiencies and that startups can easily pivot to joint ventures or mid-tier acquisitions. Side B's pressure answers were sharper and directly dismantled Side A's assumptions about organic growth and startup commercialization.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides ran coherent, mechanism-driven cases (double marginalization and VC exit needs for A; foreclosure, killer acquisitions, and rent-capture for B), and both handled the Pressure Questions format correctly with clean A1/A2 answers. The decisive difference came in how each side handled the other's central mechanism. B's framing—that private efficiency (whether from vertical integration or scale) only reaches consumers if competitive rivalry forces pass-through, otherwise it becomes captured rent—was pressed early and never squarely dismantled by A; A's answer to B's pointed Q1 ("what forces a merged firm to pass savings to consumers?") relied on vague appeals to "global competition" and "capacity pressure" rather than a specific mechanism addressing a firm that has just eliminated its direct domestic rival. By contrast, when A pressed B on the IPO-exit weakness for startups, B adapted by narrowing its claim to targeting only anticompetitive buyouts by dominant incumbents (leaving licensing, non-dominant M&A, and organic scaling open), which blunted much of A's "VC funding will vanish" argument, and A's final rebuttal did not directly engage this narrowing. The net effect is that B's core theoretical throughline survived the exchange more intact than A's, giving B the cleaner path to the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question on monopoly-rent capture (B3.1) versus A's generic "global competition" answer (A4.1) was the most decisive exchange: it exposed that A's efficiency story lacked a concrete mechanism forcing pass-through to consumers, a gap A never closed in later speeches.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won the central weighing clash: without competitive discipline, claimed vertical and scale efficiencies are captured as rents rather than passed as lower prices, and A never supplied a reliable pass-through mechanism for concentrated domestic markets. On innovation, B's killer-acquisition/replacement-effect story directly answered A's commercialization point, while narrowing strict enforcement to dominant gatekeepers blunted the VC-starvation cost. CON's double-marginalization and duplication arguments remained plausible but were outweighed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 + private-efficiency ≠ consumer-welfare framing – turned A's core efficiency claims into rents without discipline and exposed lack of pass-through mechanism.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by establishing a decisive weighing mechanism: private corporate efficiency does not translate to consumer welfare without competitive discipline. While Side B made strong, practical arguments about venture capital exits and supply chain friction, Side A successfully neutralized the core of Side B's case by pointing out that eliminated double marginalization and scale economies are simply captured as monopoly rents in concentrated markets. Side A's closing crystallization of this clash was exceptionally sharp and provided a cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal to the double marginalization argument, effectively pointing out that theoretical supply-chain cost savings only lower consumer prices if competitive rivalry forces the firm to pass them on, otherwise they are pocketed as rents.
