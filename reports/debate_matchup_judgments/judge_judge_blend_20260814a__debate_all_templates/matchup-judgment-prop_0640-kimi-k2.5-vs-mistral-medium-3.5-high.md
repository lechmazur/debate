### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A ran the cleaner, more disciplined debate across the full exchange. Its opening was complete and built a coherent causal story linking financialized ownership to both ecological brittleness and rural hollowing. Its first rebuttal successfully distinguished fund-level horizons from manager-level incentives—a distinction CON never squarely resolved—and inverted the land-price argument plausibly. Most decisively, Side A's Q1 exposed a genuine logical contradiction in CON's case: if caps are easily gamed they cannot simultaneously constrain supply enough to harm young farmers. CON's R2 answer (a "two-tier market" where domestic actors are bound but offshore actors evade) was the most substantive response in the debate, but it conceded that the policy harms domestic institutional investors while offshore capital gains advantage—a partial admission of the contradiction rather than a clean escape from it. Side A then crystallized the "fatal contradiction" framing in R2, which CON never dislodged. CON's recurring strengths—climate adaptation capital, long-term lease structures, local agronomist subcontractors—were individually plausible but were met each time with Side A's stewardship-versus-equipment distinction, which remained live. CON also entered with an underweight opening (roughly 60% of the allocated words), leaving it playing catch-up throughout. Some of CON's statistics (TIAA acreage, 40% leased-acreage share) were stated with more confident specificity than a closed-book setting warrants, which modestly discounts their authority. Side A's clipped closing is a real execution cost but not enough to shift the result given its dominance in earlier rounds, particularly the pressure-question exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Q1 was the most decisive moment: it exposed that CON could not simultaneously claim caps are easily gamed (making them toothless) and that they constrain supply enough to raise lease rates for young farmers (making them harmful). CON's two-tier-market answer in R2 partially addressed this but conceded that domestic regulated investors bear the burden while offshore actors evade it—confirming the policy distorts rather than cleans up the market, yet not dissolving the core contradiction about whether the cap is meaningfully binding.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by making the ownership-incentives clash easier to decide: financialized absentee ownership was tied to soil stewardship, rural hollowing, and speculative land pricing, while Side A relied heavily on unsupported precise claims and an internally strained 2 percent versus 40 percent lease argument. Side A had a plausible adaptation-capital and implementation case, but did not fully answer why caps on institutional land concentration would be worse than leaving resilience-critical land governed by portfolio incentives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was the pressure-question and second-rebuttal attack on Side A's claim that institutional ownership was too small to matter yet essential to leased-land access, turning Side A's own figures into a concentration and pricing-power problem.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a clear core clash on temporal and incentive mismatches between portfolios and stewardship, then exploiting A's contradictions on scale versus necessity of institutional capital. B's pressure questions forced direct engagement that A could not escape without inconsistency, and B's later speeches adapted by weighing resilience as place-based rather than tech-driven. A's specific stats and rebuttals were undercut by their own claims on 2% ownership versus 40% leasing control.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which answered the pressure questions head-on and crystallized the fatal contradiction in A's position on whether institutions are too small or too vital to cap.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): Side B (CON) built the stronger technical case across the full exchange. Three live arguments tilted the balance: (1) the retiring-farmer equity/liquidity problem — introduced in the opening and never adequately resolved by PRO, whose Rebuttal 2 answer ("caps redirect liquidity to active farmers at agricultural value") failed to explain how aging owners actually realize their life savings at those lower prices; (2) the monoculture-drivers argument — CON's claim that Midwest corn-soy dominance stems from federal crop insurance and ethanol mandates rather than ownership structure is a strong, plausible causal point that PRO never engaged or rebutted; and (3) the economic coherence of the young-farmer access argument — CON correctly noted that removing wealthy buyers without providing capital to young entrants does not improve their position, and PRO's response never bridged that gap. PRO's case carried genuine rhetorical force — the closing image of "entities that have never gone hungry, can always walk away" is memorable — and the resilience framing had real emotional weight. But PRO's rebuttals were shorter and shallower (198 and 169 words vs. CON's 274 and 281), left the monoculture-as-policy-failure point standing unchallenged, and the A1:/A2: answers were thin. CON's pressure questions were also sharper, targeting real contradictions rather than restating the case. PRO's questions were fair but CON's direct answers held up better under scrutiny. The debate is competitive because PRO's framing of food security vs. speculation is genuinely important, but CON did more argumentative work at the technical level and resolved more of the live clashes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained argument that Midwest monoculture dominance is caused by federal crop insurance and ethanol mandates rather than by ownership structure [B4.2]. This directly dismantled PRO's central causal claim — that institutional ownership drives ecological harm — and PRO never answered it. Without this causal link, the core justification for the cap collapses even on PRO's own terms.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making the cap look poorly targeted: it argued that ownership limits would reduce liquidity for retiring farmers, would not solve young farmers' capital constraints, and would not address the real drivers of monoculture or ecological harm. Side B had an intuitive community-stewardship story, but relied heavily on asserted correlations and repeated “Wall Street versus local farmers” rhetoric without sufficiently answering A’s liquidity, policy-driver, and implementation objections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the monoculture challenge by shifting causation to crop insurance, ethanol, and commodity incentives rather than deed ownership, while also pressing that lower land prices do not automatically create viable young-farmer buyers.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by more convincingly neutralizing PRO's core mechanisms—monoculture via investor returns and community hollowing—through direct counters on institutional regenerative mandates, subsidy-driven practices, and unintended illiquidity harming retiring farmers' retirement equity. PRO's answers to pressure questions on liquidity and definition remained evasive and failed to resolve contradictions, while A's rebuttals adapted tightly to damage live routes to victory without new evidence dumps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4) on liquidity evaporation from buyer caps and monoculture causes in subsidies/policy rather than deeds, directly addressing B's questions and sharpening the trade-off on patient capital vs. fragmentation.
