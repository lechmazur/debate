### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Both sides ran competent cases, but CON (Side A) built the stronger cumulative path. PRO's core argument—that life extensions cost $1–2k/kW, take 12–18 months, and then deliver 90% capacity-factor power—was undermined by PRO's own chosen examples. When PRO cited Palisades ($1.5B DOE loan targeting 2025 restart) and Diablo Canyon to prove the model works within the decade, CON immediately weaponized those numbers: a $1.5B federal commitment for roughly 800 MW is not the cheap tune-up advertised, and both projects required years of regulatory and financial effort, consuming exactly the decade-time PRO claimed nuclear would not waste. That rebuttal move was the most decisive exchange in the debate. PRO's Q2 pressure—asking how private-capital leverage translates to emissions per tonne rather than megawatts installed—was sharp, and CON's answer (the "compounding catalyst" framing) was somewhat indirect. PRO also correctly maintained that the storage cost to firm intermittent renewables is real physics, not a strawman, and CON never fully quantified a portfolio answer. Still, PRO's clipped Rebuttal 2 was an execution cost that left its closing thinner than needed. And the motion's fixed 2026–2036 window favors CON's core logic: capacity factor is irrelevant while an asset is offline, and a technology that scales in months compounds cumulative avoided emissions across every year of the decade, while a refurbishment absorbing years of the window may deliver nothing until midway through or beyond. PRO's quantitative case was logically coherent but rested on numbers that proved harder to defend once CON turned the real-world examples against them. The decisive clash—deployment delay vs. capacity factor in a fixed window—was ultimately cleaner for CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's own Palisades and Diablo Canyon examples against PRO: CON showed that a $1.5B federal loan commitment for a single reactor restart contradicts PRO's central claim of cheap 12–18-month refurbishments, effectively using PRO's evidence to prove CON's point that even life extensions are multi-billion-dollar, multi-year public commitments that consume years of the decade before displacing a tonne.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): B wins by making the decisive metric cumulative emissions per public dollar inside 2026–2036 and repeatedly showing why deployment speed, modularity, and public-capital leverage matter more than theoretical capacity factor. A had a plausible life-extension route and concrete examples, but leaned on brittle cost arithmetic, did not really defend new-build delivery, and never fully justified forcing renewables to replicate baseload with massive storage before counting emissions reductions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was the answer to A’s capacity-factor/storage math: B framed it as a false baseload-replication assumption and argued that real grids use incremental renewables, portfolios, demand response, and modest storage to displace fossil generation immediately, which directly undercut A’s cost-per-tonne calculation.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A wins by anchoring the fixed 2026-2036 window and exposing nuclear delivery risks/timeline slips even on life extensions, directly damaging B's core causal story while answering pressure questions head-on. B's numbers were undercut and examples like Palisades failed to neutralize the speed metric.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4) on Palisades/Diablo as multi-year risks plus compounding renewable deployment; directly neutralized B's answers to pressure questions.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO maintained a coherent capacity-factor argument throughout: even granting a five-year relicensing delay, nuclear at roughly 90% capacity factor produces far more firm, fossil-free electricity per public dollar than wind or solar at 25–35% CF over the same investment horizon. PRO sketched the arithmetic early (one nuclear GW ≈ 8 TWh/yr; equivalent renewable capital ≈ 1–1.5 TWh/yr) and CON never produced a competing set of numbers to show renewables out-perform nuclear in total avoided tons by 2036. PRO also successfully exposed the multi-day battery storage gap and refused to let CON retreat to cell-level costs as a proxy for system-level firming. PRO's Q1 pressure question forced CON to concede that a retired reactor gets replaced by gas, which undermined CON's implicit assumption that the counterfactual is clean. CON's time-value framing ("five idle years") was rhetorically crisp and genuinely strong, but it was decisive only if the per-year avoided-ton advantage of renewables exceeded the large capacity-factor gap — a showing CON never made. Two of CON's speeches were clipped for exceeding the word limit, adding an execution cost, and CON's battery claims remained anchored to cell cost rather than installed multi-day systems, a gap PRO pressed consistently. PRO's case is not airtight — the new-build argument effectively collapsed into life-extension — but on the motion's core metric (tons avoided per public dollar in 2026–2036), PRO held the stronger live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question — "If that reactor retires, what zero-carbon source replaces it, and how do the tons from inevitable gas backup factor into your per-dollar claim?" — was the most decisive exchange. CON conceded gas would fill the gap but pivoted to arguing the relicensing money could fund renewables instead. That pivot quietly shifted the comparison away from the original counterfactual, and CON never then demonstrated numerically that renewables deployed from that reallocation would avoid more tons by 2036 than the relicensed plant running at high capacity factor for five-plus years. PRO's subsequent Rebuttal 2 crystallized this gap clearly, leaving CON's speed argument rhetorically strong but mathematically unanswered.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A narrowly won by making the more durable per-dollar comparison: firm delivered clean energy and life-extension versus nameplate renewable capacity plus unresolved firming costs. Side B’s ten-year speed framing and new-build critique were strong and kept the debate close, but its cost math was inconsistently grounded, its battery answer was weak, and it conceded retired reactors would likely be replaced by gas. A did not fully carry new-build, but won enough on life-extension and firm avoided tons.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure and second rebuttal on multi-day storage were decisive: B could not identify a grid-scale battery solution beating reactor life-extension and then argued renewables should not be penalized for needing storage that does not yet exist, which reinforced A’s firm-versus-intermittent framing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO wins on the core metric of tons avoided per public dollar by 2036. Its direct answers to pressure questions on replacement generation and multi-day storage exposed CON's reliance on nameplate MW and unproven firming at scale. CON's clipped rebuttals and pivot to deployment speed alone left the actual energy-output gap unclosed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's labeled A1/A2 answers to pressure questions, which neutralized CON's intermittency claims with firm-output comparison.
