### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B wins because it correctly identified and maintained the decisive comparative burden from the opening—the proposition requires that algorithmic feeds produce more anxiety and depression than chronological or non-personalized alternatives, not merely that they carry risks in isolation—and PRO never cleared that bar. B's case had three live routes at the close: (1) chronological feeds carry their own unrebutted harms (real-time bullying, peer conflict, infinite refresh), (2) non-personalized feeds are not "neutral" but blunt instruments serving viral cruelty uniformly to every user, and (3) every mechanism PRO names—compulsive checking, social comparison, sleep disruption—operates across all feed formats. PRO's strongest contribution was the active-amplification / feedback-loop frame (a pause is weaponized into an infinite funnel), which is genuinely intuitive. But B's response was adequate: the recommendation space is wider than PRO's worst-case, the vulnerability pre-existed the algorithm, and a chronological feed does not prevent manual rumination and late-night refreshing. Most damaging was B's use of PRO's own answer to Q2 (compulsive behavior as escape-seeking from adolescent psychology) to show the mechanism operates regardless of feed architecture—PRO inadvertently conceded the key comparison. PRO's assertion that non-personalized feeds are "neutral" (A2.6) and that chronological feeds are naturally "bounded" were both exposed and left unanswered. Side A had vivid metaphors and a coherent mechanism story, but mechanism ≠ comparative harm, and that gap was never closed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal use of PRO's own A2 answer: PRO conceded that compulsive checking is "driven by escape" and is a "maladaptive coping mechanism" rooted in adolescent psychology—and B immediately turned this against PRO, correctly noting that if the underlying mechanism is adolescent psychology rather than feed architecture, it operates across every format. This exposed the central flaw in PRO's case (attributing a clinical pattern to algorithm design) and was never squarely answered in PRO's closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A wins a clear but competitive debate. Side B effectively pressured the comparison and exposed weaknesses in A's claims that chronological feeds are finite or non-personalized feeds are neutral. But B leaned too heavily on the idea that pre-existing vulnerability means the algorithm is only a mirror, which did not answer A's stronger claim that engagement optimization can amplify, concentrate, and deepen that vulnerability. A gave the cleaner differential mechanism: predictive feedback loops, reduced friction, globalized comparison, and retention incentives make algorithmic feeds more harmful than passive or less-personalized alternatives. B showed the alternatives are also harmful, but did not sufficiently neutralize A's scale and amplification argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's mirror-versus-feedback-loop rebuttal was the decisive exchange: it directly answered B's causation challenge by arguing that detecting vulnerability and then intensifying exposure can itself cause additional harm, even if the vulnerability pre-existed.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B wins by relentlessly enforcing the comparative burden and dismantling PRO's idealized baseline. PRO built a vivid case around algorithmic amplification, but repeatedly relied on the claim that chronological feeds are "finite and bounded," which B effectively debunked by noting auto-refresh features, push notifications, and the psychological reality that anxious teens ruminate and manually refresh regardless of architecture. The decisive moment came in B's second rebuttal, where B turned PRO's own answer about "maladaptive coping" and escape-seeking against them: if the compulsion is rooted in adolescent psychology rather than feed design, it operates across all formats, fatally undermining PRO's claim of differential harm. PRO's rhetoric was strong, but they never cleared the hurdle of proving the algorithm causes *more* anxiety and depression than the alternatives, leaving B's "mirror vs. motor" and comparative framework standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which cleanly answered the pressure questions and executed a sharp turn on PRO's retention argument. By demonstrating that PRO's own explanation for why teens don't churn (psychological escape-seeking) actually proves the compulsion is format-agnostic, B neutralized PRO's core mechanism and cemented the comparative failure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A (PRO) built a cleaner, more durable case by anchoring to a single falsifiable mechanism—the optimization target is watch time, not wellbeing—and defending it consistently across every round. PRO's sharpest move was recognizing that distress-driven fixation and genuine positive interest produce identical signals to the algorithm, meaning CON's "multi-signal suppression" argument cannot work if the system's core reward is attention capture. PRO also correctly turned CON's viral-toxicity point: content goes viral precisely because engagement-optimized systems amplify it, not because chronological feeds fail to prevent it. CON never adequately answered either of these. CON's "dynamic shock absorber" framing was vivid but internally contradictory—a system optimizing for watch time cannot simultaneously suppress content that maximizes watch time. CON's most specific counter in Rebuttal 2 (that rabbit-hole research relies on bot accounts with unnatural scrolling) reads as brittle new factual assertion unsupported by anything in the prompt, which should be discounted. CON's "fatal contradiction" argument—that PRO both denies teens self-regulation and expects them to curate their own feeds—was clever but misrepresented PRO's case; PRO argued that bounded self-exposure is structurally less dangerous than algorithmic escalation, not that teens have perfect executive function. PRO also correctly held the right burden throughout: the motion requires only that engagement optimization makes things worse than alternatives, not that alternatives are harm-free. CON never established a reason to believe a well-designed subscription-based or community-based feed produces worse outcomes than an engagement-optimized one. PRO's closing line—"a teenager frozen on a distressing video is generating valuable data"—crystallized the causal story more memorably than anything CON offered in response.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 point that the viral-toxicity argument actually collapses CON's own case was the most decisive exchange. CON had claimed chronological feeds are defenseless against harmful viral content; PRO correctly identified that content achieves viral scale because engagement-optimized systems detect and amplify its attention-holding power. CON then never recovered a coherent account of how the amplification engine is neutral while also serving as a protective filter.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by keeping the comparison fixed on marginal algorithmic amplification. Its strongest point was that engagement optimization rewards attention capture, not adolescent wellbeing, so distress-driven lingering can be treated as a reason to serve more similar content. Side B had a real countercase about chronological feeds intensifying peer comparison and personalization enabling niche support, but it repeatedly relied on an over-idealized version of engagement systems as protective “shock absorbers” and a worst-case version of chronological feeds. B’s claims about modern algorithms reliably detecting distress and intervening were asserted more than established, while A’s bounded-exposure versus escalation distinction directly answered B’s best pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s response that harms like peer comparison or actively seeking bad content exist under any feed, while engagement-optimized feeds uniquely add escalation and amplification, was the decisive exchange. It neutralized B’s “chronological feeds are also harmful” argument by showing that the proposition only required proving the algorithm makes the baseline worse.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A wins by cleanly isolating the comparative burden and landing a decisive mechanism: engagement optimization treats distress-driven fixation identically to positive interest, systematically amplifying harmful content beyond what a user voluntarily seeks. Side B’s defense relied on contested claims about algorithmic benevolence and multi-signal filtering, which A effectively undermined by pointing out the platform’s core optimization target remains watch time, not wellbeing. A also handled B’s pressure questions directly, clarifying that chronological feeds are bounded by reality and user choice, whereas algorithms manufacture escalation. B’s executive function contradiction was clever but answered adequately, and B’s unsupported claim that rabbit-hole evidence relies on bot accounts weakened its grounding. A made the debate easier to decide by consistently weighing the marginal harm added by the algorithm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 directly answered the pressure questions by distinguishing bounded self-exposure from algorithmic escalation, clarifying that the motion only requires proving the algorithm adds marginal harm. This neutralized CON’s attempt to shift the burden to chronological perfection and cemented PRO’s path to victory.
