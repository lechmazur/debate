### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): Side B (CON) built and maintained the stronger comparative case across the debate. The decisive move was established early and never dislodged: the real comparison is AI feedback versus sparse, delayed, and inconsistent human comments from overextended teachers—not AI versus an idealized patient mentor. PRO never fully answered this, because their case rested on a picture of writing instruction that many secondary students do not actually receive. CON's second key move was definitional discipline: "main source" means most frequent formative feedback during drafting, not passive acceptance of AI rewrites; teachers still conference, set rubrics, and assess final thinking. PRO never closed this gap, oscillating between "AI can harm" and "AI necessarily harms," which is a weaker claim. CON's sharpest rebuttal—"productive struggle is not the same as unguided struggle"—directly neutralized PRO's friction argument without conceding that iteration is harmful. PRO's best counter, the adolescent path-of-least-resistance psychology, is genuinely compelling and keeps this competitive, but CON's answer (assignment design, process logs, revision rationales) was concrete enough to blunt the necessity claim. CON's repeated word-limit clippings (four rounds) are real execution costs and prevent a wider margin, as they left several lines undeveloped. PRO, by contrast, executed cleanly throughout and landed the memorable "optimize text but cannot write" close. Still, the core comparative burden—what actually helps more students write better in real schools—remained with CON by the end of the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 [B4.5]: "Productive struggle is not the same as unguided struggle." This single line cleanly divided PRO's entire friction argument. PRO had conflated "cognitive friction is valuable" with "frequent feedback is harmful," and CON's distinction showed those are not equivalent. Feedback that exposes a logical gap or evidential mismatch preserves the thinking task while making it legible—this directly answered PRO's central claim without conceding that iteration undermines development.

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): Side A wins because it successfully defended the principled line between deterministic mastery practice and holistic writing development, exposing that generative AI's inherent nature as a statistical text predictor creates a path-of-least-resistance problem which idealized assignment design cannot reliably solve. Side B's substantive reframing was strong, but incurred heavy execution costs from four clipped speeches, leaving key rebuttals incomplete and preventing full crystallization of the comparative case.

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A (PRO) won by making a more analytically precise and consistent distinction throughout: "main source" means the authoritative anchor that sets the standard for what good writing is, not merely the most frequent touchpoint. PRO also correctly identified CON's core substitution: scarcity of teacher bandwidth proves AI should expand practice volume, but it does not prove AI should define what counts as good writing. The "wrong center" framing—students revising around a pattern detector instead of genuine reasoning—was PRO's strongest running claim, and CON never fully neutralized it. CON's own Rebuttal 2 partially conceded the architecture by accepting that "the teacher still decides overarching pedagogical goals," which undercut their insistence that AI is the main source. CON's bandwidth argument was compelling and real, but it recycled without progression across rounds: each later speech repeated the 120-student constraint rather than resolving the norm-setting problem PRO kept pressing. PRO's speeches were clipped twice, a small execution cost, but enough substance survived each time that the analytical thread held. CON's pragmatic realism was the debate's most persuasive single moment (the opening's "almost no feedback at all"), but it never evolved into a satisfying answer to the question of who should set the standard—and that question is precisely what the motion asks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q1 (the calculator analogy): AI can guide thesis drills and outline checks—frequent, low-stakes rehearsal—without becoming the "main source" any more than a calculator becomes the main math teacher. This directly exposed CON's conflation of frequency with authority and is the exchange that most clearly defined what "main source" means under the motion, leaving CON without a clean response to the norm-setting concern.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by preserving the motion's distinction between practice support and authoritative main feedback, directly answering pressure on definitions, and weighing quality of norms over sheer volume without evasion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1/A2 answers in Rebuttal 2, cleanly separating drills from main-source authority and rejecting CON's collapse of quantity into quality.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by drawing and defending a clean distinction between abundant AI support for practice and AI as the norm-setting main source of writing feedback. While CON effectively leveraged teacher scarcity and the need for iterative drafting, PRO's framing of "main source" as the normative anchor rather than merely the most frequent commentator neutralized CON's volume argument.
