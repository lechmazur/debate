### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-argued cases, but A's central structural critique proved more durable. A distinguished "values" from "factual assumptions" underlying a directive, arguing that a directive can misfire even when the signer's values never changed (e.g., mistaken beliefs about how bad dementia would feel or how burdensome treatment would be). B's only answer was to say only explicit written conditions can excuse non-compliance, dismissing implicit premises entirely — but A correctly noted most directives rely on implicit, not explicit, assumptions, so this response mostly restated PRO's rule rather than refuting the objection. A also pressed an evidentiary-trap point (the "clear evidence" exception is nearly impossible to satisfy for someone whose incapacity is the very condition in question) that B never really solved beyond reasserting that ambiguous behavior can't count. In the final exchange, B's closing scored a real hit by arguing A's "misfire" logic would let hindsight defeat every directive, but A's earlier rebuttal (graduated weight tied to specificity, stakes, and repeated/spontaneous/treatment-specific present conduct) had already supplied a workable stopping point, which blunted that charge. A's opening was clipped for length, a real execution cost, but the surviving content plus strong follow-through in rebuttals and pressure answers outweighed that penalty. B was clear, disciplined, and rhetorically sharp, but left the implicit-premises and evidentiary-trap objections functionally unanswered, which was the decisive live issue by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's development of the "values vs. factual assumptions" distinction (A2.3-2.4, driven home in A4.4) and B's inability to answer it except by restricting correction to explicit written conditions was the most consequential exchange in the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully isolating a fatal flaw in the proposition's specific wording: that an exception strictly for "changed values" fails when a patient's values remain stable but their factual assumptions about dementia prove false. Side B defended the principle of prospective consent well, but Side A provided a more nuanced, workable standard for interpreting directives that Side B could only dismiss as paternalism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 argument (A4.4) demonstrating that the proposition's exception cannot correct "misfires" where implicit factual premises are falsified while underlying values remain unchanged.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON demonstrated that the proposition's exception for "materially changed values" is too narrow because directives can misfire when factual assumptions prove false even while underlying values remain stable, and mechanically enforcing them then does not honor autonomy. CON answered PRO's pressure questions with a precise, proportional framework—giving enormous weight to specific refusals of invasive care while permitting contextual review for vague language applied to low-burden treatments—and showed that sustained, treatment-specific assent is distinguishable from mere disease-driven reflex. PRO failed to rebut this proportionality, instead mischaracterizing CON in the closing as having "no threshold," and never justified why binding a patient to a choice based on false factual premises respects her autonomy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4), which directly answered both of PRO's pressure questions with a structured, workable proportionality test and exposed the asymmetry in PRO's treatment of uncertainty.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides ran a sophisticated version of the classic precedent-autonomy vs. then-self/now-self clash, with strong grounding and no fabricated evidence. CON's sharpest weapon was the claim that the "clear evidence of material change" exception is structurally unsatisfiable in severe dementia because the disease destroys the very capacities needed to produce it, making the safeguard illusory exactly where stakes are highest (untreated pneumonia, restraint, forced feeding). PRO answered this directly under pressure with a concrete, non-magical example (sustained, repeated, treatment-specific requests) showing the exception can function, and then neutralized CON's "err toward present suffering" intuition by pointing out that overriding a directive can itself impose suffering (restraint, invasive tubes), so the comparison of defaults is not as one-sided as CON assumed. PRO's closing reframe—that under CON's "strong evidence, not binding command" standard, ambiguous cases hand authority to clinicians/relatives with zero patient authorization, making the directive "weakest exactly when it must resist outsiders"—was a clean pivot that CON's final speech did not squarely rebut, instead mostly restating the structural-collapse point. CON's case remained forceful throughout and never fell apart, but PRO adapted more effectively across rounds, answered the toughest pressure question with a workable (not hand-wavy) standard, and ended with the more decisive unanswered reframe, giving it a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's pressure question (concrete example of sustained, treatment-specific lucid requests as qualifying "material change" evidence), combined with PRO's closing reframe that CON's rule creates an authority vacuum filled by outsiders precisely when the patient cannot defend herself—an argument CON's final speech left largely unanswered.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the choice not as 'document vs. patient' but as 'patient's competent choice vs. outsider conjecture.' While Side A landed excellent pressure on the unworkability of the 'clear evidence' safeguard in severe dementia, Side B neutralized Side A's emotional appeal by demonstrating that forced medical intervention also causes suffering, leaving the directive as the only principled tie-breaker.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly seized on Side A's concession that present distress is not consent to treatment, pointing out that Side A's framework would therefore subject the patient to unauthorized, suffering-inducing interventions based purely on clinician or family preference.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO defended a coherent framework for durable autonomy and exposed a critical gap in CON's alternative: since present distress is not consent, overriding the directive merely substitutes outsider judgment for the patient's own competent choice. CON's structural critique—that the material-change exception is impossible in severe dementia—was blunted by PRO's competent-revision route and concrete example of sustained conduct, leaving PRO's position more intact after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B5.1 and the closing authorization challenge: once CON concedes present distress is not consent, its "strong evidence" standard collapses into unprincipled substitution by clinicians or relatives without any actual patient authorization.
