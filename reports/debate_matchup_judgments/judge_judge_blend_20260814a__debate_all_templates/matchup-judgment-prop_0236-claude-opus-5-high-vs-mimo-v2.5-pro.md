### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): A won by preserving supervised, unaided assessments while showing that broad bans perform worst in the remaining take-home context: they encourage concealment, punish candor, and invite unreliable accusations. B’s educational-purpose and norm-setting case was substantial, but it too readily equated all AI assistance with cognitive outsourcing and did not overcome A’s rubric-and-carve-out answer. A also gave the cleaner comparison: partial, auditable visibility is better than total silence under either side’s imperfect enforcement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer to the underreporting challenge was decisive: the sophisticated student may disclose minimally, but that still puts AI use into a routine conversation and permits follow-up, whereas the same student under a ban reveals nothing. B showed that transparency is imperfect, but not that imperfect prohibition produces better oversight.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won because CON conceded that no perfect forensic test exists for take-home essays on connected devices, and PRO ruthlessly converted this into a comparative showing that broad bans are enforced only against the honest and the falsely flagged while rewarding concealment. CON’s principled case for independent cognitive labor remained articulate but could not overcome the practical reality that an unenforceable prohibition inverts norms, whereas default permissibility with disclosure creates partial visibility that permits coaching and honest conversation. PRO’s closing crystallization of this trade-off—accepting visible use over hidden use in the narrow space where bans are mere wishes—provided the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s inversion of CON’s norm argument in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, showing that an unenforceable ban punishes only candor and plain prose while turning institutional integrity into a bluff.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): PRO wins the enforceability and equity clash that CON eventually concedes at B4.1. Both sides converge on in-class/supervised assessments as the core, leaving take-home work where bans produce concealment and false positives that hit ESL writers hardest — a cost CON never squarely answers until a weak both-sides equivalence. A's boomerang — partial disclosure beats total silence, turning accusation into conversation — directly answers B's underreporting pressure, while B's norms argument is reversed by A: an unenforceable ban teaches that honesty is punished.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Rebuttal 1 A2.3-A2.4 and Rebuttal 2 A4.1 — auditing concealment is impossible vs auditing disclosure is hard, false disclosure is written confession vs false denial has no defense, and partial visibility beats none.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): B won by showing that defaults matter most when teachers lack time: restriction protects foundational practice automatically, while A requires overloaded teachers to prohibit AI assignment by assignment. A's transparency model remained unverifiable, and its claim that fabricated logs still create learning was decisively answered. A offered a credible realism case, but B successfully turned that premise against A. A also suffered more clipped turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange exposed the tension between A's teacher-capacity argument and its implementation. A admitted a novice gets AI unless the teacher specifies unaided stages; B showed this demands precisely the repeated intervention that A says exhausted teachers cannot reliably provide.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): CON won by turning PRO's institutional-realism argument on its head. PRO conceded that a permissive default and restriction-by-default are structurally similar, differing only in burden placement. CON showed this difference is decisive: under PRO's policy, exhausted teachers must actively restrict AI assignment-by-assignment, so unattended work defaults to machine-generated output and lost skill-building. CON also collapsed PRO's verification case by showing prompt logs are trivially fabricated, and hung PRO on the concession that a fourteen-year-old's first essay gets AI by default unless a teacher intervenes [B4.3]. PRO's policy survived the opening but could not recover after its strongest claims were inverted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's inversion of PRO's "realism" and burden-concession into an argument that unattended assignments under a permissive default guarantee skill erosion, while restriction-by-default protects the skill when teachers lack capacity to intervene.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON won the burden/default clash. PRO conceded restriction-by-default is "functionally the same structure" [A2.4] and that a 14-year-old's first essay gets AI unless teacher adds in-class drafting [A4.3] — which is CON's policy but with per-assignment opt-out cost on overloaded teachers. B turned PRO's realism premise against it: if teachers lack capacity, unattended work defaults to machine under PRO. B also neutralized transparency by showing prompt-log fabrication is one more prompt, and PRO admitted perfect fakes are accepted [A4.1], leaving only honest students forfeiting practice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1/R2 exposure that defaults win by exhaustion and that PRO's answers to Q1/Q2 concede perfect fabrication is accepted and that foundational work needs restriction, making PRO's permissive default self-defeating under its own teacher-overload premise.
