### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): B won a close debate by consistently distinguishing regulation of harmful working conditions from an AI-triggered bargaining process. A compellingly established the value of worker knowledge and predeployment constraints, but often assumed negotiation would produce protective clauses despite disclaiming any council veto. B showed that enforceable consultation and technology-neutral limits could preserve worker input while avoiding false consent, representation conflicts, and arbitrary differences between identical human- and software-imposed harms. A’s clipped second rebuttal was also a minor execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to the fatigue example was decisive: if identical shift intensification is harmful whether imposed by a manager, spreadsheet, or model, the legal protection should attach to workload and staffing rather than the technology used. A offered opacity and contestability as distinctions, but did not fully show why those require negotiation rather than enforceable consultation, audit, and direct limits.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully defending why AI specifically requires new bargaining triggers, effectively contrasting the speed and specificity of local worker councils against Side B's reliance on slow, general national regulators. Side B had a strong analytical objection about regulating the harm rather than the tool, but Side A cleanly dispatched this by framing AI as an opaque 'loophole' that evades traditional management accountability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized Side B's legitimation argument by pointing out that a negotiated agreement is exactly what gives workers a concrete, enforceable clause to point to, rather than relying on abstract regulatory standards.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): CON won a competitive debate by locking the decisive clash on mechanism: once both sides accepted real harms and the need for enforceable limits on intensification/use, CON showed the motion’s AI-deployment trigger is unprincipled (identical effects lawful via manager/spreadsheet, unlawful via model), creates hold-up and false-consent risk, and is inferior to condition-level standards plus enforceable consultation and existing bargaining. PRO adapted well—auditors vs deployment, pace/granularity of workplace clauses, loophole framing—and asked sharp pressure questions, but never cleanly escaped the arbitrary-trigger problem; the clipped Rebuttal 2 also cost execution clarity. CON’s consistent weighing made the vote easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s fatigue-prediction rebuttal (B2.1–B2.2): accurate unbiased auto-assignment is intensification, not an AI-unique harm, so a software trigger is arbitrary.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): A won narrowly by showing that universal legal floors and workplace negotiation are complementary, not exclusive. B's rights-versus-chips framing was memorable, but it repeatedly compared bargaining to an ideal regulator capable of prescribing every contextual pace, scoring, and surveillance choice. A better defended the real function of councils: discovering harms before deployment and constraining lawful discretion without waiving statutory rights. B's outsider and expertise objections remained meaningful, while its clipped second rebuttal imposed a small execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest response was that bargaining cannot waive discrimination or safety floors and instead governs the contextual discretionary zone that general regulation cannot fully specify. This materially weakened B's liability-shield and purchasable-rights framing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully framing negotiation as a process that turns fundamental rights into bargaining chips. B's pressure questions expertly trapped A into conceding that councils might trade away deeper monitoring for safeguards, which B then weaponized to prove the model makes protections purchasable rather than universal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which seized on Side A's admission about accepting broader keystroke monitoring to argue that A's scheme allows employers to buy deeper surveillance while turning baseline protections into conditional concessions.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): B won the decisive clash: once A conceded councils can accept broader monitoring than a regulator would allow, B locked the frame that the motion makes core safeguards bargainable chips rather than unconditional floors, and that coverage fails for outsiders and subcontracted workers. A's baseline-power reply was real but never fully overcame B's "if you can compel those terms, legislate them" comparison. B's clipped R2 was a cost, not fatal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B flipping A's A1 answer on tradable keystroke monitoring into "protections become purchasable" (B4.4/B5.3).
