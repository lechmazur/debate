### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a competitive but clear debate on the strength of two arguments that CON never fully neutralized. First, the ecosystem-fracture point introduced in [B1.4] and sharpened in [B2.3]: under CON's tiered model, a predator blocked by a paying user remains free to target every free user, meaning the paywall actively circulates harm rather than containing it. CON's best answer in [A4.1]—that basic reporting flags the same scammer platform-wide after initial blocks—conflates reactive flagging with preventative access and doesn't resolve the structural gap. Second, PRO's answer to CON's Q1 in [B4.1] draws a clean, defensible line: free tools are reactive (act after harm), paywalled tools are preventative (stop harm before contact). This directly answers CON's definitional pressure without circularity. CON's core economic argument—that premiums sustainably fund safety innovation—is plausible and not wholly answered, and the abuse/overload concern is genuine, but CON never quantifies why safety verification specifically is expensive relative to total platform revenue, leaving PRO's "marginal cost" counterpoint standing. CON's closing reiterates the funding argument competently but doesn't sharpen the comparison or land a new blow on PRO's live ecosystem-fracture point. PRO's shorter closing is thin but adequately crystallizes the decisive clash. The moral clarity of PRO's framing ("profit from preventable harm") combined with the unresolved network-effect argument carries the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's [B2.3] network-effect attack is the most decisive exchange: when a paying user blocks a scammer, that scammer remains free to target free users, directly contradicting CON's claim that tiered safety protects the ecosystem. CON's response in [A4.1] substitutes post-hoc platform-wide reporting for the preventative access at issue, leaving PRO's structural critique live through the closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by establishing a clear, principled boundary between reactive baseline tools and preventative safety infrastructure, then showing that tiering the latter leaves free users exposed to predators that paying users merely block, fracturing collective security. While Side A raised credible economic concerns about costs and abuse, it never fully dismantled B’s core distinction or resolved the loophole of predator migration; instead, A largely repeated generic cost and market arguments that B had already framed as secondary to the non-negotiable priority of universal prevention.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.2), which answered CON’s pressure question by defining the boundary as reactive versus preventative safety, then leveraged that line to show that basic free blocking only catalogs harm after it occurs while identity verification and scam-response stop predators before they reach users. This reframe crystallized the clash and made it much harder for CON to treat advanced safety as a mere premium luxury.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): PRO wins by successfully framing safety as essential platform infrastructure rather than a premium enhancement, and by landing a concrete, damaging clash point: paywalling safety creates a porous ecosystem where predators blocked by paying users simply migrate to free users. CON's economic defense (premiums fund backend moderation and prevent overload) was competent but never fully neutralized the intuitive and practical harm of tiered protection. PRO also handled pressure questions more cleanly, drawing a sharp reactive-vs-preventative boundary and using a spam-filter analogy to ground verification costs as baseline integrity rather than optional upgrades. CON's reliance on generic opportunity-cost and market-incentive arguments felt less responsive to the specific stakes of the motion, and the claim that safety should "scale with commitment" weakened its rhetorical positioning on a harm-prevention topic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 directly answered CON's pressure questions by distinguishing reactive (free) from preventative (paid) tools and comparing verification costs to standard spam filters. This effectively reframed safety expenses as baseline platform integrity, undercut CON's funding argument, and kept the debate focused on the motion's core harm rather than abstract economic trade-offs.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO)): PRO built the stronger cumulative case for three reasons. First, the perverse-incentives argument [B1.2]—that platforms profit by keeping baseline safety just inadequate enough to push upgrades—was introduced early, repeated through every round, and never squarely neutralized by CON. CON's responses focused on sustainability funding but never grappled with why the incentive structure itself is corrupted. Second, CON's equity counter ("targeted subsidies") [A1.3] was invoked in every speech but never developed into a concrete mechanism, leaving it as a rhetorical placeholder rather than a genuine rebuttal; PRO's Q2 in the Pressure Questions round exposed this gap, and CON's A2 [A4.3] merely acknowledged it was "slower" without addressing why platforms with profit motives would ever implement it. Third, PRO effectively neutralized CON's bot-flood argument [A1.2][A2.3] by noting that paywalls already fail to stop sophisticated scam farms while systematically excluding low-income users from protection—a targeted, comparative rebuttal rather than mere assertion. CON's strongest original move was the arms-race distinction [A2.5], correctly noting cybersecurity requires continuous adaptive investment unlike static food-safety standards, and its accountability argument [A4.1]—that free features decouple safety from a direct revenue incentive—was genuinely interesting. But CON never landed the comparative weight of these points, delivering shorter speeches throughout, repeating its core claim without deepening it, and closing with a near-verbatim restatement of the opening. PRO's closing crystallized the key clash more sharply: the choice is between a model that treats vulnerability as revenue versus one that treats safety as infrastructure, and paid tiers for visibility/analytics survive either way.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's [B2.2] response to CON's bot-overload panic: paywalls have demonstrably not stopped sophisticated scam networks that absorb minor fees as a cost of doing business, so the only population they reliably filter out is legitimate low-income users seeking protection—inverting CON's claimed safety benefit into a direct equity harm. CON never recovered a clean answer to this inversion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger path to victory by showing that gating core safety tools behind paywalls deliberately stratifies protection, exploiting low-income and high-risk users as unprotected bait while platforms could fund baseline safety through existing ad revenue and non-safety premium features like visibility boosts. CON’s funding argument remained live but was weakened by its failure to explain why safety must specifically be the premium revenue stream rather than paid conveniences, and by its admission that targeted subsidies would be slower and less universal than a free default. PRO’s rebuttal that paywalls block poor humans but not determined scammers, combined with effective equity framing, gave the cleaner comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 response to CON’s bot-overwhelm concern, arguing that universal free verification eliminates anonymous defaults while rate-limiting and AI checks prevent system overload, whereas nominal paywalls only exclude legitimate low-income users rather than scripted fraud farms—directly answering CON’s pressure questions and turning the security argument against stratified safety.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins by successfully framing the core clash around incentive structures and equity. While CON (Side A) raised valid concerns about sustainable funding and bot friction, PRO effectively neutralized the bot argument by noting that professional scammers easily absorb nominal fees, rendering paywalls ineffective as security filters. PRO's argument that gating safety creates a perverse incentive to deliberately under-protect free users to drive upgrades proved more decisive than CON's reliance on corporate budgeting mechanics. CON's alternative of targeted subsidies remained vague and conceded slowness, failing to match the immediacy of PRO's free-by-default model. PRO's closing crystallized the moral and practical stakes more memorably, securing the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to the bot-overload concern, which dismantled CON's paywall-as-friction argument by explaining that determined scammers treat small fees as marketing costs, while universal free verification actually shrinks the anonymous attacker pool. This shifted the technical clash in PRO's favor and undermined CON's primary practical objection.
