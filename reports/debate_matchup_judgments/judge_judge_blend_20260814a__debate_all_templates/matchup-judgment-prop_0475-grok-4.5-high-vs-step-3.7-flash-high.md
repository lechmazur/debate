### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, competitive cases, but PRO's central reframe—that subsidies already pick winners, so inaction is not a neutral baseline—was never squarely dismantled by CON, and it exposed CON's "no safe timeline" stance as a de facto permanent freeze. CON's strongest material (LMIC smallholder food security, the urban/rural infrastructure mismatch) remained a live and forceful concern that PRO only partially answered with underspecified "rural siting bonus" mechanisms, but CON weakened its own grounding by repeatedly asserting a precise, uncited "3 to 5 times more expensive" figure as settled fact rather than a plausible approximation, which reads as brittle fake precision. In the pressure round, CON's A1 answer sidestepped the direct "how many years" question with a categorical refusal rather than engaging the timeline logic, a modest evasion PRO exploited well in its closing. PRO's closing crystallized the fiscal-lever framing cleanly, while CON's closing, though vivid, mostly restated prior claims without resolving the freeze-forever critique. The result is a close but clear edge to PRO on framing discipline and rebuttal leverage, even though CON's substantive food-security and infrastructure objections keep this from being a rout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's "freeze-forever" reframe in Rebuttal 2/Closing, arguing CON's demand for pre-existing price parity and rural infrastructure before any reallocation would forever block redirection, was the most damaging unanswered pressure point, since CON's response to the parallel pressure question never offered a concrete threshold or timeline, only a categorical refusal.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully framed the debate as a choice between locked-in high-emissions subsidization and a directed shift that could scale low-emissions alternatives, while rebutting CON's food-security fears as a prescription for indefinite delay. CON landed strong blows on smallholder vulnerability and energy costs, but PRO's answers to pressure questions—showing subsidy design itself could mandate rural placement and expand capacity—kept their core causal story alive more cleanly than CON neutralized it. PRO's sharper rhetorical framing and more coherent progression across rounds gave them a narrow but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal and Q2 pressure question exposing that CON's pastoral-system defense implicitly conceded industrial feedlots should lose support, undermining CON's blanket opposition to a subsidy shift while reframing CON's objections as a "freeze forever" strategy.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built the live path: subsidies already pick winners, so shifting picks lower-emissions winners. CON never supplied a start timeline, conceding indefinite high-emissions support, and never resolved Q2 contradiction that most emissions come from industrial lots it defends. PRO answered capacity and rural-siting pressure directly with location-weighted grants and volume-risk buy-down, while CON repeated stranding claims and 3-5x price without weighing future food-security risk from climate shocks to feed/pasture.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's freeze-strategy rebuttal in A4.3 plus A2.4 - CON demands pre-existing billion-scale capacity and spontaneous rural placement before any shift, turning temporary gaps into permanent excuses; soil carbon cannot offset enteric methane at subsidized herd scale.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides fought a substantive, well-structured debate, but A sustained the more decisive throughline: precision fermentation currently supplies ingredients, not the complete, culturally central proteins (steak, milk, eggs, cheese) that anchor food security, and B never squarely closed that gap despite repeated chances. B's Rebuttal 1 was clipped for length, an execution cost, and B's Rebuttal 2 leaned on several precise, unsupported figures (80-90% cuts generalized to whole diets, 8-9x land/water efficiency, "majority of 70%") introduced or reasserted late without engaging A's whole-food objection directly. A's pressure question on this point was answered only obliquely by B's "already core of commercial whole products" claim, which A effectively exposed as still niche in Rebuttal 2 and Closing. A, meanwhile, left B's point about industrial feedlots (not smallholders) absorbing most current subsidies largely unanswered, which is a live concession against A, but A's central prudence/food-security frame proved more resilient overall and was better defended under direct questioning.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's repeated insistence, unrebutted in substance by B, that precision fermentation remains an ingredient technology rather than a whole-food replacement (raised in Opening, sharpened via Pressure Q2, and driven home in Rebuttal 2/Closing) was the most decisive exchange, since B's answer relied on an unsupported assertion about existing "whole products at scale" that was never substantiated.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully reframed the debate by showing that the status quo subsidizes industrial feedlots more than small family farms, and that climate-driven agricultural shocks pose a greater long-term threat to food security than a managed transition. While CON maintained effective pressure on the ingredient-versus-whole-food gap and the epistemic risk of undefined far more reallocation, PROs efficiency and just-transition answers created a cleaner comparative path. CONs incremental alternative was shown to be insufficient against catastrophic warming, and its equity narrative was materially weakened by PROs industrial-feedlot subsidy point. The clipped speech in Turn 3 was a real execution cost, but PRO recovered in later rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PROs second rebuttal directly answering CONs pressure questions by demonstrating that the bulk of livestock subsidies flows to large industrial operations rather than smallholders, while precision fermentation yields vastly more protein per unit land and water, collapsing CONs food security and rural livelihood objections in one exchange.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): CON kept the decisive clash on national-scale complete-protein volumes, prices, and specialized livestock incomes alive. PRO's core emissions advantage (14.5% and 80-90% cuts) was conceded as ingredient-level but never proved to carry steak/milk/eggs/cheese at national scale, and admitting 'far more = majority of 70%' reinforced the starvation risk. First rebuttal clip and weak feed-cost logic cost PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 reply that 80-90% applies to narrow ingredients, not dinner-table proteins, and that sugar feedstocks plus bioreactor energy and rural infrastructure leave a gap that PRO's 'whole products at scale' claim did not fill.
