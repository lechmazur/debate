### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): PRO wins a close but real contest on the strength of two durable arguments. First, the incentive-shift case: verification authority does not require the WHO to collect tariffs; the existing IHR framework already allows member states to impose travel and trade restrictions once concealment is confirmed, so transparency becomes the least-bad strategic option even for authoritarian regimes. Second, the "absence is diagnostic" move in Rebuttal 2 credibly answered CON's stonewalling objection—locked labs and missing sequences are themselves forensic signals that trigger scrutiny. PRO's Q1 also exposed a genuine inconsistency in CON's case: if the WHO truly has no enforcement teeth, it is hard to explain why states would exit rather than absorb harmless inspections. CON never resolved this tension cleanly. CON's strongest sustained point—"visibility ≠ velocity," that forensic detection cannot outrun exponential spread—was real and never fully neutralized by PRO, and CON's rebuttal work was generally tighter and more disciplined. CON also correctly noted that PRO never provided a mechanism to distinguish calibration from suppression in real time, and CON answered Q2 more honestly than PRO answered the velocity objection. However, CON's alternative (resources and incentives) remained underdeveloped throughout, and the internal consistency problem with the exit/toothless-inspection tension weakened its core causal story. PRO's two clipped speeches are genuine execution costs that dragged down its grounding and rebuttal scores, but the core incentive-shift argument and the IHR clarification survived the exchange intact, giving PRO the cleaner path to a vote on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 was the most decisive exchange: by pointing out that if WHO truly lacks enforcement teeth, states have no reason to exit rather than absorb toothless inspections, PRO forced CON into an uncomfortable position. CON's answer—that states exit because of sovereignty sacrifice, not material punishment—was plausible but underdeveloped, and it left the internal consistency problem unresolved for the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): B won by making the practical clash clearer: legal verification access does not automatically produce timely outbreak intelligence when WHO still depends on national ministries for labs, staff, security, and records. B’s cooperation-versus-coercion frame, especially the claim that adversarial access may reduce early disclosure and slow the very surveillance needed for containment, was better answered and better weighed. A also incurred some execution cost from clipped speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive rebuttal was the “visibility versus velocity” answer: even if locked doors, missing samples, or genomic gaps eventually reveal concealment, that may come too late for exponential outbreak containment, while coercive access risks undermining the local cooperation WHO needs for real-time information.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): Side B wins by establishing a clearer, more resilient path to victory: verification shifts state incentives via IHR-triggered penalties and forensic detection of absences, directly countering past cover-ups and addressing pressure questions on enforcement without relying on perfect access. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): PRO won by establishing a cleaner causal account of pandemic failure and by effectively turning CON's best example. The decisive exchange was over the WHO's amplification of Beijing's "no clear evidence of human transmission" claim. CON introduced this as proof of WHO capture by donor-state politics; PRO countered that the WHO lacked any independent data source to contradict Beijing—exactly what stronger verification access would provide. This turn is causally tight: if the mechanism of failure was absence of independent sampling and on-the-ground access, the motion directly addresses it; CON's "budget capture" explanation does not explain why independent laboratory findings would still be suppressed. PRO also handled CON's perverse-incentives argument credibly: concealment is already the dominant strategy under the status quo and already goes unpunished, so the status quo is not obviously preferable to a regime that at least raises the cost of lying. CON's strongest live argument—that early noisy data locked into a WHO declaration triggers irreversible economic contagion—was real and partially answered, but PRO's reply (that unverified uncertainty already produces chaotic unilateral border closures) was a defensible and underaddressed response. CON's structural problem was that its main supporting example (the WHO's early COVID statements) was turned into evidence for the other side and never fully recovered. PRO answered the pressure questions on format with A1/A2 labels and directly engaged the sovereignty-veto point by noting the motion itself changes the default from favor to treaty obligation. CON's rebuttals were engaged and clearly constructed, but they circled the same trio of arguments (friction, noise, capture) without narrowing the gap on the core causal clash. The clipping of Side A's Turn 3 was a minor execution cost that did not materially change the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q2 (A4.3): CON argued the WHO's amplification of Beijing's false claim proved institutional capture making stronger access useless; PRO replied that the WHO repeated the falsehood precisely because it lacked independent access and had no data of its own to contradict the host narrative. This directly inverted CON's strongest example and made it support the motion rather than oppose it—the clearest single argumentative turn in the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): A won by making the core tradeoff clearer: early national messaging control has repeatedly enabled suppression, while stronger WHO access is a targeted check on that failure. B had plausible concerns about noisy data, panic, and institutional capture, but often overstated them as automatic sanctions or total WHO narrative control, and its alternative of incentives and trust stayed vague. A also better used the motion: if members grant stronger access, B's repeated “host consent” objection loses force, and the Beijing “no clear evidence” example was persuasively turned into evidence that weak verification caused the problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered B's pressure by arguing that preauthorized treaty access removes the national veto rather than adding a second one, and turned B's WHO-capture example into a reason for independent verification rather than against it.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins with a narrow edge because it more effectively tied repeated historical failures of national concealment (Wuhan, SARS) to the need for independent verification and directly answered pressure questions by explaining how stronger access would enable cross-checking rather than parroting, while Side A's bureaucracy, lock-in, and capture concerns remained less decisive after rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answers (A1 and A2) that treaty protocols remove the national veto and stronger access prevents host-state capture of the WHO.
