### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): PRO built a cleaner path to victory across the full exchange. The opening gave three organized, weighted arguments (lock-in, ownership, competition) with grounded analogies. The strategic pivot in Rebuttal 1 from noisy star ratings to verified work history (trips, cancellation rate, incident flags) was a genuine narrowing of the debate rather than an evasion, and PRO correctly defended that verified behavioral metrics ARE meaningful signals independent of star context. CON's strongest contribution was the deactivation dilemma (port sanitized record = safety risk; port flag = industry blacklist), and CON's response to PRO's "worker-controlled disclosure" third option—that selective editability breaks verification integrity—was technically valid and partially exposed a gap in PRO's design. However, CON never overcame PRO's core framing: the underlying injustice of reputation lock-in was never actually defended, only the implementation objections were raised. PRO's closing crystallized this as "hard is not the same as wrong," and CON's case collapsed into "the fix is imperfect." PRO's pressure question about incumbents lobbying against portability was a genuine contradiction CON never cleanly resolved—the compliance-burden answer was plausible but didn't explain why giants would oppose a tool that (per CON's own logic) only benefits them through regulatory moat. CON's shadow-rating argument in Rebuttal 2 was speculative and introduced late without strong grounding. PRO's phone-number portability and banking analogies were grounded in ordinary background knowledge without brittle precision. The margin is real but competitive because CON's verification-integrity and deactivation points landed genuine pressure that PRO only partially answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's deactivation dilemma [B2.5] versus PRO's "worker-controlled disclosure" response [A3.2], and then CON's counter [B4.2] that user-editable records break the verification chain. CON correctly identified a genuine technical tension: a standardized, trusted credential cannot simultaneously be edited to hide negative safety flags without undermining the trust it is supposed to establish. PRO never fully resolved this, though it was adequate enough that the exchange did not flip the outcome.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the debate around worker ownership and separating noisy ratings from factual work history. Side A mounted a strong defense regarding compliance moats and the dilemma of selective disclosure, but Side B effectively categorized these as solvable engineering challenges rather than reasons to accept the status quo.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's distinction in Rebuttal 1 between noisy star ratings and objective, verified work history (e.g., cancellation rates) neutralized Side A's strongest opening argument about context mismatch.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO won by sharpening the distinction between noisy star ratings and portable verified work history, which defused CON's category-error opening and established a coherent injustice narrative around lock-in. CON's closing recycled already-answered objections and overstated the uselessness of decontextualized verified metrics, while failing to explain why giants would fight a supposedly pro-incumbent mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's first rebuttal [B2.2-B2.3] distinguishing unportable star ratings from portable verified work history (trips, cancellations, safety records) was the decisive rebuttal exchange. It neutralized CON's core category-error attack, absorbed the "misleading noise" objection, and re-centered the debate on factual behavioral signals that retain value across platforms, providing the foundation that sustained PRO's case through later rounds.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): CON (Side B) won on the strength of a single, sharp analytical argument that PRO never fully neutralized: transferred ratings must be either (a) meaningful enough to bypass cold-start friction or (b) neutral enough not to corrupt receiving platforms' quality control, but cannot simultaneously be both. PRO's best response—the credit-report analogy, where historical data informs but does not dictate lending decisions—was a genuine attempt at resolution, but CON's closing correctly answered that trip-count alone does not unlock dispatch priority and incentive-tier access, which is the actual cold-start penalty. PRO's "verified work history = objective trip count" argument was underexplored and underdelivered. CON also pressed a live governance problem (no misconduct registry, no audit mechanism in the motion's text) that PRO waved away with "appeals mechanisms like credit reports," an answer that conceded the gap without filling it. The multi-homing tension—workers already freely switching apps undermines the lock-in narrative—was never cleanly resolved by PRO; the explanation that cold-start penalties make multi-homing an unpaid grind is true but left standing alongside PRO's own claim that the labor is commoditized, which CON repeatedly leveraged. PRO's narrative (digital serfdom, data captivity, worker ownership of reputation) was emotionally compelling and launched the debate well, but the analytical core progressively weakened under CON's pressure while CON's incompatibility argument only sharpened across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 crystallization of the incompatibility dilemma: if the 4.95 rating functions as a real quality signal it imports another platform's evaluation culture, but if receiving platforms can discount it the cold-start friction is unresolved. PRO's credit-report answer was the closest escape, but CON's closing correctly closed it by noting trip count doesn't unlock the dispatch-priority and incentive tiers where cold-start friction actually lives.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully dismantling Side A's central dilemma. Side A argued that ported data must either be weighted determinatively (corrupting quality control) or ignored (rendering the mandate useless). Side B resolved this false binary by comparing ported gig data to credit histories and focusing on objective "verified work history" (e.g., trip counts), plausibly arguing that competing platforms would voluntarily use this data to attract experienced workers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 effectively used the credit history analogy to show how ported data can bypass the cold-start penalty without forcing receiving platforms to abandon their own quality control.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON demonstrated a fatal, unresolved design tension in PRO's case: ported ratings must be simultaneously meaningful enough to overcome cold-start penalties and neutral enough not to corrupt receiving platforms' quality control. PRO never resolved whether receiving platforms would be required to weight imported data meaningfully or merely acknowledge it, and CON's multi-homing double-bind and context-specificity objections withstood PRO's rebuttals. PRO's moral case was rhetorically compelling but could not overcome the technical incoherence of the mandate as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's identification that PRO needed transferred ratings to be both operationally meaningful in dispatch systems and yet neutral enough not to import foreign evaluation cultures, exposing this as an impossible contradiction that the motion's text could not resolve.
