### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Both sides argued competently throughout, but Side B (PRO) holds a clear edge on three counts. First, PRO's burden-flip argument—that a consent requirement forces companies to prove permission rather than forcing grieving families to litigate violations—was introduced in Rebuttal 2 and sharpened in the closing, and CON never directly answered it. This is the cleanliest route to victory remaining after the full exchange. Second, PRO's "permanent dignity versus temporary delay" framing is a genuine comparative weighing tool that CON's case never neutralized: the harms CON feared (probate delay, estate gatekeeping) are temporary and workable, while the harms PRO targeted (a voice sold without any consent) are irreversible. Third, PRO's opening scenario was more grounded and concrete than CON's, setting an emotional and moral baseline the whole debate orbited. CON's strongest move was the "fatal contradiction" attack in Rebuttal 2 and the closing—that flexible consent is toothless while strict consent is heartless—and this put real pressure on PRO. PRO's answer was adequate but not airtight: the liability-without-consent claim assumes enforceability that CON correctly questioned. However, CON's own "existing laws suffice" argument was undermined by PRO's litigation-burden point, and CON never fully recovered. On balance, PRO built the cleaner path to a vote: modest, flexible consent still shifts the default burden onto companies, and that shift is the only mechanism preventing exploitation without banning private grief.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 response to CON's "existing laws suffice" argument [A1.4/B2.4] was the most decisive exchange. PRO pointed out that right-of-publicity claims require a grieving spouse to hire lawyers and sue a tech startup—a realistic bar that CON's framework silently assumes can be cleared. CON's later counter (A2.5) tried to mirror the critique back (new consent mandate has the same enforcement problem), but PRO's closing answered it cleanly with the burden-flip: a consent requirement means the company must establish permission before acting, not the family after the fact. CON never addressed this asymmetry directly, leaving PRO's route to victory on enforcement alive through the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): B won by making the motion’s blanket consent requirement look practically unstable: strict estate consent delays or blocks acute private mourning, while A’s later flexible versions weakened the protective force of consent. A was strong on dignity and commercialization, but repeatedly treated the motion as mainly about corporations and gave an unconvincing answer to private offline memorial use.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strict-versus-flexible dilemma in Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange: A tried to solve probate delay with informal or presumed consent, and B persuasively argued that this either fails to stop exploitation or restores the bureaucracy A was trying to avoid.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): A defended the proposition more convincingly by showing that a consent requirement—even with flexible, informal mechanisms—flips the burden onto companies and prevents unchecked commodification of intimate identity. B landed a sharp analytical frame exposing tensions between rigid and flexible consent standards, but never squarely answered A's core critique that existing law forces grieving families into expensive reactive litigation while leaving commercial exploitation unaddressed. A's closing crystallized the tradeoff as permanent dignity versus temporary convenience, maintaining a clearer path to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2, which exposed the tension in A's attempt to make consent both flexible enough for grieving families and robust enough to stop corporate abuse, forcing A into a defensive posture on enforcement.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON (Side A) held the edge across the full exchange primarily by sustaining a concrete-versus-speculative harm comparison that PRO never adequately neutralized. The "grieving widow waiting months for distant cousins to approve a voice she wants to hear once" narrative was vivid and specific, and it ran from opening through closing. PRO's responses in Rebuttal 2—that standard probate defaults to the surviving spouse and that this constitutes protection rather than delay—were logically sound but conceded the very estate-mechanism CON was criticizing, and PRO could not explain why the widow still would not face a process she never asked for. CON's alternative safeguards argument (opt-out settings, platform labeling, digital wills) remained a live route that PRO attacked but never dismantled; PRO's "opt-out means exploit by default" rebuttal was strong in principle but CON correctly pointed out that pre-mortem consent tools already exist and that platform-level policy can bear the protective load without estate gatekeeping. PRO's own pressure Q2—most people die without digital wills—was the toughest moment for CON, and CON's answer was somewhat vague, but the round did not flip the balance of the wider clash. PRO's "exploit by default" framing was its strongest rhetorical contribution and prevented this from being a runaway, but CON's consistent weighing of certain, immediate harm against speculative future exploitation gave it the cleaner comparative path throughout the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4) answering PRO's A1 and A2: CON turned PRO's own defense of estate consent against it, pointing out that confirming "estate default = mechanism" simply restates the bureaucratic lock CON had been attacking all along, and that even a well-intentioned probate process converts a personal act of mourning into a legal filing the widow never asked for. This exchange forced PRO to defend the very delay that is CON's central harm claim, and PRO never found a way out of that bind.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by making the default-state comparison clearer: without consent, intimate voice and personality traits become available for unauthorized simulation by relatives, strangers, or platforms. A's estate-default answer was imperfect but more responsive and better tied to the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's attack on opt-out as "exploit by default" was decisive, especially because B's later A1/A2 did not directly answer the legal-shield and no-digital-will questions.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side B won by demonstrating that the consent requirement creates concrete, immediate harm to grieving families through bureaucratic estate gatekeeping and probate delays, while the opt-out safeguards they proposed offered a less invasive alternative.
