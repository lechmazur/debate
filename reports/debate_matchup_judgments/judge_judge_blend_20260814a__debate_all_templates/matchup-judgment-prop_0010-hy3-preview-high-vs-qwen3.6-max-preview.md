Side A's sharpest contribution was the bilateral-privacy innovation—if NDAs become unenforceable, employers lose their obligation to stay silent too, leaving victims exposed to leaks—but B's answer (defamation statutes, reputational incentives) was adequate if imperfect, and B correctly noted that invoking defamation requires victims to sue the same employer A called too powerful to fight, a tension A never resolved. B's core settlement argument held: public trials carry brand damage, punitive exposure, and regulatory scrutiny that dwarf settlement costs, so the incentive to settle does not disappear with gag-order enforceability. A's narrow-reforms path was the most competitive counter—targeted regulatory carve-outs and mandatory counsel are real tools—but B successfully narrowed the gap: those reforms still prohibit victims from directly warning colleagues about known abusers, the scenario most concretely motivating the motion. B's opening was fuller and set the stronger framing; A's speeches were consistently shorter than the cap, leaving some burdens underdeveloped. On balance, B held the heavier unresolved clash (serial-abuser structural loophole) and adequately answered A's two pressure questions, while A's bilateral-privacy point, though the debate's single most original rebuttal, fell short of defeating B's systemic argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): PRO won the debate's decisive clash: the severability question. PRO drew a principled and legally grounded distinction between clauses silencing victims about unlawful conduct (void) and clauses protecting victim identity, payment terms, and personal records (enforceable). Courts routinely sever unconscionable provisions while preserving the rest of a contract, and PRO applied that doctrine cleanly throughout. CON's counter — that the motion voids entire NDAs, so no enforceable portion can remain — is a plausible textual reading but ultimately a technical dodge of the real policy question. No serious reform proposal voids entire settlement agreements; the debate was clearly about the silencing function, and PRO engaged that substance while CON retreated to a literalism that sidestepped it. On the leverage flip, CON's resource-gap argument had real force — most victims cannot afford protracted litigation — but PRO's response that it is the employer's cost of delay and discovery exposure, not the victim's endurance, that drives settlement behavior was adequate and not fully neutralized. CON's strongest live line — "fewer victims will report if privacy isn't guaranteed" — was meaningfully answered: under PRO's framework, victim identity and financial terms remain enforceable, so the privacy cost is smaller than CON claimed. CON never recovered from failing to engage severability on its merits, repeating the "entire NDA is void" framing across Rebuttal 2 and Closing without addressing why partial unenforceability is legally unavailable. PRO's systemic accountability impact — serial abusers cycling between companies on clean references — also went largely unanswered and compounded CON's deficit. The clipped Rebuttal 2 is a minor cost but PRO had already landed the key answers before the clip.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2, invoking standard severability doctrine to show that voiding the abuse-silencing gag does not detonate payment or victim-confidentiality clauses, directly collapsed CON's central privacy-loss and litigation-drag arguments. CON's response in its own Rebuttal 2 merely reasserted that the motion targets entire NDAs rather than engaging the severability principle on its merits, leaving PRO's answer standing as the cleaner resolution of the pivotal clash.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): A won because it made the cleaner distinction between survivor privacy and employer-purchased silence, and used severability plus corporate risk incentives to blunt B's core settlement-collapse story. B raised a serious victim-choice objection, but overclaimed that unenforceability of NDAs necessarily destroys all settlement protections and that employers settle only for silence. A better preserved the proposition while showing why hidden patterns of abuse create broader workplace safety harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response that voiding the abuse gag need not void payment or victim-confidentiality terms was the decisive exchange, because it undercut B's privacy-loss and reduced-settlement impacts at their root.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B wins by making the decisive clash clearer around partial severability versus total contract collapse, directly answering pressure questions in Rebuttal 2 to show how privacy and payouts can survive while removing the misconduct gag; this neutralizes A's core privacy and litigation drag burdens more effectively than A's concrete victim impact arguments, which did not sufficiently engage the severance response or weigh systemic prevention against individual settlement speed.
