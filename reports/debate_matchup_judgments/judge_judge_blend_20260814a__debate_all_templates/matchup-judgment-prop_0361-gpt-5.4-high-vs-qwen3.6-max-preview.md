### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A wins on the strength of two arguments that Side B never squarely neutralized. First, the "accounting illusion" rebuttal: Side A correctly identified that "full margin on a much cheaper product" is not obviously superior to a smaller share of a premium-priced transaction. Side B responded with "subscriber value compounds" and "three months outpaces a single ticket," but neither claim was grounded beyond assertion, and Side A had already flagged that diffuse platform attribution cannot reliably prove title-specific value. Second, and more decisively, Side A's day-and-date trap (Q2 in the pressure round) exposed a genuine logical gap in CON's case. If "peak attention" makes immediate streaming optimal, the same logic endorses day-and-date release. If day-and-date would cannibalize theatrical revenue, then exclusivity plainly creates value—which is all the motion requires. Side B's answer in Rebuttal 2 was evasive: claiming PRO's concession of roughly a month means PRO "surrendered" misreads the motion, which asks for a "meaningful" window, not an indefinite one. A month IS meaningful. Side B also misread the burden throughout: the motion does not ask studios to abandon streaming; it asks them to sequence properly, and Side A's sequencing argument (theaters create urgency and cultural status, streaming inherits a hit) was coherent and largely unanswered. Side B had real arguments—exhibitor split, piracy, global simultaneity, volatile box office—but these were more effective at softening PRO's case than at building a decisive affirmative alternative, and Side A addressed all of them. Side A's execution was cleaner across rebuttals and the closing crystallized the clash well.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's day-and-date trap in Q2 (A3.2) and its follow-through in Rebuttal 2 (A4.5) is the most decisive exchange. By asking what principle stops CON from endorsing day-and-date release, Side A forced a dilemma: either immediate home availability is truly optimal (endorsing day-and-date) or it is not (conceding theatrical exclusivity has value). Side B's answer—that quick streaming "aligns distribution with actual consumption" and is "not about banning theaters"—dodged the logical point entirely. Side A then correctly observed that if day-and-date would cannibalize theatrical revenue, the concession is already enough to decide the motion. This trap was never escaped and stands as the clearest reason PRO wins the round.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A won by establishing clear sequencing of premium theatrical revenue first then streaming, directly answering pressure questions with a concrete window definition and profit logic, and forcing a concession on exclusivity creating value that CON could not escape without undermining its own case. Side B offered plausible subscriber arguments but relied on unproven isolation of title-specific value and failed to neutralize the day-and-date trap in rebuttal 2 and closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 directly addressing both pressure questions while exposing CON's lack of stopping point on exclusivity value creation, which carried into a crisp closing weighing of premium sale versus blurry bundle replacement.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): PRO won by showing that CON never grounded the claim that streaming subscriber value reliably replaces lost theatrical premium revenue. The attribution problem and the day-and-date trap exposed a real burden CON could not meet: CON retreated to blurry aggregate metrics and mischaracterized a one-month window as surrendering the motion, while PRO's sequencing logic—premium urgency before bundled convenience—survived with cleaner comparative weighing and sharper crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's day-and-date pressure question (A3.2) and its follow-through in the second rebuttal (A4.5), which demonstrated that CON's logic lacked a stable stopping point and that any concession against day-and-date implicitly admits exclusivity creates value.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side A won primarily through format discipline and argumentative consistency. Three of Side B's speeches were clipped before completion—the opening, both rebuttals—which meant the judge never saw complete versions of their core arguments. That is a real and repeated execution cost. On substance, Side A's central frame ("durable capital vs. subsidized churn") held across all rounds and was consistently applied to each new clash. Its scarcity-creates-urgency mechanism was plausible and never fully neutralized. Side A also answered both pressure questions directly with A1/A2 labels and concrete content: the 45-to-75-day window tied to the monetization arc answered CON's vagueness attack, and the "non-refundable cash funds the slate while subscriber metrics reset monthly" answered the integrated-studio question. Side B's strongest claim—that the marginal home-viewing audience was never going to theaters anyway—was genuine pressure and PRO's response (the training effect) only partially addressed it. CON's closing also effectively reframed the motion around demand segmentation rather than delay. But these strong moments were embedded in repeatedly truncated speeches, which reduced their cumulative impact. Side A's case was complete, legible, and well-crystallized in closing. The margin is clear but not dominant because the substantive arguments Side B did land were analytically serious, particularly the integrated-studio revenue model and the marginal-audience challenge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's pressure question Q1 ("what exactly counts as a meaningful window?") and PRO's A1 answer. CON correctly identified that "meaningful" was doing heavy lifting without definition, and PRO responded with a specific, grounded range (45–75 days tied to the proven front-loaded monetization arc) that was plausible without overclaiming. This directly defended the motion's core prescription and neutralized what could have been a fatal vagueness attack.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side A wins due to a more complete, consistent case on immediate revenue and event status, with stronger direct responses to pressure on churn and demand decay; Side B suffered execution costs from multiple clipped turns that left rebuttals and comparisons underdeveloped despite solid ecosystem points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2, which directly refuted B's churn and flexibility claims by contrasting durable box office cash against reset subscriber metrics and trained waiting.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side A won because its core funding mechanism—that theatrical exclusivity generates immediate, high-margin capital that actually finances nine-figure productions—remained live after the full exchange. Side B’s counter that integrated streaming ecosystems capture additive demand and durable subscriber value was plausible but never squarely showed how quick-to-stream tentpoles replace box office as the primary bankroll for massive budgets. Side A’s "liquidation strategy, not a growth model" frame provided a memorable comparative weighing that Side B’s "flexibility" narrative could not fully overcome. Additionally, Side B incurred significant execution costs by having three speeches clipped for exceeding word limits, leaving key rebuttal conclusions unseen, while Side A operated cleanly within constraints.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2, which directly answered Side B’s pressure questions with a concrete window definition (45–75 days) and then crystallized the clash as "durable capital versus subsidized churn," successfully blunted B’s ecosystem-value claims and preserved PRO’s live route to victory.
