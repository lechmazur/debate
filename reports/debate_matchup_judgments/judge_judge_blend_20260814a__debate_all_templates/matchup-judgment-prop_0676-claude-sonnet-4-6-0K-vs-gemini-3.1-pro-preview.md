### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B wins because it kept the debate tied to the actual proposition: automated video and personality assessments, not all automated hiring tools. Side A’s strongest answer was targeted validation and auditability, especially the point that scale can make correction scalable too. But that alternative often sounded like the “heavy restriction” the motion allows, and A repeatedly overextended by invoking skills tests and cognitive screens that B correctly said were outside the motion. B gave the cleaner causal story: these tools use contested proxies, harm neurodivergent and disabled applicants, create black-box exclusion, and scale that exclusion across employers. A’s closing was also clipped, adding a small execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s rebuttal that the motion does not target objective skills tests but specifically video/personality judgments was decisive; it undercut A’s main overbreadth argument and turned A’s concession against micro-expression and eye-contact scoring into support for heavy restriction.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (A) wins. They directly answered pressure questions with A1/A2 format, maintained a live distinction between flawed affect metrics and valid tools, and consistently weighed targeted validation (fixable at scale) against blunt restriction (returns hiring to invisible human bias). PRO's scale argument was neutralized and their own concessions on scope were turned against the broad "ban or heavily restrict" language.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.3 and A4.1–A4.3: direct answers to B's pressure questions plus the concession that invalid metrics can be removed without banning everything.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (no reasoning) (CON)): PRO won by grounding the debate in concrete, scalable harms: automated video and personality assessments rely on pseudoscientific proxies that inherently penalize neurodivergence and disability, while extracting biometric data under coercion. CON’s regulatory alternative was theoretically appealing but remained abstract about what valid automated video personality tools look like, and CON conceded that core metrics like micro-expression scoring are invalid. PRO’s consistent focus on the impossibility of auditing pseudoscience and the unique scale of algorithmic harm gave them the cleaner live route to victory, compounded by CON’s clipped closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 1 argument that algorithmic scale cuts both ways—systematic errors can be fixed with a single update, whereas human bias is distributed and invisible—was the most damaging exchange, though PRO limited its impact by showing that standardizing normative baselines is not progress but systemic exclusion.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON wins by successfully turning PRO’s scalability argument (“scale cuts both ways” for fixes) and offering a superior policy alternative (validation requirements) that surgically removes pseudoscientific metrics without the collateral damage of a blanket ban. CON answered pressure questions directly, maintained clear comparative weighing throughout, and effectively framed the clash around instrument choice. PRO delivered strong rhetoric on industrialized bias but struggled to refute CON’s calibrated regulatory approach, often repeating opening points rather than adapting to the evolving clash. CON’s clipped closing is a minor execution cost but does not undermine a substantively stronger performance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s response to PRO’s pressure on fixing biased metrics, clarifying that validation mandates would simply remove invalid inputs (like eye-contact scoring) entirely rather than patch them, effectively neutralizing PRO’s core objection while preserving useful tools.
