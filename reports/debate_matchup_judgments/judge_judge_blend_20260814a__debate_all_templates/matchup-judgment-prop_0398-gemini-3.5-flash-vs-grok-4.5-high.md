### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-organized cases, but B's argument was more grounded in practical labor-market mechanics and better survived the exchange. B's "cost of labor vs. cost of living" distinction and the historical-wage-variance point (engineers in Idaho vs. SF were never paid identically even before remote work) directly undercut A's foundational premise that pure output value, not geography, has ever set wages — and A never squarely answered that empirical point. The pressure-question exchange was decisive: B's Q1 forced A into an extreme position (raising local workers' pay to HQ levels rather than cutting movers), which A explicitly conceded ("Yes—equity requires paying both the identical headquarters rate"). B then exploited this concession in Rebuttal 2, framing it as an "economic fantasy" that firms cannot sustain, and A's only response was a vague assertion that remote work "already multiplies capacity," without explaining why cost increases wouldn't shrink hiring. A's equity reversal (both mover and local should get raised, not cut) was clever but never resolved the practical budget tension B kept pressing, leaving B with the cleaner, more sustainable path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q1 (forcing A to choose between paying identical inflated wages to everyone or admitting a privileged premium for movers) and A's direct concession in A1 ("Yes—equity requires paying both the identical headquarters rate") was the pivotal exchange; B then used that concession in Rebuttal 2 to brand A's model an "economic fantasy," a charge A never fully rebutted.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): PRO wins because CON’s "same work, same pay" frame collapsed on feasibility. PRO showed that universal headquarters pay is economically unsustainable for most firms, forcing an impossible choice between slashing headcount in lower-cost regions or creating the very two-tier privilege CON claimed to oppose. CON never rebutted the ordinary observation that wages remain geographically differentiated, leaving PRO’s hiring-capacity and purchasing-power arguments live while CON’s global-market ideal remained ungrounded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 use of ordinary wage dispersion—software engineers in Idaho, Ohio, and San Francisco have never been paid identically despite similar output—exposed CON’s "global talent market" as assertion rather than reality, undercutting both CON’s equity and capacity defenses simultaneously.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a much stronger, economically grounded case. Side A successfully exposed the fatal flaw in Side B's model by pointing out that paying headquarters rates globally is economically unviable, as payroll differentials vastly outweigh real estate savings. Side B's insistence that companies should just pay top-tier rates to all local hires relied on hand-waving and failed to address the macroeconomic reality of labor supply and demand. Side A's internal equity and cost-of-labor arguments were decisive and cleanly answered the core clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's answer to the pressure question by pointing out that real estate savings are marginal compared to compounding payroll costs, effectively neutralizing Side B's claim that companies could easily afford to pay headquarters rates to a globally distributed workforce.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly and avoided fabricated evidence, but PRO built the more coherent economic mechanism: HQ premiums exist because of local reservation wages and talent competition, so once a worker relocates the rationale for the premium dissolves, and full HQ pay after relocation becomes an "unearned transfer" financed by the firm and by hub-bound peers. This story also exposed a quiet tension in CON's framework—CON never explained why geography-based HQ premiums were legitimate in the first place if pay is supposed to be purely value-based and location-agnostic. PRO's answer to CON's symmetry pressure question ("yes, adjustment should run both ways") was clean and consistent with its stated principle, even though CON's "corporate fantasy" counter-attack (that symmetry is rarely honored in practice) landed a real hit that PRO's closing did not squarely re-engage. CON's strongest original move—the roommate/thrift analogy, arguing employers don't claw back savings from frugal on-site workers—was clever and repeated across three speeches, but PRO's reservation-wage logic implicitly answered it (personal thrift doesn't change the local labor market a firm is competing in; relocation does), even though PRO never explicitly labeled this as a rebuttal. CON's morale and "same Zoom call" framing was vivid but mostly asserted against PRO's equally plausible "peers subsidize the double-dip" counter-framing, making that exchange a wash. On balance PRO's systemic sustainability argument (headcount, remote viability) was less thoroughly rebutted than CON's individual-fairness argument, giving PRO the cleaner path to the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to CON's Pressure Question 2 (agreeing that adjustments must run symmetrically upward for costlier relocations) combined with CON's follow-up charge that this symmetry is "a corporate fantasy" rarely honored in practice was the most decisive exchange—PRO's theoretical consistency was strong, but its failure to directly re-answer the practice-based counter-attack in the closing left that thread only half-resolved in PRO's favor.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by reframing "equal pay" from nominal dollars to real purchasing power, establishing that HQ salaries embed location premiums rather than pure output value. A cleanly distinguished labor markets from product markets, answered B's symmetry pressure with a direct and theoretically consistent "yes," and maintained the "double-dip" burden that B never fully resolved—why high-cost peers and the firm should subsidize a relocator's lifestyle surplus. B's equal-pay principle remained intuitively appealing but could not overcome A's demonstration that freezing HQ pay produces arbitrage, not equity, and threatens the sustainability of remote headcount itself.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's takedown of the product-pricing analogy and the "double-dip" reframe in Rebuttal 1, which turned B's equity argument into a subsidy argument and distinguished how labor markets actually clear.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A built a much stronger conceptual and economic case. By clearly distinguishing between labor market clearing rates (which are driven by local supply and reservation wages) and product market pricing (which is driven by global demand), Side A effectively neutralized Side B's strongest analogy. Side A's framing of the "double-dip" and "unearned real raise" logically followed from the premise that HQ bands exist specifically to offset local cost-of-living pressures. Side B relied heavily on rhetorical slogans and the roommate analogy, which failed to account for the macro-level labor market dynamics that Side A successfully highlighted. Side A also answered the pressure questions more directly and crisply, proving the theoretical symmetry of location adjustments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they cleanly dismantled Side B's product pricing analogy by explaining the difference between customer willingness to pay and local labor reservation wages, and affirmed the symmetry of location adjustments to prove it is a neutral market mechanism rather than a one-way downward ratchet.
