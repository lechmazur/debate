### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by cleanly separating the motion's two mandates—ending indefinite detention and imposing presumptive release—and dismantling the latter. Side B over-relied on claiming A conceded the debate, but A masterfully showed how their shared endpoints required A's sequence of default custody to function.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 expertly turned Side B's pressure questions around, explaining how detention provides the leverage needed to compel cooperation with consular processing, exposing the flaw in presumptive release.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by proving that CON's alternative framework—detention with time limits and judicial review—collapses into the motion. PRO showed that when CON's court-set limit expires, the state either releases into supervision (the motion) or renews detention (indefinite detention by another name). CON's opening concession that indefinite detention without review is an abuse conceded the core of the motion, and CON never recovered from PRO's "both exits lead to PRO" trap. While CON's sequence argument had force, it could not overcome the textual logic of the motion, which targets only indefinite detention while allowing bounded, evidenced custody, or the practical reality that PRO's case-management alternative handles hard cases within its framework.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure question and follow-up rebuttal asking what CON does when a court-set time limit expires and removal still has not occurred. By demonstrating that CON's position must either release the person into supervision (affirming the motion) or recycle detention indefinitely (contradicting CON's own concession that indefinite detention is an abuse), this exchange pinned CON's framework to the wall and narrowed the entire debate to a single door that opened onto PRO's ground.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO won the definitional core: prohibiting indefinite detention does not mean abolishing all detention; B's own "time limits and judicial review" [B2.1] endorses the prohibition. PRO then won the hard-case comparison: detaining a refused asylum-seeker when a foreign government blocks documents achieves no removal, while short-term ID detention plus risk-based custody [A1.2] preserves enforcement. A answered B's absconding pressure directly with lawful re-detention for proven flight [A4.1] and a plausible mechanism for case-management anchors, while B never answered what leverage detention provides when removal is blocked by a third state or what stops his renewable limits from being indefinite by another name [A3.1].
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.5 - A shows re-detention for absconders fits the motion's evidenced-risk exception and turns B's own time-limit framework into release-into-supervision or renewable indefinite detention, exposing that B had conceded the motion's bounded-detention architecture.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won decisively by setting a strategic trap in its opening and closing it flawlessly in the late rounds. By offering a counter-model of periodic review and burden-shifting, A forced B to either defend a hard, clock-based release for dangerous individuals or soften the motion by allowing continuous renewals. When B chose the latter in Turn 7, A accurately pointed out that B had abandoned the motion and adopted A's counter-model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 brilliantly capitalized on Side B's answers to the pressure questions, pointing out that if judges can endlessly renew detention based on fresh necessity findings, Side B has essentially recreated indefinite detention and conceded the debate to Side A's counter-model.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): CON won by exposing a fatal tension in PRO's case: PRO interpreted the motion as permitting judges to continue detaining dangerous non-cooperators indefinitely through renewed necessity findings, which collapsed the proposition into CON's own opening alternative of periodic review with burden-shifting and case management. At the same time, PRO admitted the empirical evidence for case-management compliance came from screened, pre-decision populations, leaving unsupported the motion's application to post-final-order non-cooperators—the very group the debate turned on. CON crystallized the clash around the margin of resistant, dangerous detainees and showed the motion's sole unique effect, if taken seriously, would be releasing exactly that population. PRO's closing appeal to the default could not survive CON's demonstration that the default PRO described was already in CON's opening framework.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4), particularly A4.5-A4.8, where CON showed that PRO's admission that judges "can keep detaining" dangerous non-cooperators meant PRO had either adopted CON's conditional regime or abandoned the motion's categorical prohibition, paired with PRO's concession that compliance data covered the wrong population.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B wins the margin debate. B defines the test as the post-final-order non-cooperator a judge would still detain. A never sustains a defense for that margin: in A4.1/A4.4 A concedes judges can keep detaining on renewed necessity and that compliance data is from screened pre-decision populations, conceding B1.4 selection bias. B shows this collapses PRO into B1.6 conditional regime - periodic review, burden on government, case-management for low-risk - leaving the categorical clock-based release as PRO's only unique effect, which is undefended and incentivizes obstruction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5-B4.7 - showing A4.1's "judge can keep detaining without fixed endpoint" is indefinite detention with review (B1.6 verbatim), and A4.4's honest admission that evidence covers screened populations, not the post-order non-cooperators the motion uniquely frees.
