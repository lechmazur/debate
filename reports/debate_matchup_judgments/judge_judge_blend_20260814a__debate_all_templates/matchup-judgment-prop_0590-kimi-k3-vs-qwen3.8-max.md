### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO built a consistent, vivid throughline: harm doesn't care about corporate letterheads, and any headquarters-anchored rule invites cheap regulatory arbitrage. PRO's toy/GDPR analogies, its Singapore–Lyon hypothetical, and its pressure questions kept forcing CON to specify what actually stops offshore evasion. CON's strongest structural move was to relabel the operative trigger as "market placement" rather than headquarters, and its rebuttal that GDPR turns on establishment/targeting rather than pure "any impact" was a sharp, accurate correction that PRO never circled back to answer. But that same pivot became CON's central weakness: across the whole debate CON never once defended headquarters as the actual trigger, and PRO explicitly named this retreat in closing ("my opponent has never defended headquarters"), a charge CON's final speech did not squarely rebut. CON's market-placement model also converged with PRO's own paradigm case (the offshore provider selling into Lyon), which blunted the practical stakes CON needed to create, while leaving PRO's broader claim — that protection should track people, not paperwork — as the last standing, memorable frame. CON's workability worries about boundless downstream liability were real and only partially answered by PRO's "deployer becomes provider" move, but they weren't enough to offset PRO's cleaner engagement with the resolution as written and its sharper closing framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's correction that GDPR's extraterritorial reach turns on "establishment or targeting," not raw impact, was the most damaging unanswered rebuttal against PRO's core analogy; conversely, PRO's closing charge that CON "never defended headquarters" exposed the deepest unresolved tension in CON's case, since CON's market-placement model never explicitly reconciled itself with the resolution's literal headquarters-vs-impact framing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A won the motion-specific comparison. B persuasively distinguished deliberate market entry from potentially boundless downstream impacts, but its own preferred regime still reaches foreign providers because their systems enter and affect the EU, while repeatedly denying that headquarters should shield them. That largely conceded A's central proposition and replaced headquarters with a third trigger. A also adequately limited unforeseeable repurposing by assigning duties to the deployer or modifier with control. B exposed imprecision in A's claim that market entry and impact are identical, keeping the debate competitive, but never established why headquarters should determine coverage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest turn separated who must comply from what triggers coverage: the provider remains responsible, while EU-facing use determines whether the rules apply. This converted B's examples of importers, representatives, and market bans into evidence that offshore headquarters cannot be the coverage criterion.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO won the moral case against pure HQ, but never answered B's decisive fork: if 'impact' means deliberate EU offering, it adds nothing to market-entry enforcement which already catches the Singapore-to-Lyon seller via importer/authorized rep; if it means any downstream effect, it becomes boundless and unknowable. B answered A's pressure directly, showed no loophole, while A collapsed market placement into impact without handling knowability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B R2 A1/A2 and closing fork - deliberate commercial act vs raw impact, making PRO redundant or overbroad, and handling the deployer-repurposing edge via control.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but CON built a tighter, more resilient case. PRO's strongest move—defining the impact trigger via "foreseeable, contracted, knowable" effects—kept collapsing into what CON correctly identified as market-targeting/placement, which CON showed is already the operative legal trigger under existing EU law. CON's pressure questions forced PRO to either produce an enforceable provider its test uniquely catches, or admit the motion reaches only unenforceable "accidental" impacts—and PRO's answer conceded exactly that ("for accidental touches, nothing"), a concession CON exploited decisively in Rebuttal 2 and the closing to frame a clean dilemma: either the motion duplicates existing conduct-based law, or it is unworkable and overreaching as literally written. PRO's closing restated its position without resolving this dilemma, while CON's closing crystallized it cleanly. CON's "doorway, not weather" framing was rhetorically sharper and did more comparative work, and its rebuttals more directly neutralized PRO's central examples by re-characterizing them as targeting cases already covered by current rules.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's use of PRO's own concession—"for accidental touches, nothing" [A4.3]—against PRO's "foreseeable impact" trigger, turning it into the decisive dilemma restated in the closing: either the motion changes nothing (targeting already covers it) or it is unenforceable overreach (true impacts-only cases).
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by exposing Side A’s false choice between headquarters and impacts. B consistently showed that market placement and output-use rules can already reach foreign providers through deliberate, knowable conduct, while a pure impacts trigger creates ex ante uncertainty. A’s foreseeable-impact qualification improved administrability but made its examples indistinguishable from targeting, and its exclusion of accidental impacts retreated from the broader trigger it defended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s central-contract example was decisive: a vendor knowingly contracting for use in EU stores is already engaging in market placement, so the example did not establish a regulatory gap. B then used A’s concession that accidental touches should trigger nothing to sharpen the dilemma that the proposal either duplicates existing law or becomes vague and overbroad.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): CON won the decisive framing clash. PRO argued impacts vs headquarters, but CON showed the real EU trigger is market-placement conduct, which already ignores headquarters. PRO's own "foreseeable substantial impact" indicators – euro pricing, EU contracts, EU stores – are targeting, so the motion either duplicates existing law or, read literally as "where impacts occur," becomes unknowable ex ante and unenforceable. PRO's concession "for accidental touches, nothing" abandoned the literal motion while its central-contract example was already covered by deployer duties, leaving no gap to justify the change.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's R1/R2 dilemma – all PRO examples are targeting already captured vs pure impacts is fog – plus PRO's R2 admission that accidental impacts trigger nothing, which CON used to show the motion as written either changes nothing or is unworkable.
