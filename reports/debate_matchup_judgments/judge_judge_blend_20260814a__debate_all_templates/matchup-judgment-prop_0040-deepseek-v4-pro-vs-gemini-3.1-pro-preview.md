### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): A (CON) wins a clear but competitive debate. The decisive exchange was A's relentless pressure on the historical-chatbot problem: the proposition bans any deceased person's data without explicit opt-in, so Lincoln, Churchill, and MLK interactive models are flatly prohibited. B's response in Rebuttal 2 conceded this cost and called it "a price worth paying"—a significant capitulation that A exploited cleanly through the closing. A also landed the estate-law argument effectively: centuries of law give heirs authority over the deceased's letters and physical property, and B's counter (generating new sentences crosses into "counterfeiting identity") was creative but A exposed it as an invented distinction that doesn't appear in the motion's text. B's strongest live argument—that existing law doesn't cover emotional distortion from a "plausible but banal" griefbot—was real and A's tort-law answer was only partially persuasive, but it wasn't enough to recover from the historical-chatbot concession or the sustained paternalism attack on B's "grief impairs judgment" framing. B's multiple speech clippings also meant several arguments were incomplete at their ends, adding a small execution cost. B's dignity/consent framework and "counterfeiting identity" reframe were genuinely strong and kept the debate competitive, but A controlled the key clash points by the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answer to Q1 ([A4.3]): after B conceded in A2 of Rebuttal 2 that the historical-chatbot ban is "a price worth paying," A immediately crystallized this as a formal concession of massive cultural loss and pressed that B was simultaneously inventing a public/private data exemption that simply does not exist in the proposition's text. This double move—locking in the concession and blocking the escape hatch—was the most decisive single exchange in the debate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by more effectively exposing the proposition's overbroad impact on inheritance rights, family mourning autonomy, and historical preservation, forcing Side B to concede limited costs as a "price worth paying" while defending an unreliable distinction between public and private data not in the motion text. Side A answered pressure questions directly on limits and paternalism with concrete legal analogies, while Side B's rebuttals relied on more abstract dignity claims that did not fully neutralize the tangible living harms and cultural losses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's handling of historical preservation in rebuttal 1 and rebuttal 2, combined with direct A1/A2 answers to pressure questions that highlighted the blanket ban and grief judgment claims, most decisively shifted the comparative burden.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by successfully isolating the unique harm of AI mimicry—manufacturing new, unconsenting speech—versus merely accessing inherited data. While Side B scored strong textual points about the motion's lack of a public/private exemption and effectively pressed the paternalism charge, Side A's weighing was cleaner: the dignity violation and emotional distortion of synthetic intimacy outweigh historical chatbots or willing-family comfort, and existing tort law fails to cover non-defamatory but profoundly violating AI outputs. Side A's moral framing of "silence is not consent" and "counterfeit identity" provided a more decisive limiting principle for a third-party reader.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over existing legal remedies versus the novel harm of synthetic intimacy. Side B argued torts and publicity rights already prevent abuse, but Side A sharply rebutted that defamation and fraud require false reputation-harming statements or financial deceit, missing the core harm of a griefbot generating plausible but emotionally distorting intimacy that the deceased never consented to. This successfully exposed a remedial gap that justified the opt-in rule.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won on three decisive clashes that Side A never fully neutralized. First, B's point that the dead cannot be defamed in most jurisdictions and that post-mortem publicity rights protect only celebrities in commercial advertising—not ordinary people whose data feeds private subscription chatbots—directly dismantled A's repeated claim that existing legal tools are sufficient. A acknowledged this framing but never offered a concrete rebuttal. Second, B's generative-AI-as-synthetic-forgery distinction held throughout the debate: A's family-curation model consistently treats the chatbot as if it merely replays the deceased's actual words, but B kept landing the point that generative AI manufactures novel responses the person never made. A's best response—"families can impose strict source-only limits"—is a regulatory proposal, not an answer to the consent gap. Third, B's analogy in the closing ("we do not let a surviving spouse sign a dead loved one's name to a new letter just because they inherited their pen") was apt and went unanswered. On the other side, A's family-trust frame and the "widow-and-child" scenario had genuine emotional and logical weight, and the point that an opt-in prohibition amplifies family conflict by giving any relative a veto was underexplored by B. However, A was clipped on three separate turns—Opening, Rebuttal 1, and Closing—meaning key transitional arguments (especially the full "better path" of transparent regulation) were never delivered, imposing a real execution cost. B's speeches were complete and progressed cleanly from opening to closing, sustaining the core consent-and-dignity frame and sharpening it with specific legal observations that remained live at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 point that the dead have no legal standing for defamation in most jurisdictions, creating a "glaring legal vacuum" that existing torts cannot fill, was the most decisive single exchange. Side A's opening and first rebuttal both relied on existing fraud, defamation, and emotional distress statutes as a safety net; B's targeted response demolished that safety net with a plausible and unrefuted legal observation, and A's subsequent answer in Rebuttal 2 never restored the claim.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): B wins by clearly distinguishing generative AI (novel synthesis exploiting the dead) from static heirlooms, exposing the legal vacuum for postmortem rights, and directly resolving pressure questions on guarantees and family conflicts with targeted comparison. A's clipped turns weaken completeness and force of rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 (B4) answering both pressure questions by contrasting forgery with passive inheritance and noting families are not monolithic.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by decisively controlling the technological and legal clash. A consistently distinguished generative AI’s novel, synthetic outputs from static heirlooms, neutralizing B’s diary and eulogy analogies. A also correctly identified that existing defamation and publicity rights rarely protect ordinary citizens post-mortem, exposing a regulatory vacuum that B’s vague family-consent framework failed to fill. While B offered a compelling emotional case for family trust and grief, it repeatedly sidestepped the motion’s explicit focus on corporate data processing and never provided a concrete, workable alternative to A’s clean opt-in default. A’s direct answers to pressure, sharper legal grounding, and clearer comparative weighing secure the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 1 and 2 dismantling B’s static-record analogies by emphasizing generative AI’s capacity to invent novel responses, combined with A’s accurate correction that post-mortem defamation and publicity rights do not cover ordinary citizens, effectively collapsed B’s legal and ethical safety nets.
