Side A effectively used this to prove why a preemptive legal mandate is necessary, neutralizing Side B's concerns about speed with practical labor law precedents like framework agreements.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing crystallized the debate perfectly by pointing out that Side B's defense of market self-correction (employers fixing AI after retention drops) is an admission that workers must absorb the cost of unconsented experiments before anything improves.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by making the central comparison clearer: a negotiation mandate supplies process, but not reliable protection, especially where councils lack leverage or technical capacity. Side A had a plausible structural-power argument, but increasingly relied on late-added supporting architecture—appeals boards, funded experts, non-waivable floors, renegotiation clauses—to make the mandate work. Side B persuasively framed those additions as concessions that the proposition alone is under-specified and outcome-indeterminate. Side A’s “unilateral deployment” harm remained live, but B effectively argued that concrete AI harms are better addressed through enforceable targeted rules.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered Side A’s pressure questions while turning Side A’s added safeguards into evidence that bare negotiation is insufficient without a separate legal architecture.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully exposing Side A's reliance on external legal architecture (baseline statutes, appeals boards, employer-funded experts) to make the mandate functional and safe. Side B effectively proved that a procedural mandate lacks a guaranteed floor of protection without these additions, whereas targeted rules guarantee protection directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 expertly capitalized on Side A's answers, pointing out that if Side A needs 'baseline statutes' to prevent bad negotiated agreements, then targeted rules are doing the actual protective work.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON showed that the proposition substitutes procedural negotiation for substantive protection, demonstrating that without imported legal architecture—enforceable floors, funded expertise, appeals—the mandate is outcome-indeterminate and risks giving democratic cover to harmful AI. PRO’s defense repeatedly added elements not present in the motion, implicitly conceding the bare requirement is insufficient. Targeted, enforceable rules offer the more reliable and equitable path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 dilemma on baseline labor laws: if existing statutes already prevent the worst AI harms then the mandate is redundant, and if they do not, councils negotiating inside an inadequate floor merely legitimize harm—collapsing PRO’s claim that the mandate guarantees meaningful protection.
