### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built the more resilient case by pressing three points PRO never fully neutralized: (1) the "necessity collapse" — PRO conceded reserves only work "paired" with quota/capacity cuts, letting A argue the cuts do the real work and the 30% closure is a costly, harmful add-on; (2) the enforcement argument — A's point that weak-institution states cannot police an expanded offshore perimeter any better than a coastline, illustrated with Indonesia/Senegal, went largely unanswered beyond assertion; (3) a genuine contradiction catch — PRO's opening insisted "the principle does not wait for perfect compensation" yet Rebuttal 2's answer to the pressure question quietly reintroduced provisioning and reallocation as the mechanism feeding hungry families, which A explicitly flagged ("you cannot make hardship enforceable and then call it mitigation"). PRO's mechanism story (density thresholds, spillover, forage/nursery locking) was coherent but was asserted and re-asserted rather than defended against these specific structural challenges, and its counter to A's temperate-recovery examples (attributing outcomes to "different biology and stronger institutions") actually reinforced A's institutional-capacity argument rather than defusing it. A's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("you need fish free from pressure, not places free from people"), while B's closing mostly restated its mechanism without resolving the conceded tensions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point that B's own pairing requirement ("reserves only work paired with quotas/capacity cuts") surrenders the necessity claim, combined with A's follow-up in Rebuttal 2 that weak-institution states cannot enforce a larger offshore perimeter — B never cleanly answered why the 30% line adds value once capacity is already being cut, nor how enforcement improves for states that already can't police what they have.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by showing that PRO never established a universal need for a permanent 30% no-take threshold. A plausibly separated reducing fish mortality from excluding fishers, offered less disruptive management alternatives, and exposed PRO's reliance on capacity cuts, enforcement, and transitional support that the proposition does not guarantee. PRO's answers about immediate food, migratory stocks, and easier enforcement remained speculative and often depended on the very complementary policies A said were doing the real work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was that if reserves refill the remaining waters only when paired with binding capacity cuts, then those cuts are the recovery engine rather than proof that every fishing nation needs a 30% closure. Side B's response relied on unguaranteed provisioning and asserted unique reserve effects without adequately defeating that comparison.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by proving the 30% reserve is not necessary to rebuild stocks and creates certain, immediate harm for dependent communities while outsourcing recovery to distant, uncertain spillover. PRO never recovered from the concession that reserves must be paired with quotas and capacity cuts—undermining the claim that large no-take zones are the essential driver of recovery—and failed to answer CON's concrete objections about unenforceable offshore perimeters in poor states and the irrelevance of single-EEZ closures to migratory species.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first-rebuttal turn that if reserves only work when paired with quotas, gear limits, and rights systems, then those management tools are doing the heavy lifting and the 30% reserve becomes a costly, eviction-first add-on rather than a necessary solution.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but PRO built the more coherent path through the exchange. PRO's opening supplied a concrete causal mechanism (spillover, broodstock, larval drift) plus plausible real-world examples, then used Rebuttal 1 to expose a genuine tension in CON's case: CON argued both that 30% would steal productive grounds and that much of the EEZ is unused deep water. PRO's pressure question forced CON to explicitly concede that effective reserves must include the productive nearshore grounds communities use, which CON tried to spin as proof of recklessness but which PRO then leveraged in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to collapse CON's "site it offshore" escape route and to reframe the debate as a clean binary: a monitorable, phased mechanism with a defined recovery path versus indefinitely renegotiable quotas that both sides agree hungry communities may not respect. CON's rebuttals repeated the "income cliff" and "poaching defeats binary lines" points effectively but never squarely answered PRO's enforcement-asymmetry argument (satellite detection vs. at-sea boarding/logbooks) or its phasing/subsidy-redirection mitigation, leaving those points live for PRO. CON's closing mostly restated the cliff concern without resolving that gap, while PRO's closing crisply synthesized the clash. This gives PRO a clear, if not overwhelming, edge on rebuttal quality, comparison, and progression across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question forcing CON to admit that effective 30% reserves must include productive nearshore grounds, and PRO's subsequent use of that concession in Rebuttal 2/Closing to dismantle CON's "site reserves in unused deep water" escape route, was the most decisive exchange of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by keeping the burden on the proposition's rigid 30% floor. Side B persuasively explained reserve biology and enforcement advantages, but never adequately justified why every fishing nation needs at least 30% rather than targeted reserves combined with quotas, gear rules, and rights systems. Its strongest answer to the transition cost relied on late-added phasing, compensation, and unsupported precise recovery timelines. Side A more convincingly exposed the siting dilemma: low-use waters may minimize livelihood harm but may not protect relevant stocks, while ecologically effective closures impose concentrated costs on dependent communities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the alleged contradiction over distant versus productive waters was decisive. It showed that meeting an area target in lightly used ocean does not necessarily deliver the promised spillover, while placing reserves where broodstock and fishing income actually concentrate intensifies the livelihood burden the motion asks communities to accept.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that a rigid 30% no-take floor must eventually close productive nearshore grounds that dependent communities actually use, creating an immediate, concentrated income cliff that alternatives such as quotas, rights-based management, and selective gear avoid. PRO built a coherent biological case for reserves and spillover, but never fully resolved the timing and displacement problem for households living day-to-day, and its phased, offshore-first proposal conceded the need to eventually compress fishers onto fewer grounds. The decisive clash was whether dependency makes the 30% mandate mandatory insurance or reckless sacrifice; CON made the latter far clearer by the final exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turn on PRO's Pressure Question, conceding that effective 30% reserves must close productive nearshore grounds but using that concession to prove the mandate is precisely reckless for heavily dependent communities, crystallizing the core clash.
