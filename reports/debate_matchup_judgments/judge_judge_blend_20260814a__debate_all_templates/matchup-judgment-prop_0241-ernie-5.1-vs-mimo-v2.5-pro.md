### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the stronger cumulative case across the full exchange. The decisive early move was distinguishing between what justifies punishment as an aim and what happens to be effective social policy—a framing the motion explicitly invites and that PRO never adequately dislodged. The preventive-detention reductio (if preventing future harm is the sole justification, high-risk people could be detained preemptively; only desert constrains state power to acts committed) was introduced in A's first rebuttal and never squarely answered in any PRO speech. The equally sharp insight—that successful rehabilitative programs such as drug courts work precisely because they embed retributive elements (accountability, acknowledgment of wrongdoing, desert)—was landed cleanly and PRO's closing attempt to relabel those elements as "rehabilitative acts" was circular rather than responsive. Side A's pressure questions were sharper than PRO's, directly targeting the central obligation the motion creates: explaining why instrumental usefulness makes something a foundational aim. PRO's answers in Rebuttal 2 restated the conclusion (rehabilitation IS the aim) without bridging the logical gap. PRO's case relied heavily on outcome metrics—recidivism figures, cost savings—which are legitimate evidence but kept sliding past the motion's actual question. The clipping of Side A's closing is a real execution cost, but by that point the core argument threads (foundational aim vs. instrument, preventive detention, borrowed retributive grammar) were already well-established and PRO had no live answer to any of them. Side B argued competently and raised genuine pressure, especially the Q2 about punishing a rehabilitated low-risk offender, but Side A's answer—proportionality tracks the act, not the prognosis—was clear and consistent with the overall framework.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal identifying that PRO borrows retribution's moral grammar in its own best examples: drug-court success depends on offenders acknowledging wrongdoing, accepting accountability, and making amends—all desert-based elements. This turned PRO's strongest evidence into support for CON and was never refuted, only relabeled.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A wins because its distinction between justification (desert) and utility held up across rounds and was never neutralized; Side B repeatedly relied on outcome data and examples that A showed embed retributive elements, while A's pressure answers exposed the framework's inability to justify coercion or serious punishment without desert.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4) directly answered both pressure questions and crystallized that successful rehab programs depend on accountability/desert.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side B won by maintaining a sharp conceptual distinction between the foundational aim of punishment (desert/retribution) and instrumental social policy. While Side A offered strong empirical evidence that rehabilitation reduces recidivism and costs, Side B successfully framed these as effects rather than the aim itself, and showed that rehabilitation borrows its moral coherence from retributive concepts like accountability and proportionality. Side B’s preventive detention objection and its turning of Side A’s drug-court example revealed a structural weakness in the PRO framework: without desert, coercion becomes mere population management. Side A never fully answered why future-oriented harm prevention does not license pre-crime detention, nor why accountability in rehabilitative programs is not fundamentally rooted in desert. Side B’s clipped closing incurred a small execution cost, but its earlier speeches had already built the cleaner, more resilient path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal turning of the drug-court evidence, arguing that victim satisfaction and offender accountability in those programs succeed precisely because they embed retributive elements (acknowledgment of desert), thereby using Side A’s own example to prove the primacy of retribution.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built a cleaner path to victory around one durable core challenge: what does retribution actually produce beyond returning suffering for suffering? A never gave a fully satisfying answer. B's separation of deterrence from retribution [B2.3, B4.3–4.4] was the sharpest single move in the debate—A had built much of its case on deterrence, and B correctly exposed that deterrence depends on the existence and certainty of consequences, not on the offender's misery during incarceration. A never closed that gap; the "retributive anchor" claim in A2.4 was asserted but not mechanistically explained. B's pressure question B3.1 ("does retribution actually heal victims?") exposed the weakest part of A's case—A's response in A4.1 cited general victim-impact research in a confident but thinly grounded way, applying a specificity standard that B had already noted A selectively demanded only from rehabilitation evidence. A's strongest late argument—the mental health courts / indeterminate detention risk [A4.2]—was specific and genuinely interesting, and B's clipped closing did not address it directly. However, B had pre-empted it in B2.6 (retributive distortions are already observable, rehab-first risks are theoretical) and in B4.3 (proportionate sentence first, then rehabilitative purpose within it). The key structural concession that Nordic systems combine proportionate sentences with rehabilitation actually favored B's framing—that sentences can be set by desert but guided in purpose by rehabilitation—rather than A's claim that the retributive framework must be primary. On balance, B's consistent forward-looking framing, effective exploitation of A's deterrence conflation, and unanswered challenge about retribution's productive outputs constitute a live route to victory that A's legitimacy and victim-closure arguments did not fully neutralize.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's distinction between deterrence and retribution [B2.3] was the most decisive single exchange. A's opening had treated deterrence as a pillar of retribution, but B correctly separated the two: deterrence requires the *existence* of consequences, not that the offender suffers purposelessly. A never mechanistically refuted this—the "retributive anchor" claim was bare assertion. B then reinforced the point in B4.3–4.4 when answering A3.2, clarifying that proportionate sentences preserve deterrence and stigma while allowing rehabilitation to guide what happens within them. This dismantled a major strand of A's case without B conceding anything substantial.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side B wins because it established a consistent outcome-focused comparison (recidivism reduction and future victims) that Side A never fully neutralized, answered pressure questions directly, and crystallized the "what does retribution produce" challenge in later rounds while Side A leaned on moral assertions and unmechanized victim-healing claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 directly answered both pressure questions on causal isolation and confinement risks, exposing A's selective empiricism and lack of mechanism for retribution's benefits.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side B wins because it successfully defended retribution as the structural backbone of criminal justice, constraining and enabling rehabilitation. While A made strong forward-looking arguments and correctly separated deterrence from retribution, B landed decisive blows by providing concrete mechanisms showing how rehab-priority risks indefinite confinement (mental-health courts extending custody, parole boards conditioning release on treatment milestones). B also offered plausible victim-impact framing that A never fully neutralized, and A's closing clipped before it could answer these pressures, leaving B's crystallization intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal (B4.2) answering A3Q2, which exposed how prioritizing rehabilitation within a retributive sentence can still create indeterminate confinement through clinical discretion and parole conditions, undermining A's claim that rehab-priority adds no new excess risk.
