### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A wins on a clear but competitive margin. Three live routes survive the full exchange in PRO's favor. First, the "payment ≠ agency / residuals ≠ rights ownership" distinction: PRO developed the argument that a residual payment for a defined use is categorically different from owning a digital voice asset capable of infinite synthetic reuse, and CON never squarely answered it—returning only to "union boilerplate guarantees residuals," which is the same thing PRO said was insufficient. Second, the "opt-in mandate prevents mandatory waivers" rejoinder: PRO argued in A2 that if opt-in consent is legally required, studios cannot include unconditional surrender clauses as conditions of hire, which directly inverts CON's "emerging actors will be locked out" argument. CON's A2 response restated that union boilerplate already prohibits total surrender via residuals, which is not the same thing and does not address the legal floor an opt-in mandate would create. Third, PRO's Q1 pressure landed: the ongoing labor fights over synthetic performance explicitly undercut CON's "collective bargaining has already solved this" claim. CON's distinction between "routine dubbing localization" and "full voice cloning for unrelated uses" is interesting but narrowly asserted without grounding, and it concedes that new consent rights were needed, weakening the "already settled" position. CON's strongest ground—the accessibility veto concern and the bit-part actor bottleneck—was real pressure, but PRO's "release form" analogy and "studios manage complex IP clearances routinely" response partially defused it without fully resolving it. CON's "90% cost reduction" figure was brittle and unanchored, a minor epistemic liability. Overall, PRO left more unanswered claims standing after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange: CON's recurring argument that opt-in would force emerging actors to waive rights to get hired depended on the current default being inert, but PRO correctly identified that a legally mandated opt-in standard resets that default and strips studios of the ability to make waiver a non-negotiable hire condition. CON's response—that existing union boilerplate already prohibits total surrender via residuals—did not engage with the structural point that absent a legal floor, studios retain the power to make waiver a precondition of casting. PRO's reframe shifted the burden clearly and CON never recovered it.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by making the cleaner case that broad boilerplate cannot substitute for specific consent over a performer’s digitally reproducible voice. Side B had a real access and transaction-cost argument, but it leaned heavily on unsupported precise claims about cost reductions, audience scale, and existing union protections. A’s consent-versus-payment framing survived B’s collective-bargaining defense and better matched the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest rebuttal was that residuals or standard contract language do not equal agency over a voice as a reusable digital asset; B’s answer depended on sweeping, undergrounded claims that current boilerplate already protects all relevant performers.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by establishing that consent is a fundamental labor right distinct from compensation, effectively neutralizing B's "fatal veto" accessibility argument by showing consent mechanisms work for other IP without destroying industries. A provided a cleaner answer to the central pressure question about how opt-in differs from the coercive status quo: it legally resets the default so studios cannot mandate rights surrender as a condition of employment. While B's union-boilerplate and global accessibility arguments were competitive, B never adequately proved that opt-in would actually scuttle dubs rather than simply require studios to treat performers as partners, and B conflated residual payments with agency throughout. A's sharper rebuttals and more direct handling of pressure questions created the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where A distinguished consent from veto by comparing AI voice rights to existing release forms and music clearances, then answered B's pressure question about waivers by explaining that an opt-in mandate would structurally prohibit studios from making digital rights surrender a non-negotiable condition of employment—resetting the power dynamic rather than replicating it.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): Side A built a more durable path to victory across all five rounds. The opening was markedly stronger: a concrete, vivid scenario grounded the stakes in a way Side B's abstract production-pipeline concerns never matched. Side A's most decisive single move came in Rebuttal 1, where it correctly identified that Side B's entire administrative-burden case rested on a category error—conflating AI cloning of the original performer's voice with ordinary third-party dubbing for localization, which the opt-in rule would never touch. Side B never fully recovered from this, because all of its gridlock and veto-power arguments depend on that conflation. Side B's best moments came in Rebuttal 2: the observation that existing right-of-publicity and defamation law already covers the hate-group-ad scenario from Side A's Q1 partially deflated that pressure question, and the point that Side A's concession on unreachable/deceased performers technically abandons a pure opt-in principle was a genuine hit. But Side A had anticipated both objections: the guild-administered residual fund carve-out for unreachable performers is a real, working analogue to existing industry practice, and the bright-line exclusion of audio-fidelity and sync adjustments from the opt-in requirement gave a workable answer to Side B's Q2 about ADR vs. cloning. Side B never offered a compelling counter to the fundamental fairness argument—that boilerplate written before AI voice cloning existed cannot reasonably be read to authorize unlimited synthetic replication of a performer's voice for unforeseeable future uses. Side B's closings mostly recycled earlier points without sharpening the live clash, while Side A's closing cleanly crystallized which burdens had been answered and which value was ultimately at stake. Side B's case was competent and raised real concerns, but it never neutralized the category-error damage or offered a principled reason why performers should bear all the downside risk of a technology no party contemplated at contract signing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 identification of the category error [A2.1]: by clarifying that the opt-in rule applies only when studios clone the original performer's voice via AI—not when they hire separate voice actors for localization—Side A dismantled the foundation of Side B's entire administrative-burden and global-distribution case in a single, clear move. Side B never squarely answered this distinction in any subsequent round.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): Side A won by making the central distinction clearer: ordinary localization or ADR is not the same as cloning a performer’s unique voice to generate new speech, so Side B’s strongest claims about production paralysis and killed distribution were overstated. Side B’s best attack was that A’s exceptions for unreachable or deceased performers weakened a pure opt-in principle, but that only partially damaged A’s case because A still gave the cleaner rationale for consent where living performers’ vocal identities are being newly exploited under old boilerplate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s first rebuttal was the decisive exchange: it exposed Side B’s conflation of standard dubbing with AI voice cloning and sharply reduced the force of the administrative-burden argument.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): Side A (PRO) won because it successfully narrowed the proposition to AI cloning of an original performer’s voice for new speech, neutralizing CON’s fears about standard localization gridlock. PRO answered CON’s pressure questions decisively by proposing prospective application, guild-administered residuals for unreachable performers, and a bright-line exclusion for standard ADR—removing every claimed administrative burden. CON never recovered from this surgical dismantling: it kept conflating AI cloning with ordinary dubbing, offered no compelling response to the bargaining-power asymmetry, and mistook pragmatic carve-outs for fatal contradictions. PRO’s moral case—that studios should not extract unforeseen AI labor for free from decades-old boilerplate—remained intact and clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s second rebuttal [A4.1–A4.2], where it answered CON’s pressure questions by establishing that opt-in applies only prospectively, unreachable performers are covered by existing guild residual funds, and standard audio post-production is explicitly excluded from the definition of cloning. This removed CON’s administrative paralysis and litigation arguments in one exchange.
