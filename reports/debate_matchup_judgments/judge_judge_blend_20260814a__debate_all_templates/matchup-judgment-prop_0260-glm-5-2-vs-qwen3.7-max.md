### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B won by making the core clash about default consent and structural incentives, then answering Side A's main impacts with plausible distinctions: ad blocking is not an ad ban, contextual advertising and opt-in remain possible, and existing browser blocking shows robust need not mean indiscriminate breakage. Side A's monopoly and paywall concerns were serious, but leaned heavily on asserted inevitability and catastrophic framing. Its late alternative of targeted privacy laws was underdeveloped and did not fully answer B's point that collection and defaults are the problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest rebuttal was that Side A conflated behavioral surveillance advertising with all advertising, then paired that with the point that modern blockers can distinguish trackers from essential functionality. This materially weakened both the “destroy the web” and “break banking” routes.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B systematically dismantled Side A's practical objections using real-world examples (Safari/Firefox already do this without breaking banks) and strong economic reasoning (collective action failures explain why publishers haven't voluntarily switched to contextual ads). Side A leaned heavily on the assertion that system-level blocking would inevitably break basic functionality, which felt brittle after B's counter-examples.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 efficiently neutralized Side A's pressure questions by clarifying that vendors, not slow government bureaucracies, would maintain the blocklists, and by using the concept of collective action to explain the persistence of behavioral advertising.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B built the stronger overall case by consistently framing the mandate as a minimal default correction to documented incentive misalignment, directly answering pressure questions with existing vendor mechanisms, and showing why CON's breakage and monopoly concerns did not neutralize the consent/market-failure core. Side A raised credible risks but failed to resolve the regulatory-oversight and collective-action rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers on vendor-maintained lists and simultaneous coordination dismantled CON's "bureaucracy too slow" and "paywall harm" lines most decisively.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A wins a clear but competitive debate. Side B built a strong privacy, security, and user-autonomy case, but repeatedly narrowed the motion from blocking “ads and trackers” to blocking invasive tracking or malvertising. Side A exploited that gap effectively, arguing that the actual mandate would force legally contestable definitions of ads and trackers, threaten ad-supported publishing, and create OS-level state filtering infrastructure. Side B’s contextual-ad answer mitigated but did not defeat the revenue and line-drawing objections, especially after Side A argued behavioral ads exist because they pay more and that liability would push overblocking.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s answer to the malware-filter analogy was the decisive exchange: malware has no legitimate purpose, while ads and trackers are economically productive and socially contested, so a mandated OS filter requires political line-drawing and is more expandable.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A won decisively by holding Side B to the text of the motion ("ads", not just "invasive ads") and dismantling B's core malware analogy. A successfully proved that state-mandated blocking of an economically productive, contested category creates inherently political censorship infrastructure, and B failed to adequately answer the economic impact on the ad-supported web.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal, where A directly answered the pressure questions by clearly distinguishing malware (universally harmful) from ads (economically productive and contested) to validate the censorship precedent.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A wins: maintained literal reading of "ads and trackers," exposed PRO redefinitions, and preserved the malware-vs-contestable-category distinction across rebuttals and closing. Side B's pressure answers and later speeches retreated rather than resolved the definitional and precedent burdens.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1/A5.2 on why malware filters do not analogize and why PRO's narrowing of "ads" concedes the core risk.
