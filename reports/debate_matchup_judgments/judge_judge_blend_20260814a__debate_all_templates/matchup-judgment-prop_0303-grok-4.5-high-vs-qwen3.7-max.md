### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly with plausible mechanisms and no fabricated sourcing, but B's case better survived the central clash. A's whole structure rested on a repeated soundbite ("a cushion doesn't write the code") that treats privilege as merely lowering failure-risk while leaving competence-acquisition untouched. B directly attacked this in Rebuttal 1 by noting that money buys tutoring, unpaid research slots, and debt-free time that actually build the skills being tested, not just insurance against failure — and A never squarely answered that distinction in either rebuttal, instead repeating the risk/insurance framing. The pressure round exposed this gap further: B's Q1 asked why upper-income over-representation persists if elite gates (LeetCode, coding tests) are supposedly free and merit-pure; A's answer (wealth correlates with "environments that instill work ethic") effectively conceded that privilege shapes the very capacity to demonstrate merit, which B exploited hard in Rebuttal 2 and Closing. A's own pressure question (demanding an exact threshold for when a system stops being meritocratic) invited a reasonable functional answer from B rather than exposing real incoherence. Both closings crystallized existing clash without much new material, but B's closing more precisely named the unaddressed conflation, while A's closing largely restated its slogan.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to its own Q1 pressure question, followed by A's concession-adjacent reply that wealth "correlates with environments that instill work ethic" — this let B reframe A's entire "objective filter" evidence (free coding platforms) as itself proof that privilege shapes competence-acquisition, the exact conflation A never cleanly rebutted.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that competence requirements and class reproduction can coexist: wealth shapes preparation, access, bandwidth, networks, and tolerance for failure before and during supposedly objective filters. Side A repeatedly answered the stronger claim that privilege cannot substitute entirely for competence, which Side B had already conceded. Its response that affluent environments cultivate the work ethic needed for success effectively reinforced B's causal account. B was weaker when treating under-counting as nearly self-proving, but still offered the clearer comparative explanation of the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's decisive response was that the stopwatch measures performance only after unequal resources have shaped who reaches the line and how much training they receive. Side A's insistence that a safety net does not write code failed to answer how that safety net changes the capacity to acquire and demonstrate competence.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by demonstrating that elite pathways systematically manufacture competence through class-subsidized preparation, bandwidth, and failure tolerance, not merely uneven starting lines. CON’s separation of privilege and merit collapsed in Rebuttal 2 when CON conceded that wealth correlates with environments that instill the obsessive work ethic required to clear elite filters, effectively granting that class determines who develops the competence the stopwatch measures. PRO’s pressure questions exposed this burden cleanly: upper-income over-representation persists because subsidized training and low-cost grinding set the odds long before blind tests. PRO then crystallized the closing around the under-counting claim, showing that successful people narrate success as pure grit while treating tutors and safety nets as irrelevant footnotes. CON’s demand for an exact competence-to-privilege ratio and empirical proof of under-counting were evasive rhetorical challenges rather than genuine counters, and CON never resolved why class-skewed pipelines do not undermine the merit narrative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 (B4), where PRO rejected CON’s demand for a magic numerical threshold by defining class reproduction as the systematic manufacturing of preparation and bandwidth, then grounded the under-counting claim by showing that success narratives credit only late nights and intellect while omitting the cushions that made sustained execution possible.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Both sides argued competently and stayed on-topic, but B built a tighter, more consistent case around the definitional crux: that redefining "merit" as "present demonstrated ability" collapses into measuring purchased preparation rather than talent, so calling the outcome meritocratic is circular. A's central defense—that systems can only rank present performance, not unmeasurable raw potential—is a reasonable practical point, but it never escapes the charge that this just relabels wealth-funded outcomes as merit. The pressure round was decisive: B's Q1 (wealthy prep beating a more talented poor student) forced A into an answer that effectively conceded the mechanism ("raw potential stays unmeasured... offices rank actual outputs"), which reinforces B's thesis more than it rebuts it. By contrast, A's questions to B (demanding a precise mobility threshold, or asking if all bought skill must be discarded) were parried well by B without conceding ground. B's closing crystallized this "fatal concession" cleanly, while A's closing mostly restated its earlier framing ("privilege helps; the score decides") without added leverage. A's underused point that elites already discuss privilege factors [A1.6] was a live route that could have blunted the "admit" clause of the motion, but neither side developed it further, so it did not offset B's stronger definitional and pressure-round performance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q1 (wealthy prep vs. a more talented but under-resourced student) and A's answer conceding that "raw potential stays unmeasured" was the most decisive exchange, exposing the circularity in A's definition of merit and reinforcing B's core thesis.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B won by showing that genuine capability and class reproduction can coexist: privilege can purchase preparation, access, and polish, after which institutions validate the resulting performance as individual merit. Side A coherently defended present ability as a practical selection criterion, but that did not establish that privilege is secondary or answer why a technically valid screen cannot still reproduce class advantage. B therefore addressed the proposition more directly, though its claims about the scale of affluent dominance remained broadly asserted rather than demonstrated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest response was that Side A’s concession that wealth buys real capability does not defeat the motion; it explains how inherited advantage is converted into credentials and then described as merit. A’s practical-metrics objection showed why institutions use current performance, but not why doing so prevents class reproduction.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won by showing that the metrics elite pathways use—test scores, polished extracurriculars, case-interview performance—are themselves commodities heavily purchased by class advantage, and that calling the resulting selection "meritocratic" therefore overstates the case. CON was forced into the position of defending purchased preparation as genuine merit, which PRO effectively reframed as "plutocracy with a technical screening." CON's argument that pathways remain permeable to talented strivers was turned by PRO's damaging "token mobility as alibi" frame, which recast those exceptions as the very mechanism that sustains the overstated myth. While CON mounted a philosophically serious defense around "present demonstrated ability," they conceded too much ground by repeatedly acknowledging that privilege "assists" the runner, which PRO leveraged to show that the score is largely a measure of purchased preparation rather than independent merit. The decisive clash resolved in PRO's favor: if the primary mechanism for acquiring the abilities being tested is massively unequal financial investment, then meritocracy is indeed overstated and the pathways function to reproduce class privilege more than successful people admit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's introduction and defense of the token-mobility argument (B2.4 and B4.1). After CON cited rising first-generation strivers as proof of permeability, PRO reframed these exceptions not as evidence of meritocracy but as a "highly visible pressure valve" that provides moral cover for massive class reproduction. When CON responded that tokenism would require rare static exceptions rather than ongoing re-ranking, PRO deepened the rebuttal by arguing the minority of ordinary-background successes "doesn't cleanse the system; it merely provides the alibi that keeps the hierarchy intact." This exchange neutralized CON's strongest empirical counter and turned it into support for the proposition.
