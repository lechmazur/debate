### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) carried three decisive advantages through the exchange. First, the limiting principle delivered in Rebuttal 2—"real money for a random outcome at the point of payment"—directly answered CON's slippery-slope worry and excluded battle passes and direct cosmetics, leaving CON's overinclusiveness charge only partially live: CON's residual claim that one loot-box tab contaminates caps on a whole game was never fully established because PRO's principle could reasonably track specific transaction types rather than the whole title. Second, PRO's "default exposure vs. default safety" frame was durable throughout: CON's alternative requires parents to find, configure, and maintain tools against professional manipulation, which leaves children exposed before any protection is activated—PRO made this asymmetry concrete and CON never eliminated it, only partially reframed it. Third, CON's concession in Rebuttal 2—"extracting money from psychologically vulnerable minors is not acceptable"—was a genuine gift PRO exploited cleanly in closing: once the moral harm is conceded, the dispute narrows to which default the law should set, and "protection until conscious opt-in" is more persuasive than "exposure until parents catch up." CON's best remaining argument—the opt-out dilemma (easy opt-out = thin protection; hard opt-out = state paternalism)—was real but PRO's "simple parental authentication" answer, while not airtight, was adequate. CON's Rebuttal 2 being clipped added a modest execution cost. The "precision beats paternalism" framing in CON's closing was rhetorically crisp but came too late to displace the established clash, and the underlying tension between targeting the specific mechanic versus the proposition's game-level framing was more a draw than a win for CON. Overall PRO made the debate's decisive comparison easier to grasp and sustained pressure across rounds better.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers were the most decisive exchange. By offering a clear limiting principle (randomized real-money reward) that excluded battle passes and direct cosmetics, PRO defused CON's slippery-slope argument and simultaneously exposed that CON's "targeted friction" model still leaves children in the default-exposed position. The combination of a workable line and the exploitation of CON's own moral concession ("extracting money from vulnerable minors is unacceptable") turned CON's strongest concession into PRO's closing ammunition.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a competitive but real margin on the back of two arguments CON never fully neutralized. First, the ethical core: using children as a revenue backstop through paid-randomness mechanics is not democratization, and "free access" cannot justify it. CON's revenue-contraction counter was asserted rather than demonstrated, and the museum/student-discount analogy fails because those models do not use variable-reward loops to extract repeat spending. Second, and more decisively, the default-framing insight: under PRO's proposal, a disengaged parent's inaction leaves the child protected; under the status quo, a disengaged parent's inaction leaves the child exposed. CON repeatedly hammered that "the same parent who missed a menu setting will miss the approval notification," but this cuts the wrong way — PRO's system makes inaction safe, the current system makes inaction risky. CON recognized this was the pivot point but never answered it squarely. CON's enforcement dilemma (existing youth accounts only capture already-supervised kids; reaching unsupervised minors needs invasive verification) was their strongest attack, and PRO's "flip the default for unverified users" answer was imperfect — CON correctly noted this still requires some form of adult confirmation. But PRO's reframe that unverified accounts receive protective defaults rather than open access was responsive enough, and CON's "can't have it both ways" argument ultimately depends on PRO's framework being logically circular, which it isn't quite: the burden-flip means the enforcement cost falls on unlocking risk, not on proving adulthood. PRO's clipped Rebuttal 2 is a real execution cost, and CON's closing was crisper and more organized. Overall, however, PRO's ethical structure and the default-direction argument hold the decisive clash after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's consistent reframe that optional versus default protection is the core dispute, and that under a default-safe system a disengaged parent's inaction leaves the child protected rather than exposed. CON raised this as a contradiction ("same parent misses both settings") but never showed why the directionality of inaction shouldn't favor protection — and this asymmetry was the live argument PRO held at the close.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A won by establishing a clear ethical framework and consistently dismantling Side B's practical objections. Side B made a critical logical error in arguing that default limits fail if parents are disengaged; as Side A's framework implies, a default limit successfully protects the child precisely when the parent is not there to approve an increase. Side A also provided a cleaner answer to the enforcement dilemma by proposing safe defaults for unverified accounts, which Side B failed to counter.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answer to the revenue question was highly effective: pointing out that if a safer model earns less than unrestricted youth spending on paid randomness, it proves the risky model is extractive, not that the safeguard is wrong.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by establishing a clearer harm story and a more coherent enforcement model. PRO successfully framed gambling-like monetization as exploiting youth impulse control, rebutted the economic doom argument by showing alternatives exist and the motion is narrow, and decisively answered CON's enforcement objection with a "flip the default" mechanism for unverified accounts. CON failed to engage with that nuance, repeatedly attacking a narrower strawman of existing youth accounts, and never resolved the tension that their own solution depends on the same parental engagement they criticized. PRO's moral weighing—that children should not be the most exploitable customers funding free access—remained live and comparatively stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The enforcement exchange in Rebuttal 2, where PRO argued for locking gambling-like spending by default on unverified accounts until adulthood is confirmed, directly undermining CON's claim that the plan requires universal invasive ID verification. CON never recovered from this pivot.
