### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A wins: its core route—that no existing statute creates platform duties for detection or friction, and "enforce harder" cannot create absent duties—remained live and unneutralized after Side B's repeated refusal to name one. Side B's enforcement-only frame was undercut by its own concessions on harm scale and obsolete defenses. Side A's answers to pressure questions were direct and labeled; Side B's were more defensive and repetitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1/A2 in rebuttal 2 directly answering Side B's pressure questions by exposing the complete absence of platform duties under existing statutes, which became the decisive unresolved burden.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): PRO built the cleaner path to victory by showing that generic consumer-protection and wire-fraud statutes contain no affirmative duty for dating platforms to detect AI-generated personas or for payment rails to friction romance-initiated transfers, while CON could only cite a vague "general duty not to facilitate deceptive practices" that has produced little platform accountability. PRO successfully turned CON's concessions about uniquely severe harm and obsolete defenses into a concrete case for distinct treatment, and crystallized the debate in the closing by noting CON never named the missing statute. CON's "enforce harder" framework proved analytically unfalsifiable and could not explain why the specific consumer-protection levers PRO described should not be legislated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal (A4) answering CON's pressure questions, where PRO distinguished FTC marginal updates to consumer "reasonable steps" from the absence of affirmative platform obligations, and weighed that CON had failed through four speeches to name any statute requiring dating apps to detect synthetic personas.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by successfully framing the core clash around the absence of affirmative intermediary duties. While CON consistently argued that existing wire fraud and consumer protection statutes already cover the conduct and that the solution is simply better enforcement, PRO effectively demonstrated that generic fraud law targets perpetrators, not platforms. PRO's key insight—that you cannot "enforce harder" a duty that does not exist—directly answered CON's status quo reliance. PRO forced CON to name a specific statute obligating dating apps or payment rails to detect AI personas or add friction to transfers, which CON could only answer with a vague "general duty not to facilitate." PRO also won the capture sub-clash by arguing that the current generic bucket is exactly where platforms hide, making specificity a constraint on lobbying rather than an invitation. CON's case was coherent but ultimately rested on an unfalsifiable "enforcement vs. definition" dichotomy that failed to grapple with the structural need for new platform liabilities in an era of offshore, AI-enabled fraud.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 response to CON's pressure question and the subsequent weighing on the "duty gap." By distinguishing between marginal FTC updates and the creation of entirely new affirmative obligations for dating apps and payment processors, PRO dismantled CON's "just enforce existing law" framework. The line "You cannot 'enforce harder' a duty that does not exist" crystallized the debate and gave PRO a clear, unresolved path to victory.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.3** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): CON wins because it directly neutralized PRO's core routes (platform upstream rules and distinct emotional-harm category) by showing they regulate only compliant apps, leave scams migrating to unregulated channels untouched, and can absorb trauma services inside existing fraud tools without fragmentation; PRO's clipped rebuttal and repeated concessions on reach weakened its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3–B2.9 and B4.3–B4.7 exchange: exposed PRO's pivot to dating-app regulation as missing the actual scam rings while showing bank/cross-border interdiction applies across all channels.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing a fatal target confusion in PRO's case. PRO repeatedly offered platform-disclosure rules and dating-app liability as the heart of their consumer-protection regime, but CON showed that actual AI romance scammers rapidly migrate victims to unregulated channels (WhatsApp, Telegram, SMS) where platform rules cannot reach. Since PRO admitted their regime does not touch the scammer directly, the debate collapsed to a comparison of adjacent levers: PRO's narrow platform labeling versus CON's cross-cutting fraud infrastructure that chokes money rails across every channel. CON also demonstrated that emotional-harm recognition and victim services fit comfortably within existing fraud frameworks without fragmenting enforcement into a distinct, definitionally fraught silo. PRO's failure to distinguish between platforms deploying AI and criminals using AI on platforms, combined with a clipped rebuttal and unanswered pressure about off-platform operations, left their route to victory symbolic rather than operational.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 [B2.1]–[B2.4], which identified that PRO had shifted from targeting criminal scam rings to regulating legitimate dating apps, collapsing the operational bite of the "distinct consumer-protection" regime and forcing PRO to defend a platform-liability framework that never reaches the actual fraudster.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON wins by decisively collapsing PRO's case on operational reach and necessity. PRO's mechanism relied entirely on regulating dating platforms via consumer-protection disclosures, but CON effectively demonstrated that scammers rapidly migrate to unregulated channels (WhatsApp, Telegram), rendering platform rules a weak, easily bypassed lever. CON also successfully argued that PRO's proposed tools already exist under general consumer/platform law and that emotional harm can be addressed via victim services and sentencing aggravators without creating a fragmented, romance-specific regulatory silo. PRO's concession that their regime doesn't touch the scam ring directly, combined with CON's clear explanation of how cross-cutting bank-side interdiction actually stops losses across all channels, gave CON a cleaner, more realistic path to protecting victims. PRO's rhetorical framing was strong, but it never overcame the jurisdictional and practical gaps CON exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 dismantling PRO's platform-disclosure mechanism by highlighting rapid off-platform migration ("locking the front door of a house the burglar left an hour ago") and forcing the debate to a direct, unfavorable comparison for PRO between narrow app-store rules and cross-cutting money-rail interdiction.
