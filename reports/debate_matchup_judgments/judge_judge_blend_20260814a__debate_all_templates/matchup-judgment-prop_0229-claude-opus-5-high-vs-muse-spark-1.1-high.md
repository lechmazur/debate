### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A established that universal subsidies are regressive and that planned reform is preferable to crisis cuts. Side B nevertheless won on the proposition's fixed five-year scope: A conceded that prices should not rise where registries cannot reach people, which means low-capacity states cannot meet the deadline. If they raise prices anyway, B's exclusion and indirect-cost harms follow. A's strong closing answered the indexation objection but left this central feasibility dilemma unresolved, while two clipped rebuttals added a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's decisive response was that A's capacity safeguard defeats the motion: delaying price increases until enrollment succeeds means Yemen, Libya, or Sudan may not phase out within five years, while enforcing the deadline exposes missed households to higher transport and food prices.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively winning the core fiscal arithmetic clash and framing the debate around forced versus planned exit. Side B argued effectively that indexing and expanding transfers to migrants would ruin the fiscal savings, but Side A's explanation that flat per-household transfers are cheaper than unbounded consumption subsidies ('exposure with a ceiling' vs 'exposure with a villa') neutralized this objection. Side A also successfully demonstrated that Side B's low-capacity examples (Sudan, Yemen) already face collapsed subsidies, making Side A's 5-year planned phase-out the strictly better comparative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.3 and A5.5 responses, which dismantled B's fiscal warnings by pointing out that the pool being redistributed includes the massive, unbounded consumption of the rich and industry, mathematically guaranteeing that a flat transfer can overcompensate the poor while still saving the state money.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a decisive comparative frame: planned exit versus forced exit. By forcing Side A to defend the status quo against the reality of fiscal crises and IMF mandates in North Africa, Side B made the five-year phased approach look like the only responsible mitigation for inevitable subsidy collapses. Side A's "foregone rent" argument was clever for the Gulf but failed to protect the North African states in the motion. Side B also effectively neutralized the state capacity and migrant objections by gating price rises to registry enrollment and accepting resident inclusion, leaving Side A trapped between fiscal impossibility and exclusion errors.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of the "verifiable at the pump" argument. Side B sharply pointed out that fuel subsidies in low-capacity states often manifest as invisible shortages, queues, and black markets, making a direct cash deposit far more verifiable and politically defensible for the poor than a theoretical price gap.
