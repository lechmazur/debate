### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): CON built the stronger path to victory through superior pressure questioning and more damaging rebuttals. The "who sets the cap?" question in B3.1 was never adequately answered: PRO's A1 in Rebuttal 2 invented a "predictable, rule-based timeframe" that the motion's own language does not specify, and CON correctly identified this as an invention rather than a policy. PRO also failed to name a single jurisdiction for the cost claim when pressed in B3.2, responding only with "multiple jurisdictions" at "a fraction" — rhetoric, not evidence. CON's selection-bias turn in B2.3 was the debate's most productive analytical moment: rather than simply contesting the appearance-rate data, CON reframed it as proof that community programs succeed by exporting hard cases to neighborhoods, and PRO's closing actually conceded this logic while calling it "smart governance," which CON rightly turned into an admission about displaced risk. PRO's strongest asset — the psychological harm and liberty-as-default arguments — was never squarely refuted by CON, and the probation analogy in A4.3 had real traction, but PRO never converted these into a decisive comparative advantage because the operational gap (no cap, no clear mechanism for hardest cases) remained live throughout. Execution costs also hurt PRO: the opening was clipped, and the cost argument was left vague at exactly the moment CON demanded specificity. CON's own brittle "60–80 percent" figure should be discounted, and CON never offered a reform alternative or engaged the mental health dimension, but these weaknesses did not equal PRO's unanswered burden on the motion's core mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.3 selection-bias reframe, sharpened across Rebuttal 2 and the closing: PRO's embrace of intake screening as "smart governance" was turned against it — community programs post strong appearance rates precisely because they offload hard cases, meaning the motion either leaves those hard cases in a definitional void or releases them once an unspecified clock expires. PRO never closed this loop, making it the debate's decisive unresolved clash.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): B won by turning the presumptive-release qualifier into a burden and cap problem: if high-risk or unremovable cases cannot be held indefinitely, A never explains the cap, authority, or safety plan. A answered the initial straw man that this meant unconditional release and had intuitive rights and cost points, but its cost and compliance support stayed vague and it did not satisfy B's pressure questions. B's selection-bias and hard-case rebuttals left the clearer reason to reject the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's cap dilemma in Rebuttal 2 was most decisive: A said proven risks could be detained but also that a time limit would trigger release, letting B argue the motion either permits indefinite detention in disguise or releases genuinely dangerous cases without a defined solution.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B wins by exposing Side A's core weakness: no workable definition of a detention cap or time limit for proven risks, leaving the motion either theatrical or unsafe.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2's direct hit on the invented "predictable timeframe" and the motion's silence on caps, turning PRO's own screening concession against them.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO built the more coherent and consistently defended case across all rounds. Three advantages held up through the full exchange: (1) cost-effectiveness — CON called PRO's appearance-rate data "cherry-picked" but never supplied a counter-example or mechanism, leaving the cost comparison standing; (2) pull-factor — CON asserted deterrence as "predictable" while simultaneously conceding that record arrivals under detention-heavy regimes reflect push factors, which cut directly against CON's own deterrence logic; (3) framework discipline — PRO repeatedly clarified that the proposition preserves targeted, time-limited, judicially reviewed detention for the genuinely high-risk tail rather than abolishing custody altogether, which defused CON's strongest recurring point. CON's best live argument — that time limits eventually force release of individuals who are unremovable due to non-cooperative states — was partially answered by PRO (identity verification as a precondition; the detained subset with judicial review), though PRO never fully addressed the indefinite-unremovable scenario. Still, CON also never explained how indefinitely warehousing someone whose removal is literally impossible actually advances the stated purpose of detention, leaving this clash roughly neutral rather than a CON win. PRO's closing incorrectly claimed CON "could not name a single category" when B4.1 had in fact listed specific categories, a credibility slip; but CON's closing was clipped for over-length, a real execution cost that offset it. Overall PRO made the cleaner comparative case and kept its burden better defined across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answer to B3.1: PRO explained that identity verification is a precondition for case-management release, meaning no-ID individuals enter the detained subset with judicial review rather than presumptive release. This directly and honestly answered CON's sharpest concern, reframed the policy correctly, and shifted the burden back to CON to explain why that subset would be mishandled — which CON never satisfactorily did.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): A won narrowly because it kept the motion focused on prohibiting open-ended custody while preserving targeted, reviewed detention for genuinely high-risk cases, and it better weighed concrete human, fiscal, and administrative costs against B's more speculative pull-factor and sovereignty claims. But B still did not justify indefinite detention as proportionate or necessary for the broader system, nor did it adequately answer A's majority-case comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was in Rebuttal 2, where it used A's time-limited detention concession to argue that no-ID, fraud, or non-cooperative-state cases would eventually be released. That exposed A's weakest point, though it was not enough to overcome A's broader case against indefinite detention.
