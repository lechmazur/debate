### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate by making the accountability problem feel structural and by answering the main CON alternatives. B effectively argued that accountability can follow law and institutional design, and the fiduciary-duty rebuttal was a real challenge. But B leaned heavily on generic regulatory optimism and an underdeveloped claim that bans drive states into proxies, while A persuasively distinguished combat roles from support roles and framed the current contractor model as uniquely giving states both deniability and legitimacy. A also answered the democratic-choice argument cleanly: a democratic ban is itself a form of democratic control, not its negation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was the response to the shadow-networks argument: a ban need not eliminate every covert abuse; it removes the legally normalized outsourcing of lethal force and forces states either back into accountable public military channels or into politically costly illegitimacy.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): Side A built a stronger comparative case by strictly adhering to the proposition's focus on "combat roles" and the state's monopoly on lethal force. Side B argued well on the theoretical governability of corporations but faltered by treating the motion as a blanket ban on all PMC activity (missing A's crucial distinction between logistics and combat). Side A capitalized on this by proving that outsourcing lethal decisions carries unique structural risks to democratic consent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing argument effectively neutralized Side B's point about regulating other industries by sharply distinguishing between economic activity and the state's monopoly on lethal force.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by showing that the accountability gap Side B (PRO) identified stems from mutable legal design and political-will failures rather than structural inevitability. CON successfully rebutted PRO's core fiduciary-duty claim by demonstrating that fiduciary obligations are statutory constructs routinely modified for public goods, and blunted the enforcement-gap argument with the "proves too much" parallel to labor and environmental reform. CON's shadow-networks objection—that a blanket ban would redirect capability needs into less accountable proxies—remained inadequately answered, and CON maintained the more coherent democratic-theory frame: democratic accountability is achieved by closing governance gaps through law, not by preemptively removing a policy tool from democratic deliberation. PRO's case retained rhetorical force but could not establish that PMCs are uniquely or permanently ungovernable among regulated corporate activities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8) dismantling of PRO's fiduciary-duty immutability and enforcement-gap claims—showing fiduciary duties are statutory constructs that legislatures routinely modify for public goods, and that persistent political resistance does not prove structural impossibility—was the decisive exchange that most damaged PRO's path to victory.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): B won by showing that A proved current enforcement gaps, not that contractor combat roles are structurally incapable of democratic accountability or that a ban is superior to regulation. A’s Nisour Square example and authorization-versus-enforcement distinction were strong, but B kept alive the points that these gaps are statutory, that uniformed militaries also fail accountability, that a combat-role ban creates its own gray zones, and that banning visible contractors may push operations into less accountable channels.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive rebuttal was the “hard to regulate is not structurally unregulable” exchange, paired with the argument that the same ambiguity A attacks in regulation would migrate to defining and enforcing a combat-role ban.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A won by successfully proving that the enforcement gap for contractors is structural (rooted in commercial structures and profit motives) rather than just a solvable policy oversight. Side A effectively neutralized Side B's political feasibility arguments by correctly noting the proposition asks what *should* be done, not what Congress will easily do.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively destroyed Side B's reliance on tactical direction with the sharp distinction: "Contract termination is a business remedy for a democratic failure."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by showing that democratic accountability requires enforceable mechanisms, not merely authorization, and that PMCs structurally evade military justice, transparency, and oversight due to commercial confidentiality and jurisdictional gaps. While Side A (CON) offered credible regulatory alternatives and a compelling covert-action concern, PRO successfully undermined the regulatory-fix argument by highlighting fifteen years of legislative failure and distinguishing tactical military direction from genuine enforcement authority. The structural accountability gap proved more decisive than CON's optimism about future regulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to Q2 distinguishing tactical direction from accountability—arguing that commanders cannot court-martial contractors, access personnel records, or compel testimony, reducing contract termination to a business remedy rather than democratic enforcement.
