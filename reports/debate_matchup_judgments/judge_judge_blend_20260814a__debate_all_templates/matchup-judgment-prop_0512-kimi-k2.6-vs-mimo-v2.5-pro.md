### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): PRO built a cleaner and more adaptive path to victory across the full exchange. The conditional framing—"if companies truly pay their way, the cap never binds"—was PRO's central structural claim, and CON never fully neutralized it. CON's best counter was the "belt over a belt" argument: PRO's own Rebuttal 2 conceded that utilities already bill industrial customers for grid and water costs through interconnection agreements and capacity contracts, so the cap is either redundant or undefined. But PRO's closing delivered the critical distinction CON missed: utility billing and legislative tax credits are two separate fiscal instruments. A developer can pay every interconnection fee and still receive a tax subsidy that leaves ratepayers covering a remaining gap. The cap applies to the second category, which existing utility mechanisms do not reach. CON's belt-over-belt argument targets the first category and slides past the second. PRO operationalized the threshold in Rebuttal 2 (documented marginal cost via utility interconnection agreements and water capacity contracts, adjudicated by the same agency already issuing the credit), which materially answered CON's sharpest pressure questions. CON's responses to those answers in Rebuttal 2 focused on the chilling-effect-before-adjudication concern, which retained some force but was partially addressed by PRO's contractual-precondition framing. CON's closing was also clipped, a real execution cost at the crystallization stage. PRO's closing was concise and landed the "paid invoice before cashing the public check" frame cleanly. CON argued well throughout—the selective-rigor point, the elastic-standard dilemma, and the chilling-effect-precedes-adjudication argument all had genuine bite—but none fully closed the gap PRO identified between utility billing and separate legislative subsidies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure questions (Q1: concrete threshold? Q2: who adjudicates?) followed by PRO's Rebuttal 2 answers were the most decisive exchange. PRO operationalized the standard using existing utility interconnection agreements and public utility commissions as adjudicators, reducing CON's strongest attack from a structural objection to a degree-of-chilling-effect dispute. CON's follow-up "belt over a belt" response was strong but missed the utility-billing/tax-credit distinction that PRO landed in the closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A won by effectively resolving Side B's core dilemma (that the policy is either redundant or an impossible veto). Side A clarified that while utilities may bill for costs, legislatures hand out tax incentives that offset them; linking the two ensures public assets aren't drained by corporate welfare.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's final explanation that existing utility structures do not stop legislatures from handing out tax breaks that leave ratepayers covering the gap, effectively dismantling Side B's 'redundancy' argument.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A wins by successfully splitting Side B's core "redundant or veto" dichotomy. While B effectively pressured A on the operational definition of "full cost internalization," A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing cleanly distinguished utility cost recovery from legislative tax incentives: existing rate structures bill developers, but they do not stop legislatures from issuing tax credits that effectively refund those costs back to the public. By framing the cap as a contractual precondition tying the subsidy to the utility invoice, A provided a concrete, legible mechanism that directly answered B's redundancy and uncertainty charges. B's chilling-effect argument remained plausible but abstract, and the clipped closing slightly hampered its final crystallization. A's sharper policy distinction and cleaner rhetorical framing made its path to victory clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) directly answering B's pressure questions by specifying that the threshold is documented marginal cost in interconnection agreements and the adjudicator is the PUC. This concretized the mechanism, neutralized the "undefined standard" attack, and set up the winning distinction between utility billing and legislative subsidies.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A built the stronger cumulative case across the exchange. Its three-part frame—double subsidy, timing mismatch (infrastructure costs arrive immediately while abated tax revenue is years away), and the consumptive-water distinction—held up across rounds. The consumptive water point is the most decisive single clash: Side A correctly identified that evaporated cooling water leaves no shared durable asset behind, and Side B's A1 response (expanded treatment capacity, upgraded mains) describes the infrastructure delivering water to the facility, not what remains after evaporation. That gap was never closed. Side A's "self-enforcing elegance" framing—if you internalize costs you face no cap—was also largely undefeated: Side B's capital-flight counter-narrative depended on assuming the project was viable only by externalizing costs, which Side A acknowledged and turned into a feature rather than a bug. On the limiting-principle pressure, Side B finally answered in A2 (development agreements, incremental rate tiers), but this answer concedes those tools can run alongside uncapped incentives rather than as a precondition, leaving Side A's core condition intact. Side B's strongest unresolved point is that "full" internalization is an impossible standard for genuinely shared infrastructure—a real tension in the proposition's language—and the auto plant/steel mill consistency challenge had some bite. But these scored only partial damage. Side A carried three clipped speeches (a real execution cost), yet the visible arguments still outperformed. Side B's case was competent and raised genuine concerns about shared infrastructure and capital flight, but it lacked a clean answer to consumptive water use and provided its limiting principle too late and too narrowly to win the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 water distinction: "water is not a shared durable asset; it is consumed and depleted. Millions of gallons daily drawn from an aquifer or municipal supply do not linger to benefit the community after the data center's cooling systems are done with them." This separated water from grid infrastructure in a way that exposed the category error underlying Side B's entire shared-benefits argument, and Side B never adequately closed that gap.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won by successfully framing 'full internalization' as an impossible standard for shared public infrastructure, and by exposing execution flaws in Side A's case. Side A clipped three of its speeches due to length, which cost them analytical finish. Furthermore, Side A undermined its own credibility by claiming Side B 'never answered' their question before Side B had even spoken, and then repeating the claim in the closing while simultaneously critiquing Side B's exact answer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 masterfully answered the pressure question on evaporated water by distinguishing the consumptive water (paid for via volumetric rates) from the durable infrastructure left behind (mains and treatment capacity).
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B wins by successfully challenging the practicality of “full internalization” and offering a more concrete, policy-realistic alternative. While Side A effectively highlighted the timing mismatch and consumptive water costs, Side B consistently pressed the walk-away scenario, demonstrated how existing tools (impact fees, rate tiers, bonds) already provide a limiting principle without killing deals, and exposed the contradiction in treating shared, durable infrastructure as a private bill. Side B’s rebuttals did more comparative work, directly answered pressure with the required format, and crystallized the clash around practical governance versus a rigid accounting standard that risks capital flight. Side A’s case was principled and clear but ultimately relied on dismissing Side B’s alternatives as “hollow” without fully explaining why proportional recovery fails for hyperscale loads, and the repeated clipping of Side A’s turns slightly hampered its ability to fully develop its weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 [B4.1-B4.2] directly answering the pressure questions. It cleanly separated consumptive water from system capacity, articulated a clear limiting principle (proportional cost recovery via standard development tools), and turned Side A’s “self-enforcing” claim into a liability by highlighting the walk-away scenario. This exchange decisively shifted the debate from abstract fairness to practical policy mechanics.
