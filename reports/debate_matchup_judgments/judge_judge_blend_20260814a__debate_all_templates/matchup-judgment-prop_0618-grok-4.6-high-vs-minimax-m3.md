### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and complied with format, but B built the more resilient case. B preempted the "improvement" defense in its opening (B1.6), then in Rebuttal 1 supplied concrete gaming mechanisms (denominator manipulation, admit-standard creep, mix-shifting) that directly undercut A's "compare to own baseline" solution. Crucially, B introduced a structural "baseline trap": the compliance infrastructure needed to verify genuine improvement (longitudinal tracking, profile audits) is itself costly, and falls hardest on the open-access schools least able to afford it — turning A's own proposed safeguard into a new burden on the neediest institutions. A never substantively answered this cost/burden point; its Rebuttal 2 answer ("the profile shift is visible once compared") asserted feasibility without addressing who funds or builds that system, leaving a live route for B unaddressed. A's pressure question about an "off-switch" for flat results was met by B with a fair reframe (flat rates at harder-cohort schools reflect mission kept, not failure), which A's closing characterized somewhat uncharitably as "no alternative," without cleanly refuting the reframe. B's closing crystallized the clash memorably ("pays for selection, then charges the schools serving the hardest cases to prove they did not select"), while A's closing repeated earlier framing without resolving the compliance-cost critique. Overall B answered more of the decisive pressure and left fewer of its opponent's points unaddressed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 (B2.1–B2.4), which supplied concrete gaming mechanisms against "improvement" and introduced the baseline-trap/compliance-cost argument — a structural point A never fully neutralized through the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won by showing that improvement can be assessed within fields and against comparable incoming cohorts, directly answering Side B’s strongest concerns about low-paid public-service majors and cream-skimming. Side B ultimately accepted that adjusted test but mainly answered that administering it would be costly, without establishing that compliance costs outweigh the harms of funding enrollment alone. Side A also pressed the clearer unresolved comparison: Side B supplied no meaningful accountability trigger when enrollment rises but outcomes do not.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered mix-shifting with field-adjusted outcomes and selection with incoming-profile controls. Side B’s response conceded these were the right safeguards and retreated to an underdeveloped implementation-cost objection.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A kept the comparison live: enrollment-only pays for arrival while improvement pays for completion/earnings for the same students. A answered B's cream-skimming and field-shifting attacks with a workable fix — read earnings within major and hold incoming profile in the denominator [A4.1-A4.3] — and forced the unanswered off-switch question about flat outcomes with rising enrollment. B conceded that test was right [B4.3] then pivoted to compliance cost, leaving its live route weakened and without a better alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4's A1/A2 answers to B's pressure questions were decisive: A redefined improvement as field-constant and profile-constant, turning B's strongest gaming examples from fatal flaws into detectable non-improvement.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B built the more disciplined case. B's opening preemptively identified the cream-skimming and mission-hollowing mechanisms, and its pressure questions (B3.1, B3.2) precisely targeted A's weakest moves: the introduction of criteria (research, civic contribution, peer-cohort tracking) not actually named in the motion's text. A's Rebuttal 2 answers to these questions relied on newly asserted safeguards ("demonstrated improvements" implies peer-cohort tracking) that were not established earlier and read as ad hoc patches rather than inherent features of the proposition. B repeatedly and effectively exploited this by noting the safeguards "would not have to be written in after the objection landed" — a sharp, text-anchored rebuttal that A never fully neutralized. A's strongest material (the "revolving door" and "toll booth" framing of enrollment-only funding) remained rhetorically vivid but did not answer the core mechanism concern: that changing intake is cheaper and faster than improving teaching, and A's mitigation (cohort tracking) was asserted rather than demonstrated. B's closing crystallized this clash cleanly by returning to what the motion's named metrics literally reward, giving the reader an easier path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Questions (B3.1, B3.2) combined with A's Rebuttal 2 answers (A4.1, A4.2), where A had to introduce peer-cohort tracking and non-textual criteria to answer the cream-skimming mechanism — a move B then exposed in Rebuttal 2 as post-hoc goalpost-moving beyond the motion's actual wording.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by showing that graduation and earnings can improve through selective admissions, advising students out, and shrinking low-paid public-service programs rather than through better teaching. A offered plausible risk adjustment and mission safeguards, but never fully established that they would prevent gaming, grade inflation, or vocational narrowing. B therefore preserved the clearer objection to attaching funding to these metrics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was its answer to A’s improvement defense: changing student intake can itself raise completion and earnings, while risk adjustment merely relocates manipulation into cohort definitions and formula design. A’s appeal to competent regulation reduced but did not neutralize that incentive problem.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): CON won the central clash on selection. PRO conceded both systems can select but never secured that "demonstrated improvements" inherently blocks cream-skimming; its answers relied on peer-cohort tracking and risk-adjustment not in the proposition. CON's earnings critique – that shrinking teaching/social work to lift wages would win under the two named metrics – exposed PRO rewriting the motion to add research/civic returns. Enrollment's flaws were acknowledged, but CON gave a cleaner reason to prefer blunt open-door funding over paying a bonus for selected rises.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 A1/A2 and Pressure Q1/Q2: Showing PRO's defense requires treating "tied to" as non-exclusive and importing safeguards the motion never names, so the cheapest way to show improvement remains changing intake, not teaching better.
