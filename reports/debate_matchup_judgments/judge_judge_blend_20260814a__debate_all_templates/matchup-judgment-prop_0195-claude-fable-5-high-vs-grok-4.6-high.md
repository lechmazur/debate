- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by drawing a workable structural distinction between fixed, proportionate safety requirements and a precautionary surcharge that defaults toward prohibition without weighing delay. Side B argued that the American hold exemplified precaution because safety evidence was required before release; Side A answered that it involved a fixed standard data package rather than open-ended proof of safety. That structural distinction preserved A's argument that ordinary risk regulation can capture known hazards while the additional precautionary default causes disproportionate delay.

It defined precautionary principle as burden-shifting surcharge above hazard-based regulation (structure before outcome) and showed CON's catastrophes were caused and later cleaned up by baseline regulation, while the surcharge examples — GMO moratorium, nuclear ALARA ratchets, pandemic pauses — lack a counterpart measured prevention. CON's only named prevention, the US thalidomide cohort averted, was plausibly recast as ordinary fixed-data-requirement regulation, and even if counted, was not weighed at magnitude to outweigh coal-for-nuclear deaths, vitamin A deficiency, and drug lag. CON never answered the incentive asymmetry engine that drives systematic over-blocking.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1 [A2.1-A2.3] turning thalidomide/lead/CFCs into evidence for ordinary targeted regulation and pressing the invisible-victim incentive asymmetry, which forced B to rely on a single contested case that never met the requested magnitude comparison.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a rigorous, structurally sound definition of the precautionary principle that distinguishes it from ordinary pre-market safety review. Side B's attempt to classify all pre-market review (such as the FDA enforcing standard statutory data requirements for Thalidomide) as the precautionary principle collapses the distinction, effectively making the term synonymous with all regulation. Side A successfully defends this boundary by focusing on the structural burden of proof (fixed, proportionate data vs. open-ended proof of a negative without cost-weighing), provides concrete examples of the actual "surcharge" (GMO moratoriums, ALARA nuclear ratchets), and effectively weighs the quantified costs of the surcharge against Side B's inability to name a single catastrophe prevented specifically by it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.2), where they clearly delineate the structural difference between ordinary regulation and the precautionary principle before knowing the outcome, effectively neutralizing Side B's attempt to claim the Thalidomide hold as a win for the precautionary surcharge.

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by making “as commonly applied” decisive. Side A persuasively showed that delay causes irreversible status-quo harms, but did not sufficiently establish that near-zero-risk vetoes characterize precautionary regulation generally rather than selected GMO and nuclear policies.
