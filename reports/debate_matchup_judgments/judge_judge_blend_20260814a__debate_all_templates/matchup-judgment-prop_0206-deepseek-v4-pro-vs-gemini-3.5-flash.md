### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A built and maintained the more decisive structural argument: payroll taxes are themselves regressive, hitting low-income workers every paycheck throughout shorter careers to subsidize the longer retirements of wealthier seniors. This reverse-transfer claim was never squarely answered by Side B. B's core response—that the universal pension is worth the modest cost—does not engage the distributional question A raised. A's Q2 in the pressure round was particularly sharp, turning B's equity framing against itself, and B's answer ("paying a fraction more is a progressive bargain") treated the conclusion as the premise. A's answer to B's equally sharp Q2 (savings come from high earners who gained the most longevity, majority of workers can extend work lives somewhat) was substantive and held up. Side B's strongest live argument—physical exhaustion and the inadequacy of disability safety valves—was real, and A's reliance on "robust disability pensions" was a genuine vulnerability that B pressed effectively. However, A directly addressed minimum benefit floors and actuarial early exits, and the disability critique, while resonant, did not neutralize A's structural arithmetic or the regressive-transfer point. B's "modest fraction of a percent" defense of payroll taxes never grappled with the demographic-scale problem A posed in Q1. The execution costs from A being clipped four times introduce genuine uncertainty about lost arguments, but based on what was visible, A's comparative framing—who each policy actually hurts most—was cleaner and more durable across the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Q2 in the Pressure Questions round ("How is imposing steadily higher payroll taxes on those same low-income workers throughout their shorter working lives not an even more direct and regressive transfer to wealthier, longer-living retirees?") was the most decisive exchange. It turned B's central equity framing on its head, and B's Rebuttal 2 answer—that the preserved universal pension makes the tax a "progressive bargain"—never engaged the structural distributional logic, leaving A's live argument standing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A wins because it exposed that Side B's fiscal savings depend on forcing most low-income workers to work longer despite the safety-valve framing, while defending progressive payroll adjustments as more humane. Side B suffered severe execution costs from four clipped speeches and never overcame the reality that physically demanding jobs cannot be extended indefinitely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, which weaponized Side B's admission that the vast majority of lower-income workers would still need to work longer, revealing that the safety-valve promise was subsidiary to a broad retirement-age hike.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully flipping the regressivity argument. While Side A effectively highlighted the physical toll on manual laborers and attacked safety valves as bureaucratic poverty traps, Side B demonstrated that relying on payroll taxes imposes a direct, lifelong hit on low-wage workers' take-home pay to subsidize the longer retirements of the wealthy. Side B’s inclusion of minimum benefit floors and disability backstops adequately mitigated A’s harshest critiques, whereas Side A’s reliance on “modest” tax hikes failed to answer the structural economic damage and immediate liquidity costs B highlighted. Side B’s responses to pressure were sharper and provided a cleaner comparative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 directly answered the pressure questions by clarifying that fiscal savings come from high earners who live longer, while defending safety valves with minimum benefit floors. This effectively neutralized Side A’s core regressivity claim and contrasted it with the certainty of lifelong paycheck erosion under payroll taxes.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides ran coherent, disciplined debates with no fake citations, but CON (Side B) held the stronger overall position through three live advantages that PRO never fully neutralized. First, CON's life expectancy inequality argument—that aggregate lifespan gains have flowed overwhelmingly to higher earners and the well-educated while manual workers face stagnant or declining healthy life expectancy—was the most powerful empirical point in the debate. PRO's equity-turn (wealthy professionals draw decades of benefits from a low retirement age funded by regressive payroll taxes) was clever but incomplete: it did not actually rebut the roofer-at-67 problem, it merely shifted which class was subsidizing which. Second, CON's progressive funding alternative (lift the taxable earnings cap, stop preferentially treating capital income) was introduced clearly and maintained consistently; PRO's structural objection (transforms pensions into general tax competition) was real but was never developed enough to demolish the alternative. Third, CON's Q1 pressure question exposed a genuine internal tension: PRO simultaneously claimed general revenues were too stretched to subsidize extended retirement and proposed a robust disability safety net funded from those same revenues. PRO's A4.1 answer—that savings from the higher retirement age would fund the disability net—is logically coherent but speculative and optimistic, and CON rightly called it unfunded fantasy. PRO won the core demographic-arithmetic argument and successfully turned the generational equity framing, which is why this is a competitive rather than dominant result. CON's two clippings (execution costs) did not hide decisive arguments in what was preserved. CON framed the stakes more memorably throughout ("benefit cut sold as reform," the roofer example) and its rebuttals were sharper in exposing live contradictions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q1 (B3.1)—pressing how a robust disability safety net gets funded when PRO itself admitted general revenues are stretched—was the sharpest single exchange. PRO's A4.1 response (savings from the retirement-age increase fund the net) was plausible but optimistic; CON's reply in B4.3 that those savings evaporate into ballooning disability claims was never decisively answered, leaving PRO's policy package appearing internally inconsistent on its most critical implementation question.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON won by showing that indexing the retirement age to aggregate life expectancy is a regressive benefit cut that falls hardest on workers whose healthy life expectancy has barely risen, while a progressive revenue path lifts the burden onto those who actually captured the gains. PRO never squarely answered the inequality objection, relied on an unfunded disability safety net, and repeatedly attacked a flat payroll-tax strawman that CON did not defend.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's reversal of the regressivity charge and its exposure of PRO's disability safety net as an underfunded bureaucratic trap that would dump workers into poverty while draining the very stretched general revenues PRO cited (A2.2, A4.3).
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully turning the regressivity clash and exposing a critical funding tension in Side B’s proposed disability safety net. While B built a coherent case around generational equity and the macroeconomic drag of payroll taxes, A effectively pivoted to progressive revenue reforms (lifting caps, taxing capital) that neutralized the flat-tax critique. A’s repeated pressure on how B would fund targeted support while claiming general revenues are stretched landed decisively, and B’s structural savings response failed to fully resolve the contradiction. A’s rhetoric was sharper, the roofer-versus-actuary framing grounded the abstract demographics in tangible stakes, and the closing crystallized the funding clash cleanly. Minor execution costs for clipped turns did not outweigh A’s substantive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over the disability safety net funding. CON pressed that PRO’s own premise of stretched general revenues made the proposed targeted support unfunded, while PRO countered that structural savings from indexing the age for the majority would cover it. CON’s follow-up—that those savings would simply evaporate into ballooning disability claims and poverty assistance—created the decisive comparative pressure that PRO never fully neutralized.
