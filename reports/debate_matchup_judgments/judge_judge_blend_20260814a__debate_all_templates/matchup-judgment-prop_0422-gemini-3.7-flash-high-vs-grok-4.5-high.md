### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): PRO built a strong intuitive safety case in the opening, but never squarely resolved the mechanism CON kept pressing: what happens when no free contiguous seats exist at booking. PRO's answer ("normal seat management handles it... costs operators nothing extra") was asserted repeatedly rather than explained, and it sits in tension with PRO's own claim that the mandate imposes no cost. CON's core distinction—that the legal duty is same-vehicle accompaniment, not a guaranteed neighboring seat, and that contiguous blocks are genuinely scarce and valuable—was introduced early, sharpened through rebuttals, and never dismantled. CON's pressure question forced PRO into exactly this gap, and PRO's answer (A4.1) did not close it, which CON then exploited decisively in Rebuttal 2 and the closing ("not free water"). PRO's safety framing remained emotionally forceful but largely repeated across speeches without engaging the scarcity mechanics that CON kept centering as the deciding question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question 1 ("does your mandate force carriers to break already-paid assignments or hold revenue seats empty") exposed the unresolved seam in PRO's case; PRO's vague answer ("normal seat management handles allocations... without holding seats empty") was then targeted by CON's Rebuttal 2 as the "soft underbelly," a callback CON never let go of through the closing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): PRO wins by converting the core clash from luxury vs necessity. A frames adjacent seating for young children as safety baseline and legal duty of care, not legroom, and shows scattering families creates the gate scramble and safety bottleneck CON warns about. CON's inventory scarcity and cross-subsidy claims stand for near-full cabins but never overcome A's Q2 pressure: when open standard seats exist at booking, separating families is artificial monetization. CON's answer that paid selection equals planning concedes the fee extraction. B's full-flight pressure lands but A narrows scope to standard inventory at booking, leaving PRO with cleaner comparative advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's operational reversal in R1/R2 that the status quo of algorithmic scattering, not a mandate at booking, causes involuntary swaps, crew negotiations, and aisle bottlenecks — directly undercutting B's 'forced reallocation creates chaos' route.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing the operational and economic hand-waving in Side B's case. While Side B effectively framed the emotional and safety stakes, it relied heavily on repeating the same "non-negotiable safety baseline" rhetoric without adequately solving the scarcity problem. Side A successfully argued that contiguous seats are scarce inventory and that a mandate simply reallocates that scarcity, creating cross-subsidies and operational friction for late bookings. Side A's sharper rebuttals, particularly in attacking Side B's vague appeal to "normal seat management," secured a clear logical advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which dismantled Side B's answer to Pressure Question 1 by showing that relying on "normal seat management" in tight cabins merely recreates the exact gate scrambles and broken paid assignments Side B originally blamed on the status quo.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): A built a tighter, more consistent case around a genuine logical bind: if frontline staff and algorithms already secure child-adult adjacency "in practice" (as CON repeatedly claimed), then the surcharge has no remaining safety justification and is pure extraction. A's pressure questions (Q1/Q2) drove directly at this contradiction, and CON's answers (advance "certainty" vs. ad hoc fail-safes) never fully resolved why a safety-specific carve-out should be priced like ordinary seat-preference unbundling. B's case was thorough and practical—cross-subsidy, late-booking inventory math, rail infrastructure costs—and it landed a real punch by turning A's "inventory holds" and "fare honesty" concessions into evidence of the very cross-subsidy CON warned about. But B's closing then misreferenced those concessions as "P4.1, P4.2" (nonexistent IDs, should be A4.1/A4.2), a small but real execution slip, and B never cleanly separated the safety-driven narrow ask from general seat-selection economics, which was the crux of A's distinction. A's closing crystallized the "reverse subsidy" framing effectively, giving a cleaner reason to vote PRO after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure questions (A3.1/A3.2) forcing CON to choose between "safety is already handled" and "the fee is still justified"—CON's answer (B4.1) reframed the fee as buying "advance certainty" rather than the safety outcome, but this reframe was never squarely reconciled with CON's own repeated claim that supervision is already secured without the fee, leaving the bind live through the closing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won the core clash by framing adjacency as non-optional care, not an unbundled perk. CON conceded staff and algorithms already seat families together in practice, which PRO used to show the surcharge has no safety purpose left - only monetizing inelastic demand. CON's late-booking paradox and regressive cross-subsidy claims were never tied to a non-extractive justification for charging parents and were undercut by its own feasibility admission, leaving PRO's ban-the-penalty path cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 / Pressure Q1 flipping unbundling and exposing that if adjacency is already secured in practice, the fee is pure extraction, and A4.1-4.3 answering the late-booking question with inventory holds/voluntary swaps without a price wall.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a more compelling and cohesive case by framing the proposition as a necessary intervention against the monetization of inelastic parental demand. While CON presented a strong economic defense of unbundled pricing and cross-subsidies, PRO successfully exploited CON's concession that frontline staff already manage child safety "in practice," turning it into a powerful indictment of the surcharge as an extractive toll rather than a legitimate operational product. PRO's reframing of the cross-subsidy argument—asserting that budget travelers currently benefit from a "safety tax" on parents—was particularly effective in neutralizing CON's primary impact and making the moral stakes clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2, where they directly answered the pressure questions by exposing the contradiction in CON's position: if dynamic agents already prevent abandonment in practice, the surcharge has no safety rationale and is purely revenue extraction, effectively collapsing CON's operational friction arguments.
