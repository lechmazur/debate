### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO established a genuine moral foundation—waiting time is algorithmically controlled, workers face real penalties for logging off, and effective wages are suppressed below legal minimums—and that core injustice narrative survived the exchange largely intact. However, CON's multi-apping argument was the decisive live route to victory that PRO never cleanly resolved. If each platform owes minimum wage for every minute a worker is active on its app, a driver on four apps simultaneously generates four separate minimum-wage obligations for the same sixty minutes of shared waiting—a windfall no hourly employee receives, or the platform response (exclusivity clauses, login lockouts) that eliminates the multi-apping flexibility PRO claimed to protect. PRO's retainer analogy is creative but legally strained: minimum wage is a floor on compensable labor, not a market rate for multi-client retainer billing, and the analogy breaks down precisely because the same single hour of divided attention cannot simultaneously constitute four distinct hours of labor. CON also correctly noted that platforms can respond to standby liability by cutting per-task rates and capping log-ins, restructuring rather than improving total pay. PRO's answer—that the minimum wage floor prevents total pay from falling below the baseline—was partially satisfying but did not address the login-cap mechanism that eliminates access entirely. PRO's closing admission that platforms already cap logins and set rates unilaterally weakened its own flexibility defense without fully converting it into a PRO win. Across the exchange, CON maintained consistent pressure on the two hardest structural problems with the proposed rule; PRO answered adequately on the margins but left both problems incompletely resolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response in Rebuttal 2 to PRO's retainer analogy (A4.1–A4.2): one hour of divided attention across four apps is not four hours of labor, so four minimum-wage obligations for sixty shared minutes is a genuine stacked windfall, and once platforms face that liability they will impose exclusivity clauses that destroy the very multi-apping flexibility PRO defended. PRO never produced a clean answer to this specific mechanism.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A established a plausible fairness case that platforms benefit from standby labor and should not exclude controlled waiting from minimum wage calculations. But Side B made the decisive clash clearer: simultaneous multi-apping means the rule can create multiple minimum-wage obligations for the same hour, and platforms would likely respond with exclusivity, log-in caps, scheduled shifts, and lower task rates. Side A's answer that each platform receives independent standby value helped, but it did not fully resolve the duplicate-time problem and sometimes sounded internally strained. Side B also did more comparative weighing on flexibility and platform responses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal to Side A's multi-apping answer was the key exchange: it reframed Side A's per-platform liability as stacked wage obligations for one hour of divided attention, then tied that directly to exclusivity and loss of flexibility.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): CON wins by effectively dismantling PRO's solution to the multi-apping dilemma. While PRO cleverly analogized standby pay to a freelancer billing multiple retainers, CON sharply rebutted that minimum wage compensates labor time, not platform value, exposing the legal and economic incoherence of paying four separate wage floors for one hour of divided attention. CON also built a stronger causal chain showing how attaching direct costs to waiting time would inevitably trigger exclusivity clauses, login caps, and rate flattening, undermining PRO's flexibility and wage-gain claims. PRO's dismissals of these downstream effects as red herrings failed to adequately weigh the operational realities CON highlighted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's retainer analogy, correctly distinguishing minimum wage (a legal floor for time/labor) from B2B value billing, which decisively neutralized PRO's attempt to resolve the multi-apping stacked windfall problem and clarified why the motion would force platforms to end multi-apping entirely.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. The core PRO frame—that algorithmic management through acceptance-rate floors, queue priority, and location tracking constitutes employer direction no less than a physical dock—held up well and CON never neutralized it decisively. CON's best argument was the roster-cap fear, but PRO's Q2 pressure question exposed the internal contradiction: if platforms could profitably run with fewer workers, why maintain the surplus now? CON's A2 answer (A4.2) conceded that surplus labor costs platforms nothing under current rules, which directly confirmed PRO's central claim that the business model externalizes waiting costs onto workers. That self-undermining answer was the pivotal moment in the debate. CON's "three-taps-to-log-off" autonomy argument was weakened by PRO's consistent rebuttal that forfeiting queue priority, bonuses, and dispatch ranking is economic coercion even if no formal disciplinary record exists—and the warehouse-worker-on-her-phone analogy blunted the "freedom to browse" counterpoint effectively. PRO also landed the sharper rhetorical frames ("digital dock," "privatizes convenience, socializes cost") and maintained a cleaner consistent logic throughout. CON's arguments were competent and the flexibility/supplemental-worker concern was real, but CON left it underweighted against the concrete sub-minimum effective wages PRO identified. PRO's minor misuse of invented reference IDs (C2.1, P2.1) in closing is a small execution cost but does not change the comparative outcome.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 in the Pressure Questions round was the most decisive exchange. Asking why platforms maintain a surplus of unpaid labor if they could already profitably serve demand with fewer workers forced CON into an answer (A4.2) that conceded platforms maintain oversupply precisely because idle logged-in workers cost nothing under current rules—which is exactly the externalization of waiting costs that PRO argued minimum-wage compliance should stop. CON handed PRO the debate's central concession at its most visible moment.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by making the central clash clearer: active waiting under algorithmic management is still time reserved for the platform, and excluding it lets minimum-wage law be evaded through oversupply and unpaid availability. Side B’s flexibility and roster-cap arguments were competitive, but they leaned heavily on treating economic penalties as merely optional perks and did not fully answer why a legal wage floor should ignore time the platform benefits from controlling. Side A’s closing had some flawed reference labels, but its substantive framing remained stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response that roster caps “admit the scam” was the decisive exchange: it turned Side B’s cost argument into proof that platforms currently rely on unpaid surplus labor, while also reframing caps as internalizing the cost of availability rather than a reason to preserve sub-minimum effective pay.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing algorithmic queue priorities, acceptance thresholds, and bonus structures as de facto employer control, effectively neutralizing Side B’s “voluntary flexibility” argument. A also executed a decisive turn on B’s roster-cap warning, arguing it actually conceded that the current business model relies on externalizing the cost of instant availability onto unpaid labor. A handled pressure questions directly, clarified how counting waiting time closes the minimum-wage evasion loophole, and maintained sharper, more memorable rhetorical framing throughout. B’s case was logically coherent on flexibility and unintended consequences but struggled to disentangle formal incentives from practical economic coercion, leaving its core clash unresolved and its closing largely recapitulative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to the roster-cap argument across Rebuttal 1 and 2, where A flipped B’s prediction into a concession that platforms currently profit by maintaining a cost-free labor surplus. A argued that internalizing waiting-time costs would force efficient scheduling rather than destroy opportunity, successfully shifting the burden of oversupply risk back to the platform and dismantling B’s primary impact scenario.
