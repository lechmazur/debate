### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and this was a genuinely close debate, but A ultimately controlled the decisive exchange. A's pressure question forced B to name a concrete case where the veto "reduced harm" rather than merely killed Council action; B's answer (Hungary, Czechoslovakia, Ukraine) quietly redefined "harm reduced" as "avoided a UN-authorized war it couldn't finish," never actually showing reduced harm to the victims. A exploited this gap cleanly in Rebuttal 2 and the closing, leaving B's central "circuit-breaker" evidence hollowed out. B's best moment—turning A's concession that coalitions already bite into "the stamp isn't worth the walkout"—was a sharp and legitimate pressure point, but A had already pre-empted it with the stigma/legitimacy argument (veto doesn't add enforcement, it subtracts legal cover for coalition action), and repeated that framing consistently to the end. B's case rested heavily on an abstract claim that legitimacy requires continued P5 presence, which is plausible but never got concrete grounding once its main historical examples were undercut. A's throughline (impunity shield, extortion framing, coalition enforcement without need for P5 buy-in) survived the exchange more intact than B's throughline (admission price, circuit-breaker, legitimacy needs P5 in the room).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's dismantling of B's Hungary/Czechoslovakia/Ukraine "circuit-breaker" examples—showing they proved harm continued rather than harm reduced—neutralized B's only concrete evidence for the veto's protective function.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that abolition risks trading imperfect great-power participation for resolutions whose legitimacy and enforcement value deteriorate after withdrawal. Side A plausibly argued that non-P5 coalitions can impose costs, but never fully answered why a Security Council authorization would remain a universal, valuable stamp once major powers reject the institution and construct rival frameworks. B therefore offered the stronger comparison: coalitions can already act, while continued great-power participation is the scarce benefit abolition jeopardizes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to A’s coalition argument was decisive: if coalitions already provide the practical bite, abolition mainly adds formal authorization, yet withdrawal by major powers undermines precisely that authorization’s universality. This turned A’s claimed concession into B’s marginal-benefit versus catastrophic-cost comparison.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully proving that the UNSC's authority is derived entirely from the presence of the great powers, meaning a withdrawal would destroy the very legitimacy Side B was trying to unlock.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, competing frames — A's "veto as licensed impunity" versus B's "veto as pressure valve that keeps enforcement capacity inside the tent" — and the debate stayed genuinely live through both rebuttal rounds. A's pressure questions landed the sharpest blow: if the veto doesn't stop P5 unilateral action (Ukraine) and doesn't stop client-state atrocities (Syria, Gaza), what exactly does it prevent that justifies the cost? B's answer (A2 in R2: it prevents P5-vs-P5 direct war) was clever and internally consistent but remained speculative and unfalsifiable, with no concrete instance offered, whereas A's costs were concrete and repeatedly conceded by B ("Syria and Gaza are not stopped by their occupancy; they are the acknowledged cost"). That concession, repeatedly pressed by A in both rebuttal 2 and closing, was never fully neutralized. B's "verdicts without bailiffs are press releases" was a strong rebuttal, but A's answer — that regional coalitions and willing states already supply real capacity, and the veto's marginal contribution is legal cover for inaction rather than enforcement muscle — was a reasonable, if not fully proven, response that kept A's throughline intact. B's opening was also clipped by the word limit, denying B a fully stated initial case and costing some argumentative completeness. On balance, A pressed the more decisive, more concretely grounded comparison and closed the debate more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure questions (Q1/Q2 in Turn 5) forcing B to admit Syria and Gaza are "the acknowledged cost" of the veto rather than something it prevents — this concession structured the rest of the debate and A repeatedly exploited it in rebuttal 2 and closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive decision by centering the scarce resource: enforceable capacity. A powerfully showed that the veto guarantees paralysis in the hardest cases, but did not adequately establish that regional organizations and willing coalitions could replace the logistics, financing, sanctions reach, and participation lost through major-power withdrawal. B therefore preserved the stronger comparison: abolition may unlock mandates against some abuses, but at the cost of making partial cooperation and enforcement substantially rarer. B’s clipped opening is an execution cost, while A was rhetorically superior, but it does not reverse the substantive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to A’s substitute-capacity claim was decisive: the veto does not create P5 resources, but helps keep those resources available for cases where interests align. By contrasting selective compliance with permanent exit and challenging regional bodies’ ability to coerce withdrawing powers, B exposed the central weakness in A’s proposed rump Council.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's core link between the veto and enforcement capacity. Side B's argument that "selective action when five governments agree does not require a veto" neutralized Side A's main benefit, while B's observation that today's Council also cannot coerce a great power neutralized A's main harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing observation that "CON says a rump cannot coerce a withdrawing great power. Neither can today's Council," combined with the logic that agreeing parties don't need a veto, perfectly collapsed Side A's comparative framework.
