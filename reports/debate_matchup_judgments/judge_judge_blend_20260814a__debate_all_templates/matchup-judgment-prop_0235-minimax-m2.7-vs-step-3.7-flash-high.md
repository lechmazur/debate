### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): The debate was genuinely close, but CON edges out a win on three decisive margins. First, CON's asset-value ratio anchor—that even rising Dutch defense costs represent a tiny fraction of protected assets—was never cleanly defeated. PRO's response was that trajectory matters, not current ratios, but CON held that even projected future costs remain proportionally small and PRO offered no specific quantitative counter to this. Second, CON's A2 in Rebuttal 2 was the sharpest single exchange: when PRO pressed Japan's continuing coastal erosion as proof that zoning fails, CON correctly distinguished zoning's function (preventing new construction exposure) from halting natural geological processes. This preserved CON's governance argument while also partially conceding that natural forces continue—a concession PRO tried to exploit in closing but, critically, PRO's closing was clipped before that argument was completed. Third, PRO suffered two clipped speeches (Rebuttal 1 and Closing), both of which cost presentational coherence at critical moments; CON's closing was complete and crystallized the clash cleanly. PRO's live route to victory—the long-term trajectory argument and lock-in trap framing—remains genuinely compelling and was never fully neutralized. The "managing transition now vs. chaotic forced evacuation" framing in A2 of Rebuttal 2 is PRO's strongest moment. But CON's sustained pressure on the "one-time cost" myth and its effective distinction between dense and sparse coastlines (which PRO partially conceded) gives it the cleaner path to a verdict under the proposition's "on balance" framing for wealthy nations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 in Rebuttal 2: When PRO pressed that Japan's coastlines are still eroding despite decades of strict zoning, CON responded that zoning's explicit purpose is preventing new vulnerable construction—not halting natural geological erosion—making PRO's framing a category error. This was a sharp, specific rebuttal that preserved CON's governance argument while neutralizing the pressure question, even though it implicitly confirmed that natural processes remain unstoppable under any defense regime.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): A wins narrowly because it forced the key cost-benefit comparison: B showed defense costs rise, but never proved they outweigh the value of dense coastal assets, ports, and communities, while A made retreat’s lost output and relocation harms central. B had a strong long-term lock-in frame, but it remained too abstract, was clipped twice, and did not fully answer adaptive defense plus zoning. A’s many overprecise unsupported figures hurt its grounding, but the qualitative comparison survived.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer that Japan-style zoning is meant to limit human exposure, not stop erosion, blunted B’s lock-in pressure and helped reframe continued defense spending as rational protection of valuable assets rather than proof of failure.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by narrowing the clash to the dense, economically vital coastlines that dominate wealthy nations, showing that retreat from these areas would destroy trillions in productive assets and permanent tax revenue with no ecological offset, while hard defenses cost a minute fraction of the value they preserve. PRO’s universal "subscription vs one-time cost" frame collapsed when CON demonstrated that retreat creates ongoing fiscal liabilities and that adaptive governance like zoning mitigates lock-in. PRO never recovered the comparative weighing on GDP, port infrastructure, and household wealth, and two clipped speeches left key burdens underdeveloped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s pressure questions and follow-up rebuttals exposing that PRO’s "one-time retreat cost" actually generates permanent fiscal liabilities from lost tax revenue and displaced households, while cornering PRO into conceding that dense coastlines receive no ecological benefit from retreat—leaving PRO with no offset for catastrophic economic loss.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): CON won on the decisive clash of the debate. PRO's best arguments—perverse incentives, ethical responsibility, co-benefits of retreat—were live throughout, but several execution failures let CON seize the comparison. First, PRO's anchor example conflated Hurricane Ian (which hit Florida's southwest coast) with South Carolina's hardened barriers and post-Katrina levees, repeatedly presenting a geographically confused causal story as a core proof. This undermined credibility on the ground PRO chose to fight on. Second, and most decisively, when PRO answered CON's scale pressure question in Rebuttal 2 by introducing a "phased approach" targeting the highest-risk 20% of properties first, CON immediately and cleanly turned this into the debate's winning move: if 80% of coastal property remains under hard defenses in PRO's own framework, managed retreat is supplementary, not prioritized. PRO's closing did not engage this critique at all, instead cycling back to the perverse-incentives argument as if the 80% point had not been made. CON's "operational versus aspirational" framing—hardened defenses work today, PRO's Coastal Resilience Fund is a future mechanism that does not yet exist—was never neutralized. CON also contributed an original and unrebutted argument: managed retreat funding creates its own moral hazard analogous to the perverse-incentive critique PRO lodged against hard defenses. CON's format issues (two clipped speeches) were minor since the decisive arguments preceded the clips; PRO's closing failure to answer the live 20%/80% burden was the larger execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.2): after PRO introduced a phased approach targeting only the highest-risk 20% of properties, CON immediately identified that this means 80% of coastal property remains under hard defenses—making retreat a narrow carve-out rather than a prioritization. PRO's closing never addressed this, leaving it as the clearest unanswered burden in the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B wins a clear but competitive debate. Side A had the strongest feasibility objection and better epistemic discipline, especially against Side B's brittle and partly confused coastal-damage examples. But Side B made the better comparative case on the motion: high-risk coastlines are not the same as all coastal cities, some critical infrastructure can still be defended, and continued hard-defense priority worsens long-term exposure through perverse incentives. Side A never directly answered Side B's pressure questions about what remains eligible for retreat if critical infrastructure is effectively everything, or how hard defenses can buy time for voluntary retreat while Side A also calls voluntary retreat unworkable at scale. Side A's late claim that Side B only defended a supplement was rhetorically sharp but somewhat overstated, because Side B framed phasing as prioritization over time rather than a permanent 20 percent carve-out.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure-question exchange was decisive: it exposed the tension between Side A's claim that defenses buy time for just retreat and its claim that voluntary retreat cannot scale, and Side A's A1/A2 largely evaded rather than answered that contradiction.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a fatal tension in PRO's case: PRO defended managed retreat as the priority, but under pressure conceded it would begin with only the highest-risk 20% of properties while hard defenses continued for the remaining 80%. CON skillfully turned this into proof that PRO was defending a supplemental carve-out rather than a true prioritization. CON also showed that PRO's proposed "voluntary, community-led" process collapsed into a federally directed crisis mandate once PRO described the needed funding mechanisms and bureaucratic restructuring. CON's operational-realism argument—that hard defenses exist and protect communities now, while scaled managed retreat is an unbuilt promise—remained live because PRO never demonstrated a current program at the required scale. PRO's perverse-incentive and ethical arguments were blunted by CON's counter that buyouts create identical moral hazards and that hard defenses preserve autonomy and intergenerational wealth in place. PRO's closing repeated earlier framing without resolving the scale and voluntarism problems, while CON crystallized the clash around the 80% concession and operational urgency.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal, where it demonstrated that PRO's phased 20% approach meant hard defenses remained the default for 80% of coastal property, and then connected PRO's proposed Coastal Resilience Fund and standardized federal appraisals to the coercive crisis mandate PRO had claimed to reject.
