### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins on the balance of live arguments after the full exchange. CON's most durable argument—that a ban does not stop bad-faith employers but merely strips away the auditable layer, replacing it with unrecorded managerial bias that is harder, not easier, to contest—was stated in the opening, sharpened in every subsequent speech, and never substantively answered by PRO. PRO's closing crystallizes the accountability gap well but still does not explain how employers who are willing to discriminate will be constrained once they simply revert to undocumented gut decisions. CON also identified specific pro-worker benefits (early burnout detection, retention support for low-wage workers, seniority preservation) that PRO only waved away with a generic power-asymmetry claim, never actually rebutting the mechanism. On the other side, PRO's Q2 was the sharpest moment in the debate and drew a near-concession from CON ("there is often no concrete individual evidence linking the score to the adverse action"), keeping the accountability-gap argument alive. PRO's closing distilled this well. However, CON's answer pivoted to a disclosure-plus-audit regime as the better remedy, and that alternative was never dismantled: PRO only noted that audits are post-hoc, not that mandatory pre-decision disclosure would also fail. On net, CON held two unanswered lines—ban swaps auditable harm for invisible harm, and concrete pro-worker benefits are forfeited—while PRO held one strong but partially answered line about the evidentiary gap. That asymmetry, combined with CON's more complete closings and better engagement across rebuttals, earns CON a clear but competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 ("what concrete evidence would allow a regulator or court to prove the prediction itself drove the adverse action?") was the most damaging single exchange. CON's direct answer essentially conceded the evidentiary gap, which briefly appeared decisive. CON recovered by pivoting to mandatory-disclosure requirements as the fix rather than a ban, but PRO's closing never dismantled that alternative, leaving the exchange as a partial draw that damaged CON without fully handing the round to PRO.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that AI predictions create an inherent accountability gap: employers can act on unverifiable probabilities while citing ordinary metrics as cover, and workers cannot prove the hidden driver. Side B conceded in Rebuttal 2 that opacity makes weaponization rational, validating A’s causal story. B’s regulatory alternative—mandatory disclosure and audits—collapsed because if the score is never documented, its influence is unprovable. A sustained this structural argument consistently, answered pressure cleanly, and crystallized the clash in closing, while B never recovered from the tension between its cost-alignment claim and its concession on employer incentives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Rebuttal 2 exchange where A demonstrated that disparate impact review cannot catch individualized pretext because the algorithmic score leaves no observable link to the adverse action, and B’s admission that firms weaponize opacity to avoid liability undermined its own claim that employer incentives naturally favor retention support.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully establishing that the core harm of predictive AI is preemptive and invisible, occurring before any observable conduct or adverse action is recorded. Crucially, Side A's pressure questions exposed a fatal contradiction in Side B's case: after arguing in Rebuttal 1 that replacement costs align employer incentives with worker support, Side B conceded in Rebuttal 2 that employers actually weaponize score opacity to manufacture neutral paper trails and avoid costs.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Pressure Questions round forced Side B to abandon its core economic incentive argument. When Side B conceded in Rebuttal 2 that firms use algorithmic opacity to sidestep retention costs and manufacture facially neutral justifications for penalties, it validated Side A's preemptive harm mechanism and undermined its own claim that market forces naturally align employers with worker support.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won through superior framing, sharper pressure questions, and more decisive crystallization. The core clash came down to whether banning AI prediction tools eliminates manageable bias versus whether it protects workers from preemptive punishment for unacted-on legal choices. PRO successfully established that the decisive harm is category-specific: unionization-likelihood and quit-risk predictions penalize workers for contemplating protected conduct before they act, and no audit mechanism or labor filing can reach those harms when the label is secret and the retaliation is pre-emptive. CON's audit-mechanism answer (A4.1) — compare flagged drops against verifiable output — never closed the gap PRO opened: a neurodivergent worker fired for low keystroke counts before deliverables are measured has no discrepancy to trigger any review. CON's existing-law-suffices answer (A4.2) similarly failed because PRO's Q2 had already identified the no-paper-trail problem, and CON's claim that "aggregate outcomes" in disparate-impact filings solve it understated how secret pre-promotional denials evade exactly that aggregate. PRO also answered CON's Q2 (distinguishing permitted from banned data) with a clear test: permitted tools measure current, transparent, job-relevant output; banned tools scrape indirect proxies to forecast legal, unacted-on choices and penalize workers for those forecasts. CON never produced a comparably crisp counter-test. The execution cost of PRO's clipped B4 speech is real but modest, since the clipped portion came after the core A1/A2 answers were already on record. CON's closing was repetitive rather than crystallizing, leaving PRO's specific scenarios (neurodivergent worker, secret union-risk label) unanswered at the finish.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure questions B3.1 and B3.2 were the most decisive exchange. B3.1 forced CON to explain what audit catches disability-proxy bias before the worker is fired — CON's answer (compare flags against output metrics) was undercut in B5.1 with the crisp point that no measurable discrepancy exists if the worker is fired before deliverables are complete. B3.2 forced CON to show how enforcement discovers a secret unionization-risk label with no paper trail — CON's aggregate-outcomes answer was plausible in theory but never addressed the pre-promotional-denial scenario where no termination record exists.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by showing that the targeted predictions rely on unaccountable, non-job-related proxies to penalize workers for protected, unacted-upon choices. Side B’s audit and labor-law defenses collapsed under A’s pressure: a neurodivergent worker fired for keystroke proxies before completing deliverables cannot trigger an outcome discrepancy, and a secret “union risk” label leaves no paper trail for enforcement. While B correctly noted that human judgment is also biased, A proved the ban addresses a distinct, scalable harm—automated preemptive punishment—that alternative oversight cannot remedy. A’s closing crystallized these concrete harms, while B recycled abstractions without closing the enforcement gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s closing rebuttal of Side B’s audit and labor-law defenses: A showed that outcome audits fail when a worker is fired before deliverables can disprove the proxy, and that disparate-impact law fails when secret algorithmic labels leave no discoverable causal trail, leaving CON with no viable mechanism to prevent preemptive retaliation.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): PRO wins by successfully framing the core harm as preemptive punishment for unacted, legal choices, which systematically neutralizes CON's proposed safeguards. When CON argued that outcome tracking and existing labor enforcement could catch bias and retaliation, PRO sharply rebutted that workers penalized before they can produce output or organize leave no discrepancy or paper trail to audit. PRO also provided a workable line-drawing test (direct current metrics vs. indirect proxy forecasts) and viable alternatives (surveys, stay interviews), answering CON's pressure questions cleanly. CON's case was competent but relied on an idealized view of algorithmic auditing and employer benevolence that PRO effectively punctured. CON's closing reiterated earlier efficiency claims without adapting to PRO's decisive timing/preemption clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing rebuttal to CON's audit mechanism: pointing out that a worker fired or denied a promotion based on a predictive flag has not yet produced the "verifiable output" CON's audit relies on, leaving no measurable discrepancy to trigger review. This cleanly dismantled CON's primary safeguard and crystallized the preemptive harm clash.
