### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) narrowly wins this closely contested debate primarily because its friction-mechanism argument — smartphones require visible, deliberate, socially costly engagement; AR glasses make identification passive, continuous, and undetectable — provided the clearest causal explanation for why AR glasses represent a categorical shift rather than a mere incremental threat. CON never adequately answered this mechanism. CON's best counter ("friction is a habit, not a legal mechanism") actually inadvertently supported PRO's point: if habits erode over time with other surveillance technologies, that is an argument for acting before AR glasses accelerate the erosion, not against it. PRO also answered the pressure questions more directly, giving a specific mechanism for Q1 (friction elimination) while CON's A1 was largely an empirical observation ("it hasn't happened yet") rather than a true mechanism. PRO's framing — that the ban establishes surveillance as the exception and anonymity as the right, while professional exceptions vindicate rather than undermine that structure — was the stronger normative frame and was never fully dismantled. CON's strongest surviving argument was the alternative-policy path (data retention limits, anti-doxxing statutes, breach liability), which PRO did not directly defeat, and CON's point that the ban "catches only honest users while bad actors route around it" was genuinely sharp. But PRO's irreversibility-at-collection argument gave a principled reason why after-the-fact conduct law is insufficient, and the "surveillance as ambient default" framing remained live. Two clipped speeches on Side A (format execution cost) further weigh against CON, even though most substantive content appeared preserved. The margin is real but competitive: CON put together a coherent and rhetorically effective case anchored in a plausible alternative; PRO simply answered the central mechanism question more precisely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's answer to CON's Q1 (what mechanism makes AR glasses different from smartphones). PRO's friction response — that smartphones require visible framing and active use, creating social consequences that self-limit mass identification, while AR glasses make continuous passive identification completely undetectable — was specific, plausible, and directly answered the core empirical challenge. CON's reply that "friction is a habit, not a legal mechanism" and that habits erode was too abstract and actually conceded PRO's point: acknowledging that normative friction is the only current brake on smartphone surveillance implicitly validates why eliminating that friction through AR is a categorical, not incremental, change. This exchange was the pivot on which the debate turned.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A wins by effectively demonstrating how the physical form factor of AR glasses removes the social and physical friction of surveillance, distinguishing it from smartphones. Side B fails to adequately address this friction argument, weakly claiming it is just an erodible habit, and suffers from execution errors including clipped speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which dismantles the smartphone comparison by highlighting the unavoidable social and physical friction of raising a phone, proving that form factor fundamentally alters social reality.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B wins by successfully framing the clash around conduct versus form factor and grounding the debate in existing technological reality. B's smartphone analogy effectively neutralized A's core claim that AR glasses would categorically transform public anonymity; if the lookup capability already exists in billions of pockets without creating the predicted dystopia, the form factor alone cannot justify a preemptive ban. B also won the practical weighing: A's reliance on "social friction" was persuasively reframed as an eroding habit rather than a legal safeguard, and A's "act before ubiquity" stance faltered against B's historical comparisons and enforcement critique. While A articulated a strong principled case about irreversible harm at the moment of capture, B's conduct-based alternative, combined with the collateral damage to accessibility users and the practical unenforceability of device bans, provided a cleaner, more empirically grounded route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over "social friction" was decisive. A argued smartphones require visible, active use that deters mass surveillance, while AR glasses remove this barrier. B sharply countered that friction is merely a social habit, not a legal mechanism, and habits erode with normalization—meaning a ban targeting one form factor cannot preserve anonymity when the underlying capability and harmful conduct remain legal and accessible. This rebuttal collapsed A's categorical distinction and shifted the debate to conduct law, where B held the comparative advantage.
