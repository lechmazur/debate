### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won on the debate's central clashes. First, PRO consistently challenged CON to name a single concrete scenario where an uncorroborated AI flag alone—with no human review—should trigger suspension. CON's best answer was the mass identity-theft scenario (A4.1), but PRO's rebuttal correctly noted that CON's own description included human review running in parallel, meaning the proposition was never actually violated in CON's example. CON effectively conceded the principle while only disputing the enforcement mechanism. Second, PRO's "bright-line erosion" argument was more durable: standards that can be waived will be waived under workload and political pressure, while a "never solely AI" rule creates a violation so clear that dereliction cannot be disguised as compliance. CON's counter—that "meaningful human review" is undefined and therefore unenforceable—had real force, but PRO answered it adequately: an imperfect definition of "meaningful" still produces a clearer line than CON's proposed performance standards, which appeared late, were underdeveloped, and never overcame the objection that they too can be argued around after the harm. PRO also correctly formatted A1/A2 responses, answered questions directly, and maintained argument consistency throughout. CON's multiple clipped speeches—Opening, Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2, and Closing—imposed real execution costs at critical moments; we cannot credit arguments we never saw. PRO's framing that CON's framework "protects agency discretion while the proposition protects the person" was the cleanest comparative weighing in the debate and was never squarely refuted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to CON's mass identity-theft scenario (B5.1): PRO correctly identified that CON's own best case described human review running in parallel with the automated hold, meaning a human does examine the facts before lasting deprivation—which is exactly what the proposition requires. CON's supposed counterexample turned out to confirm the proposition's principle rather than defeat it, and PRO landed this cleanly in the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): A won by making the motion narrow and repeatedly forcing B to defend the actual prohibited act: suspending people solely on an AI suspicion without meaningful review. B’s attacks on “meaningful” being vague were partly compatible with defining better review rather than rejecting the proposition, and sometimes misdescribed A as allowing rubber-stamps. B’s best exception, a brief automated hold during mass identity theft, was plausible and kept the debate competitive, but it arrived mainly as an emergency carveout and did not overcome A’s stronger due-process framing, opacity/false-positive harms, and point that corroborating evidence can trigger rapid human verification rather than AI-only deprivation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s “solely” rebuttal was decisive: it reframed B’s emergency examples as cases with corroborating evidence that the proposition permits, leaving B needing to justify the much harder case of an unreviewed AI-only suspension.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): Side A (PRO) won by successfully pinning Side B (CON) on the core burden: CON never defended a scenario where an AI flag alone, with zero human review, should trigger suspension. CON's strongest example (mass identity theft) explicitly included parallel human review, which PRO correctly turned to show it actually satisfies the motion. PRO also effectively dismantled CON's "redundancy" trap by clarifying how AI triage works (flag → human verification → action) and used the speed camera analogy to sharply distinguish factual capture from algorithmic inference. While CON raised valid concerns about the vagueness of "meaningful," their alternative performance metrics did not justify opposing the motion, and their repeated focus on definitional quibbles left the substantive harm of automated-only suspensions unanswered. PRO's framing of "never" as a necessary, non-negotiable floor against bureaucratic erosion was clearer and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to CON's mass identity-theft scenario in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, correctly noting that concurrent human review satisfies the proposition and exposes CON's inability to defend truly automated-only suspensions.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins on three live routes that PRO never adequately closed. First, the scale argument: CON repeatedly pressed that ten thousand simultaneous coordinated fraud flags cannot each receive meaningful human review before disbursement; PRO's best answer—"stage payments while a human confirms each flag"—either allows fraud to clear or re-creates the deprivation harm PRO claims is impermissible, and PRO never resolved the contradiction. Second, the "never" critique: CON correctly identified that PRO's proposition forecloses even tiered, time-limited, automatically restorative suspensions, which are standard legal instruments (TROs, reasonable-suspicion stops, asset freezes); PRO's closing counter—that all those examples require a human to authorize at the moment of action—was rhetorically strong but did not answer CON's sharper point that all those examples also rest on probabilistic thresholds rather than proved facts, making an AI flag a legally equivalent threshold with human review as adjudication. Third, the comparative harm framing: CON consistently argued a reversible, time-bounded suspension with mandatory review is a lesser harm than permanent, unrecoverable fraud loss; PRO's "retroactive payment is not restitution" reply is true but does not engage the comparison CON draws, only the individual case. PRO had genuine strengths—the due process framework, the "who decides" closing frame, and the "accounts receivable dressed in procedural language" line are all effective—and the bank-card/state-citizen distinction is valid. But PRO's multiple speech clippings are an execution cost, and PRO never named a workable standard that is both meaningful enough to satisfy its own criteria and fast enough to prevent mass fraud at realistic government scale. CON's tiered proposal survived the exchange more intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response in Rebuttal 1 to PRO's "payments can still be monitored" claim: watching money leave while your hands are tied is not fraud prevention, it is fraud observation. This single line directly neutralized PRO's core answer to the scale problem and was never adequately answered, leaving CON's strongest route to victory intact for the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B wins a clear but competitive debate by making the absolutism of “never” the decisive issue. Side A argued powerfully that temporary deprivation of food, shelter, or status is real harm and that post-hoc review is not the same as due process. But Side B better exploited the scale exception: coordinated fraud waves where requiring individualized human review before any suspension makes prevention impossible. Side A never fully solved the ten-thousand-flags problem beyond asserting that review can be contextual and quick, while Side B gave a more workable tiered alternative with expedited post-review and automatic restoration. Side A’s closing point that traditional interim measures still involve a human at the moment of action was strong, but it came too late to overcome B’s repeated weighing that the proposition’s absolute rule blocks necessary temporary safeguards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s most decisive rebuttal was that Side A’s “meaningful review” standard works for twenty files but collapses at thousands of simultaneous coordinated flags, turning the proposition into paralysis rather than process.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully exploiting the operational burden of "never" at scale. While Side A built a compelling moral and due process case around human accountability, it never adequately answered how agencies should handle coordinated fraud waves involving thousands of simultaneous claims without letting funds disperse. Side B's tiered proposal in Rebuttal 2 smartly adapted to pressure, conceding pre-suspension review for individual flags while holding the line on mass events. Side B also effectively parried Side A's closing analogies by distinguishing individual-scale interim measures from mass-scale administrative reality. Side A's "who decides" framing was rhetorically sharp but ultimately couldn't overcome the practical paralysis argument. Side B made the debate easier to decide by clearly weighing reversible temporary harm against irreversible systemic loss and offering a workable sequencing compromise that still preserved human review.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 introduction of the tiered system (individual flags get pre-review; coordinated waves get post-suspension expedited review with automatic restoration) directly answered Side A's pressure while preserving the core CON stance on scale. It forced Side A into a corner where its standard either becomes checkbox compliance or functional paralysis at scale, and Side A's subsequent "stage payments/monitor" response never closed that gap.
