### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO wins on the debate's decisive exchange. CON's strongest claim was that embedded price caps cannot stop off-platform credential sales (screenshots, unmoderated forums), which would render the motion's narrowly tailored limits unenforceable against the scalping harms CON feared. PRO countered in Rebuttal 2 that blanket bans face the identical bypass: scalpers already sell screenshots under total non-transferability regimes every day. CON never answered this turn, and it is fatal—if blanket bans cannot stop off-platform scalping either, CON's core harm story collapses and the comparative advantage shifts to the scalpel-not-axe framing PRO maintained throughout. PRO's safety rebuttal was also clean: a determined harasser can simply purchase a non-transferable seat directly, meaning transfer restrictions are a poor substitute for identity verification at entry, and CON never squarely neutralized this. CON made real points—the resource gap for small community groups, the practical difficulty of vetting every transferee—but these were adequately met by PRO's observation that courts already apply proportionality standards to comparable safety and accessibility rules. CON introduced a new unsupported closing claim (legitimate fans can already get standard refunds) with no prior groundwork, an execution cost in the final round. PRO's consistent scalpel-vs.-axe framing was earned by the argument rather than grafted onto it, and the closing crystallized the clash cleanly without expanding it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 argument that "the army of scalpers already exists" bypassing blanket bans via screenshots and off-platform deals was the most decisive exchange. It directly inverted CON's own off-platform credential-sale argument: if total bans cannot stop those sales either, then the motion's tailored price caps are no worse at preventing scalping than CON's preferred nuclear option, and the buyer's property interest tips the balance. CON never answered this turn.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON carried the three decisive clashes. On enforcement, CON's cash-side-payment-in-the-parking-lot scenario was vivid and mechanically tight: the scalper lists at the legal cap, collects untraceable cash before the gate, and disappears. PRO's answer—that side payments on unlawful transactions are legally unenforceable—misses the practical timing: the scalper already has the money before any barcode check, so the legal unenforceability deters nothing and strands the honest fan. On non-scalping restrictions, PRO's "entry conditions vs. transfer restrictions" distinction worked cleanly for age limits but CON landed a direct hit by pointing to residency requirements and rival-fan-section rules, which operate precisely by restricting to whom a ticket may be transferred—not merely who may enter. PRO never answered that counter. On the litigation-trap claim, CON added the small-promoter cost angle, which PRO left unrebutted. PRO's strongest uncontested point—that official platforms profit from scalper resales and thus have no incentive to enforce caps—was genuine and CON only half-answered it, keeping the margin competitive rather than decisive. But CON's rebuttals were consistently sharper, its concrete examples (community-theater residency tickets, rival sections, parking-lot cash exchange) made the stakes tangible, and its closing crystallized the exchange more effectively than PRO's repetitive "rent-seeking middlemen" refrain. PRO's "entry conditions" move was the cleverest analytical play of the debate, but CON broke it with a single well-chosen example, which is the most decisive rebuttal exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 [A4.1-A4.2] directly attacked PRO's "entry conditions vs. transfer restrictions" distinction by pointing out that residency requirements and rival-fan-section rules function as transfer restrictions—controlling to whom the ticket can be transferred, not merely who may enter. This punctured PRO's main defensive move and PRO had no clean reply, leaving CON's non-scalping-restriction argument largely intact.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won the debate by systematically dismantling Side A's two main defensive mechanisms. When A argued that P2P caps would be enforced through barcode cancellations and the legal unenforceability of side-payments, B astutely pointed out that black market transactions rely on upfront cash, meaning the scalper vanishes with the money and the honest fan is left with a cancelled barcode. Furthermore, B effectively countered A's "entry conditions" reframe by demonstrating that restrictions like local-resident tickets inherently function as transfer restrictions, leaving them vulnerable under the motion. Side A's repetitive and clipped later speeches failed to adapt to this pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 dismantled A's claim about side-payments being legally "unenforceable" by noting that scalpers don't rely on court enforcement; they take the cash and vanish, leaving the buyer stranded.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A prevailed by maintaining a grounded practical critique of enforcement collapse and litigation exposure that Side B never neutralized, even after direct pressure questions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1/A2 answers on entry-condition vs transfer-restriction distinction and anonymous side-payment evasion.
