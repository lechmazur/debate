### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): CON built the stronger cumulative path to victory by consistently identifying and pressing a real structural problem in PRO's case: the motion as written bans training by any company absent explicit pre-death opt-in, with no carve-out for memorial, educational, or language-preservation uses. PRO's successive qualifications—"family can provide opt-in on behalf of the deceased," limiting the rule to "commercial mimicry," distinguishing non-commercial memorials—each either contradicted the motion's plain language or amounted to defending a different, narrower rule. CON correctly caught the family-consent rewrite in Rebuttal 1, noted it strips the very autonomy principle PRO claimed, and held that pressure across subsequent rounds. The "paperwork absolutism" frame in CON's closing was the debate's cleanest crystallizing line: converting the historical absence of an opt-in form into a permanent legal veto manufactures refusal from accident, not from choice. CON's alternative scheme—ex ante limits on private/confidential data, disclosure mandates, and penalties for deceptive impersonation—was specific enough to be credible and targeted the actual harms without the collateral suppression PRO's rule imposes. PRO's strongest argument—that CON's framework still permits commercial exploitation of public data by default—had real force and was never fully neutralized, which is why this is not a dominant result. But PRO's closing was short, failed to address the overbreadth problem cleanly, and left the motion-rewriting contradiction standing. On balance, CON made the debate easier to decide and left PRO defending a narrowed version of the motion that the motion's own language does not support.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 (B2.1–B2.4): catching PRO's claim that "family can provide opt-in on behalf of the deceased" and showing it directly contradicts the motion's requirement that the person must have "explicitly opted in before death." This forced PRO into an awkward walkback in Rebuttal 2 and was the pivot point that weakened PRO's autonomy framework for the rest of the debate—if family consent substitutes, autonomy isn't doing the work; if it doesn't substitute, PRO's earlier concession on non-commercial uses collapses.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by cleanly exposing Side A's attempts to rewrite the motion. Side A tried to carve out exceptions for family consent and non-commercial use, which Side B correctly pointed out contradicted the prompt's strict pre-death opt-in requirement. Side B's argument that the proposition creates a 'retroactive paperwork' veto for those who died before the technology existed remained the strongest, unanswered point in the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal (B2.1) cornered Side A by pointing out that allowing family consent violates the motion's "explicitly opted in before death" clause, effectively neutralizing Side A's attempt to soften the proposition's impact on memorials.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully framing the proposition as an overbroad rule that converts historical silence into a permanent legal veto. While Side B (PRO) built a coherent moral case around autonomy and proactive prevention, they repeatedly had to narrow the motion to commercial mimicry to survive CON's overbreadth charges, which CON correctly flagged as evasive given the proposition's explicit text. CON's tailored alternative framework banning private data scraping, deceptive design, and false endorsement directly answered PRO's exploitation concerns without the collateral damage to memorials, museums, or public archives. CON's rebuttals were sharper, particularly in dismantling PRO's family-consent contradiction and answering pressure questions with precise public/private distinctions. CON also closed with cleaner weighing and more memorable framing around retroactive paperwork absolutism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 sequence, where they exposed Side B's contradiction on family consent versus the motion's explicit pre-death requirement, then cleanly answered pressure by distinguishing private data bans from regulated public material use, proving targeted rules solve the harm without manufacturing a veto from silence.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won on the three most consequential clashes. First, the "authority problem": CON never identified a legitimate decision-maker who could authorize commercial impersonation after death. The company profits from saying yes; heirs can be pressured or disagree; a label is not consent. CON's answer was essentially that public availability of data is sufficient authorization, but that conflates access with permission for synthetic commercial speech—a distinction PRO held consistently throughout. Second, the enforcement clash: CON's best punch was that a ban drives development underground. PRO neutralized it cleanly with the fraud analogy ("we still ban fraud even though some fraud goes underground"), which CON did not squarely rebut. Third, the motion-inflation problem: PRO repeatedly and credibly narrowed the motion to commercial chatbots generating novel synthetic speech, while CON kept re-inflating to language preservation and educational simulators. That inflation was the scaffolding for CON's "draconian ban" rhetoric; once PRO stripped it away, the cost-benefit argument lost much of its force. CON's strongest standalone point—that retroactive consent is impossible because the technology didn't exist when most people were alive—was addressed adequately by PRO (uncertainty cuts toward restraint, not appropriation; categorical posthumous consent is already practiced in wills and digital-legacy settings). Side A's Rebuttal 1 clipping is a real execution cost and depressed the rebuttal score, but the visible content still made the key points, and Side A's Rebuttal 2 answered the pressure questions directly and cleanly with clear A1/A2 labels. CON's closings leaned on enforceability without freshly resolving the authority gap, leaving A's central line of attack live at the end of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to CON's enforcement objection in Rebuttal 2: "No law stops every underground violation, but that is not an argument for legalizing the conduct. We still ban fraud even though some fraud goes underground." This directly neutralized CON's most recurring and structurally important argument—that prohibition merely moves harm underground—and CON never answered it in either subsequent speech.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by clearly distinguishing between passive archiving/quotation and active synthetic impersonation, making a compelling case for why the latter requires affirmative consent. Side B relied heavily on the argument that a ban would drive development underground, which Side A easily dismantled by pointing out that illegality effectively deters mainstream commercial deployment, just like fraud laws.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 (A2) to the pressure question about "unlimited new speech," brilliantly flipping it to argue that the open-ended nature of a chatbot makes prior consent more necessary, not less.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently isolating the unique harm of generative mimicry—manufacturing new speech in a dead person’s voice—and demonstrating that neither corporate discretion, heir vetoes, nor post-hoc labeling can legitimately authorize it. Side B effectively narrowed the motion to deflect Side A’s slippery-slope claims about archives and language preservation, while cleanly answering pressure questions on consent scope and enforcement. Side A raised valid practical concerns about enforceability and grief tools, but repeatedly conflated static public records with interactive impersonation and never resolved the core authority problem: without explicit opt-in, Side A’s framework defaults to commercial exploitation of silence. Side B’s principled framing and tighter clash management made the decision clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 directly answered Side A’s pressure on consent scope and enforcement by analogizing to established posthumous permissions (wills, estates) and standard data-law deterrence, then pivoted to dismantle Side A’s authorization gap. This exchange crystallized why Side A’s reliance on heir opt-outs and existing torts failed to address the unique, pre-emptive harm of synthetic impersonation.
