### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-argued cases, but the debate turned on a single question: does long dwell driven by envy/comparison register to an engagement-optimizer as a "win" (PRO) or as a low-quality signal the system learns to throttle (CON)? PRO's line—"staying and wellbeing come apart"—was sharper and more consistently maintained across rounds, and its Pressure Question (Q1) forced CON to specify exactly which signal treats sticky-but-anxious dwell as harm. CON's answer introduced increasingly specific but unsupported claims (dwell-to-like ratios, "churn in weeks three to eight") that read as invented precision rather than grounded reasoning, weakening its epistemic footing relative to PRO's more general, defensible claims about rewatching, late reopens, and next-day returns. CON's proximal-vs-distal exclusion reframe was genuinely original and the strongest part of its case, but PRO neutralized it by noting the engagement feed can still surface the missed party and then escalate to sharper status content, so dilution is not guaranteed by the ranking objective. PRO's closing crystallized the clash cleanly and left CON's rebuttal resting on an optimistic, under-grounded assumption that platforms reliably self-correct toward mood repair over long horizons.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question asking which signal would classify long dwell + next-day return + no hide as harm rather than a retention win, and CON's answer introducing granular but speculative signal claims (dwell-to-like ratios, "churn in weeks three to eight") that PRO then exploited by noting many users are "hoverers" who don't fit the churn window CON relied on.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that engagement signals can treat anxious dwelling, repeated checking, and continued use as successes even when wellbeing declines. Side A offered a strong proximal-exclusion comparison, but repeatedly assumed that long-term retention requires mood repair and introduced overly specific, unsupported claims about ranking signals and churn timing. B also clarified that harmful comparison need not win every recommendation auction; optimization need only increase its frequency and intensity relative to a bounded feed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s B4-B5 response was decisive: months-long use can coexist with worsening mood, so continued retention does not prove that the system diversified away from harmful material. This directly undercut Side A’s central claim that engagement optimization naturally protects wellbeing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully reframing the chronological baseline as a "live exclusion tracker" of proximal threats, which is developmentally more anxiogenic than the distal comparisons in algorithmic feeds. A also provided a more sophisticated and plausible mechanism for algorithmic incentives, arguing that modern metrics (time-to-return, like ratios) penalize anxious doomscrolling that leads to churn, thereby incentivizing mood-repairing, interest-based content. Side B relied on a simpler "dwell time equals stickiness" model that A effectively dismantled.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered the pressure questions by distinguishing between raw dwell time and complex retention signals (ratio of dwell to likes, time-to-return), effectively neutralizing PRO's claim that envy-driven scrolling is rewarded by the algorithm.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): A built a coherent, escalating causal story—negativity bias plus adolescent social hypersensitivity meaning the ranker systematically over-serves threat/comparison content—and then converted B's own phrase ("uniquely doses whatever already worked") into a load-bearing concession that B never fully de-fanged. A's fixed-time "same minutes, different composition" framing directly answered B's repeated demand for an isolated sorter-vs-clock comparison, even though it relied on a hypothetical rather than real data. B's methodological pressure (self-selection, confounded switching, "amplification isn't manufacture") was strong and kept the "not established" framing alive, but B's Rebuttal 1 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, cutting off its final rebuttal point mid-sentence — a real execution cost per the judging rules. B's closing mostly reasserted the burden-not-met framing without landing a fresh knockout on A's negativity-bias mechanism or the fixed-time dosage argument. Both sides avoided fake citations and stayed within plausible-mechanism territory, though A's "thirty peaks vs five peaks" specificity edged toward invented precision, which B correctly flagged. On balance, A answered pressure more directly, exploited a genuine concession, and closed more decisively, giving a clear but not overwhelming edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.4), where A directly leveraged B's "uniquely doses whatever already worked" line via negativity bias and the fixed-minutes thought experiment, was the most decisive exchange; B's Rebuttal 2 pushback ("that is not a ranking monopoly") was solid but arrived after B's earlier rebuttal had already been clipped, weakening its cumulative force.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by presenting two coherent causal pathways: engagement ranking concentrates sensitivity-matched social threats and prolongs use, thereby worsening rumination and sleep displacement. Side A effectively challenged B's assumption that anxiety always outperforms humor or belonging, but its attempt to classify ranking-induced extra use as a “different claim” was unpersuasive; increased use is plainly a mechanism by which the feed can cause the proposition's harms. B's threat-over-humor premise remained somewhat asserted, keeping the debate competitive, but its combined dose-and-duration case was stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second pressure question exposed the key weakness in Side A's position: if a following-only feed stalls sooner and sleep displacement causes distress, then engagement optimization can harm through the additional use it generates. Side A answered directly but incorrectly treated that causal pathway as outside the motion.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B built a much more persuasive and mechanistically sound case. B's argument about "dose" and "density" (30 peaks of emotional content vs. 5 peaks plus mundane dead zones in chronological feeds) effectively isolated the harm of the sort order. B also provided a highly plausible explanation for why engagement algorithms favor anxiety-inducing content for teens (negativity bias, longer dwell time on threats vs. quick swipes on jokes). Side A had strong points about FOMO in chronological feeds but relied too heavily on demanding an "isolated comparison" and struggled to convincingly rebut B's psychological mechanisms. A's clipped turn and defensive closing further weakened its position.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they answered the pressure questions by contrasting the quick swipe of a joke with the pause/stare/save of an anxiety-inducing video, providing a concrete and plausible mechanism for how the algorithm optimizes for teen distress.
