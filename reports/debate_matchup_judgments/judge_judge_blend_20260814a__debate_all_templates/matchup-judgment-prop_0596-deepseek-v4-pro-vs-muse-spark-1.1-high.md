### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by turning Side A's innovation objection against the status quo: an unaffordable cure with almost no buyers is itself a weak revenue model, while lower prices, expanded volume, and royalties can preserve returns. B also defended a narrow last-resort trigger and showed that reasonable voluntary pricing could avoid licensing. A repeatedly asserted that licensing would erase investment, but never established why poor-country licenses must eliminate rich-market revenue or why royalties on much larger volume could not compensate. Its proposed access alternative was mostly hope that prices would eventually fall. Two clipped turns and brittle cost claims further weakened A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to the finite-market challenge was decisive: one-time treatment does not mean demand disappears during the patent term because millions remain untreated and new patients continue to be born. That directly undercut A's central arithmetic, while A never squarely disproved B's expanded-volume model.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B successfully turned Side A's core economic argument. While Side A insisted that compulsory licensing destroys R&D incentives, Side B mathematically demonstrated that monetizing unserved global populations yields more total revenue than selling to a tiny fraction of the market at prices payers refuse. Side A failed to overcome Side B's point that royalties would stack on top of high-income sales and that demand renews annually.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Turn 7 (A1), which cleanly neutralized Side A's "self-erasing market" claim by pointing out the 300,000 annual new births and proving that volume-based royalties outperform a $2.2M price tag that nobody pays.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): CON built the cleaner path by repeatedly locking the debate on one-time curative economics: R&D must be recovered from a single episode in a finite patient pool, so a compelled "affordable" royalty collapses the capital case in a way chronic HIV drugs never did. PRO's volume-expansion arithmetic and "credible threat" negotiation story never overcame that structural difference or the chilling effect on the whole pipeline; CON's consistent "you cannot access what no incentive creates" framing therefore decided the clash more decisively after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's R1/R2 dismantling of the HIV analogy (recurring high-income volume vs. one-shot finite market that shrinks its own demand) most cleanly neutralized PRO's central precedent and volume-rescue math.
