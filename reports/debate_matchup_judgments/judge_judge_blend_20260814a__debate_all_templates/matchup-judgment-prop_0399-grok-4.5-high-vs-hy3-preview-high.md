### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): A built a disciplined, consistent case around the resolution's built-in trade-off: finite public budgets, tryout-based exclusion even with subsidies, and the sheer scale of kids who never reach elite tiers. A's pressure questions (on budget zero-sum and evidence for the "role model" causal chain) exposed the weakest links in B's case, and A's answers, while modest on hard evidence, honestly acknowledged the closed-book limits rather than overclaiming. B countered with a creative "pyramid of excellence" and "breaking the paywall" framing that had real appeal, but it repeatedly denied the zero-sum premise embedded in the motion without fully accounting for coaching stipends, travel costs, and tournament fees that don't stay in the community, and it leaned on somewhat unsupported specificity (e.g., "most elite programs now mandate multi-sport participation," "physical capital," "structured periodization") that reads as rhetorical overreach rather than grounded argument. B's answers to A's pressure questions restated assertions ("hub" facilities, social-proof effects) without directly engaging the scarcity mechanism A kept pressing. A's closing crystallized the clash cleanly around scale and opportunity cost; B's closing largely repeated earlier framing. The debate was close and both sides argued competently, but A's tighter grounding and sharper handling of the decisive budget-tradeoff pressure gives it a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Q1 on finite budgets ("how does covering travel, trainers, and year-round elite schedules... not divert field time, coaches, and facility dollars from the far larger group") forced B into its weakest answer—claiming shared off-hours facility use—which never addressed the coaching, travel, and tournament costs that are the bulk of elite subsidies, leaving the zero-sum critique largely intact through the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by centering the motion’s actual priority choice: finite public resources should maximize participation, health, and inclusion across the much larger recreational population. Side B established a real equity benefit for talented low-income athletes, but never adequately answered the opportunity cost or scale comparison. Its claims that elite programs create shared infrastructure and reliably increase broad participation were largely asserted, introduced late, and insufficient to show that elite subsidies should receive priority.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest rebuttal was that subsidizing tryout-selected teams may reduce financial barriers without overcoming exclusions based on time, transportation, early selection, and limited roster space. This directly weakened Side B’s claim that elite subsidies constitute equity for the whole community.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A wins because it demonstrated that public budgets force an unavoidable trade-off and that subsidized elite travel teams still exclude most children—especially low-income late bloomers—through non-financial barriers, while broad recreational access directly fulfills the public mandate for inclusion and lifelong health. CON’s hub-and-role-model counter remained theoretically plausible but empirically thin, and it never fully neutralized PRO’s equity and scalability arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s second rebuttal and closing, which exposed that CON’s facility-sharing and role-model spillovers still leave working families and late bloomers on the outside, decisively restoring the burden to the majority’s access.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but A's was better grounded and executed the sharper rebuttal sequence. A's opening combined equity, developmental science (burnout/overuse injury), and cost-efficiency into a cohesive case tied directly to the motion's language. B offered a genuinely original reframe—that public subsidy is what keeps elite pathways from becoming pure wealth filters, and that "impact density" beats headcount—but this rested on largely unsupported valuations (one scholarship "multiplies impact" beyond many rec hours) asserted with more confidence than grounding. The pivotal exchange came in the Pressure Questions/Rebuttal 2 sequence: A's Q1 pressed whether subsidies actually solve the unpaid-travel-time barrier for working families, and B's answer effectively conceded that "the residual time cost is inherent to high-level competition itself," admitting the subsidy only partially closes the exclusion gap it was supposed to solve. A seized this concession in closing to argue B's equity premise was self-defeating. B's counters to A's "budget zero-sum" argument (calling it a "budgetary cartoon" and citing unspecified sponsorships) were assertive but thin compared to A's plausible, generally-accepted causal claims about overuse injury and burnout. A's closing did contain a real flaw—citing nonexistent reference tags ([C4.1, P3.1])—which is a genuine execution cost, but it does not overturn the substantive edge A built through sharper, better-grounded rebuttal work across the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's concession in Rebuttal 2 that "the residual time cost is inherent to high-level competition itself" [B4.1], answering A's pressure question about unpaid travel time, undercut B's central equity claim and let A close by arguing the opposing case had defeated its own premise.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): A won the public-priority comparison: broad access plausibly reaches more children per dollar and advances health, equity, and local cohesion, while B mostly asserted that a scholarship or community symbol outweighs those aggregate benefits. B's equity-at-the-top case was credible, but repeatedly treated prioritization as eliminating elite pathways and never established why intensive travel must receive priority over broadly accessible programs. A's unsupported “hundreds” language and erroneous closing references narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest response was that specialized clinics and school-based training can preserve talent development inside a broad-access system, undercutting B's claim that subsidized travel teams are the only alternative to wealth-based exclusion. B answered that clinics cannot replicate sustained elite pressure, but did not show why that difference outweighed the larger public reach.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by proving that subsidized elite travel teams fail as an equity tool because unpaid parental time and hidden logistics still exclude most working families, while embedded rec-based skill clinics offer a viable alternative that serves talent without cannibalizing finite public budgets. Side B’s impact-density frame could not overcome the proposition’s public-mandate logic after A exposed how narrowly elite subsidies actually reach disadvantaged youth.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where it answered B’s pressure questions by proposing municipal rec-based skill clinics as an alternative to cross-state travel teams, directly collapsing CON’s claim that elite travel subsidies are the only route to specialized coaching for working-class athletes while reinforcing the zero-sum budget tradeoff.
