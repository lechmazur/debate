### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that regulators can demand the same observable disclosures and remedies under ordinary conditional review, denying transactions for nondisclosure without imposing a rival-state status presumption. A offered strong asymmetry weighing and a compelling control-based rationale, but never established why that narrower tool was inadequate. A’s closing also mischaracterized B as favoring approval when documents are withheld, despite B explicitly supporting denial in that circumstance. B therefore preserved both its precision objection and its supply-cost argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.2 was decisive: B answered A’s hidden-intent pressure by explaining that review can rely on ownership, veto, offtake, financing, and concentration data, with nondisclosure itself warranting denial. This undercut A’s claim that only a presumptive block can place the evidentiary burden on the buyer.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A established a dominant strategic frame early on by pointing out that Side B's proposed alternative (imposing structural conditions on deals) was functionally identical to the rebuttals invited by Side A's presumption. Side B struggled to differentiate its stance, ultimately conceding that deals should be denied if buyers withhold structural disclosures—which Side A cleanly absorbed as proof that the burden must sit with the buyer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 "fork" completely trapped Side B, pointing out that if structural remedies are unacceptable (making the presumption a ban), then Side B's own behavior-based review collapses; if they are acceptable, then the presumption works.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): PRO won by exposing that CON's preferred remedies were functionally identical to what a rebuttable presumption invites, creating a damaging fork that CON never escaped: if the conditions are credible, the presumption is rebuttable; if they are not, CON's own behavior-based review collapses. PRO also turned CON's Indonesian nickel example into evidence of chokepoint expansion rather than diversification, and crystallized the debate around who should bear uncertainty when evidence is structurally unavailable before a crisis. CON never adequately explained how behavior-based review identifies concrete risk prospectively without relying on the same structural proxies PRO defends.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's fork in Rebuttal 1 (A2.1) showing CON's proposed board limits, offtake caps, and ring-fencing are exactly the instruments a rebuttable presumption would ask the buyer to bring, collapsing the supposed distinction between the two policies.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by reversing A’s central error-cost comparison. B plausibly showed that blocking scarce financing can leave mines unbuilt and prolong dependence on rival-controlled refining, while host-state licensing, physical jurisdiction, and transaction conditions make upstream ownership risk less irreversible than A claimed. A offered possible substitute investors but never established that they would reliably fund the marginal projects at issue. B sometimes treated the presumption too much like an outright ban, which kept the debate competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered A’s pressure by distinguishing auditable mitigation conditions from opaque beneficial ownership, then used A’s inability to identify a dependable substitute buyer to undermine the claim that delay is readily revisitable.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully reframing the debate around supply rather than ownership purity, effectively turning Side A's "asymmetry of error" against them. B proved that blocking rival capital prevents mines from being built at all, which is an irreversible harm that entrenches existing midstream dependence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 decisively capitalized on Side A's concession that if no substitute buyer appears, the project's 'risk-adjusted value is too low,' pointing out that this contradicts the entire premise of critical-minerals policy.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): B wins by dismantling A’s foundational asymmetry claim: a blocked mine is not “revisitable” when mining capital is scarce and development cycles are long, and A’s “market test” rigs the outcome by removing the deepest bidder and then citing the collapse as proof the deposit was worthless. B’s “hostage” reframe showed that upstream assets on home soil remain under the host state’s licensing and emergency powers, while the real chokepoint lies in midstream refining. Combined with the epistemic-vs-observational distinction on opacity, B demonstrated that targeted mitigation outperforms a blunt presumption.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4’s “hostage” reframe and the epistemic-vs-observational distinction, which turned A’s opacity argument against it and showed mitigation is more auditable than the presumption.
