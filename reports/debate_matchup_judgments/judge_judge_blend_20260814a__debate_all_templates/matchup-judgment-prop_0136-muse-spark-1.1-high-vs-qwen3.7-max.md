### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B built a coherent, mechanism-driven case (housing/childcare lower the 20-year cost curve; cash gets absorbed or arbitraged) and used it consistently across rounds. B's pressure question forced A to admit no East Asian cash bonus has ever produced a sustained rise, and A's answer conceded this outright, resting the cash case on an untested hypothetical. A's strongest reframe—marriage-liquidity as the real bottleneck—was neutralized when B pointed out a marriage deposit is itself a housing-affordability problem, and A's rebuttal (banks lending against a future guaranteed baby bonus) was speculative and unconvincing, weakening A's timing response. B's closing crystallized this by turning A's own $80-150k concession into proof that scaled cash is fiscally impossible while housing/childcare at least have partial real-world implementation (HDBs, hoikuen expansion). A's closing largely recapped without repairing the exposed timing/evidence gap. B answered pressure with clear A1/A2 labeling and substantive engagement; A's answers were formally compliant but substantively weaker, especially the bank-lending mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Q1 ("where has any one-off baby bonus produced a sustained rise, not just a timing bump?") followed by A's concession that no properly scaled bonus has ever been tried—this exposed that A's entire case rested on an unfalsifiable hypothetical, which B then leveraged in closing via the $80-150k admission.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won by successfully establishing that East Asia's fertility crisis is a supply constraint (lack of space and care) that a demand subsidy (cash) will merely inflate. Side A also effectively neutralized Side B's timeline argument by demonstrating that young adults project future structural costs when deciding whether to marry, exposing the implausibility of using a post-birth bonus to fund a pre-marriage deposit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A2 response explaining that the anticipation of career death and lack of space is precisely what deters marriage, brilliantly turning Side B's focus on lifelong childlessness into support for structural reform.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won by exposing a decisive timing contradiction in Side B's case. CON admitted that pre-marriage housing deposits were the core barrier to fertility, yet proposed baby bonuses arriving after birth, which cannot unlock the marriage decision. PRO turned this brilliantly by showing that the deposit barrier is itself a housing affordability problem. PRO further showed that in supply-constrained markets cash gets arbitraged into higher rents and scarce waitlists, whereas housing and childcare provision expands real capacity. B's late, speculative attempts to repair the timing mismatch—such as banks lending against future state transfers—lacked grounding and never neutralized the core clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal turn in [A2.5], where it showed that CON's marriage-deposit barrier is actually a housing affordability problem that a post-birth cash bonus cannot solve, decisively undermining CON's core causal chain.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A built a consistent, escalating empirical case (rural Japan/Korea, Japan's eliminated daycare waitlists, and especially Singapore's near-universal housing plus subsidized childcare paired with 0.9 fertility) that directly attacked the proposition's causal mechanism: structure without desire doesn't move births. Side B never fully neutralized this pattern. Its best move—forcing A to reconcile Singapore's existing cash bonuses with A's pro-cash claim (B3.2)—was answered credibly in A4.2 by distinguishing small, dribbled payments from the large lump-sum mechanism A had specified since the opening (A1.6), and by noting structural policy had already failed at maximal scale while large cash had not been tried. B's closing never circled back to rebut that distinction, effectively conceding a decisive pressure exchange. B's own defense increasingly relied on redefining "childcare" to include paid leave and full labor-market reform, which A exploited (A2.2, A4.4) as an admission that childcare alone doesn't work — undermining B's core claim. B's rebuttals were competent but reactive and didn't reclaim ground lost after Pressure Questions, while A adapted its Singapore/rural evidence with increasing precision and tied it to a clean per-dollar/per-year efficiency frame in the close. Both sides met formatting requirements for Q&A, but A's answers were sharper and more damaging to the opposing route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Singapore exchange: B's Q2 (B3.2) pressed A on why cash bonuses would succeed there when Singapore's existing baby bonus failed alongside its structural supports; A's answer (A4.2) — distinguishing size/delivery format and noting structural policy already failed at maximal scale — was the most decisive moment, since B never returned to rebut it in later speeches.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully grounding the debate in the empirical realities of East Asia, repeatedly weaponizing Singapore and Japan to prove that structural policies (public housing and daycare) have already failed to raise fertility. Side A failed to defend the feasibility of overhauling urban housing markets or respond to the failure of Singapore's maximal structural support, allowing B's "first-year cliff" framing to easily outcompete A's "18-year spreadsheet" argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal effectively used Side A's concession about rural areas to prove that cheap housing alone cannot overcome the unwillingness to sacrifice urban adult lifestyles, completely neutralizing A's primary gatekeeper argument.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): CON successfully showed that structural support has repeatedly failed to raise fertility where implemented most extensively—Singapore’s universal housing and subsidized childcare, Japan’s eliminated daycare waitlists, and cheap rural housing across the region—while PRO was forced to expand "childcare" into full labor-market reform to explain those failures. CON’s marginal-decision framing, which holds that couples respond to immediate fungible relief at the decision margin rather than long-run structural promises, provided a cleaner comparative mechanism and remained intact after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s first rebuttal and pressure questions turning PRO’s rural-housing concession into evidence that desire and lifestyle—not affordability—are the binding constraints, and exposing that PRO’s model must redefine childcare to include total work-culture reform in order to survive empirical counter-examples.
