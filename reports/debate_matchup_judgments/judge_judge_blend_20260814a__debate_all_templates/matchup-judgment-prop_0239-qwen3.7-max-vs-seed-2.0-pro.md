### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the motion centered on whether consent morally cleanses exploitation, not on whether bad transactions should always be banned. Side A had a coherent autonomy and anti-paternalism case, but leaned too heavily on redefining desperate consent as non-consent and then on policy consequences. Side B’s point that one can condemn the powerful exploiter without blaming or necessarily punishing the desperate party was the cleaner answer, and Side A’s concession that a wealthy party may exploit poverty so long as the desperate party consents made B’s sufficiency challenge much stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was answering that calling the kidney sale unethical stigmatizes the buyer, not the seller, and that no exact fairness formula is needed to know “one week’s rent for a permanent organ” is wrong. This directly undercut A’s demand for objective precision and its claim that B’s view only harms the vulnerable.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A wins by directly neutralizing Side B's primary intuition pump (the kidney exchange). While Side B relied heavily on the feeling of disgust a bystander would have, Side A effectively argued that this disgust is a privileged luxury. Side A's argument that stigmatizing the buyer practically removes the desperate seller's only lifeline proved to be a decisive, unanswered turn.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal brilliantly turned Side B's answer about stigmatizing the buyer, pointing out that driving the buyer away leaves the seller to starve with their kidney intact, thereby exposing the practical harm of Side B's moral framework.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B demonstrated that ordinary consent persists even under desperate conditions, and that recognizing a transaction as unethical assigns blame to the powerful rather than the victim. Side A’s attempt to redefine desperation as non-consent collapsed under scrutiny, and their answers to pressure questions conceded that wealthy parties may exploit the poor, leaving only the claim that subjective squeamishness invalidates survival choices. B’s separation of moral judgment from policy, and their crystallizing intuition that we would not ourselves make the exploitative offer, provided the cleaner comparative frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s first rebuttal (Turn 3), which exposed that Side A redefines consent to exclude virtually all hard choices and rightly separated the question of whether a transaction is unethical from whether it should be banned.
