### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides built sophisticated, well-structured cases and handled pressure questions cleanly with proper A1/A2 formatting. CON's strongest sustained point was that the motion's "likely to include minors" trigger is vague and that age-gating is easily evaded, hitting compliant creators while reckless actors escape via truthfulness rules just as easily as via gates. PRO's strongest and ultimately decisive point was structural: CON's preferred remedy (truthful disclosure, provider consent, anti-deception rules) explicitly permits fully truthful, disclosed persuasion of minors toward irreversible procedures, since CON conceded "truthful persuasion is not misconduct." PRO correctly identified this as leaving the core upstream harm—manufactured demand before any clinical gatekeeping—completely unaddressed by CON's regime, and CON's closing response ("that's not a concession minors deserve no protection") sidestepped rather than refuted this gap. CON's vagueness/enforceability critique remains a real and partially unanswered cost against PRO (PRO's threshold language does go beyond the bare motion text), but it does not fully neutralize PRO's more resolution-central claim that only PRO's mechanism actually targets the persuasive marketing itself, which is what the motion is about. That makes PRO's path to victory slightly more resilient after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing use of CON's "truthful persuasion is not misconduct" line ([B4.1]) to show CON's alternative permits fully disclosed marketing to persuade minors toward irreversible procedures was the most damaging, resolution-relevant exchange, and CON's response did not squarely repair it.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the core harm as upstream algorithmic exposure and normalization, rather than just deception or clinical access. Side A bit a difficult bullet by admitting their model allows truthful cosmetic surgery marketing directed at teens, which Side B effectively weaponized to show a massive gap in Side A's protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing powerfully crystallized the debate around Side A's concession that "truthful persuasion is not misconduct," proving Side A's alternative fails to address the root harm of manufacturing demand in minors.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the upstream/downstream clash live: marketing creates demand before clinical consent, so waiting for consultation is too late. CON's overbreadth and porosity critiques raised costs but didn't neutralize the workable read of "likely to include minors" as substantial known minor reach, nor the analogy to existing alcohol/gambling age-gating. CON's honest A1 that truthfulness does not block persuasion allowed PRO to frame the choice as allowing disclosed teen-targeted procedure ads versus reducing default passive amplification while preserving searchable risk info.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's perfection-rebuttal + pressure Q2 on evasion, and CON's A1 admission that truthful persuasion is not misconduct, which PRO turned into the decisive concession in closing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and stayed on the motion, but PRO did more to neutralize the decisive objection. CON's strongest weapon was the scope/overreach argument (age-gating sweeps in legitimate sponsored education) and the "leakage" argument (gating originals doesn't stop reposts/peer circulation). PRO answered scope directly in A4.1-A4.2 with a concrete functional test (payment + procedure-specific promotion + audience signals + safe harbor), which, while not airtight, gave a workable boundary that CON's closing did not seriously re-attack—B5's closing simply restates the leakage point rather than re-pressing the vagueness of the safe harbor. On leakage, PRO's response (A4.6, A5.3) that reducing deliberate algorithmic amplification is still meaningful harm reduction even if imperfect, and that "we don't need perfect insulation to reject deliberate amplification," is a reasonable rejoinder that CON never fully rebuts beyond repeating the original point. CON's clinic-as-backstop argument is repeatedly met with the "insecurity is cultivated before treatment; consent doesn't erase it" line, which lands well and CON's closing doesn't add a new angle to overcome it. PRO's closing crystallizes the "when must protection begin" framing effectively, while CON's closing is solid but largely reiterates rather than sharpens new ground. Pressure questions were used well by both sides, and both answered with A1/A2 format properly, but PRO's answers (functional test) did slightly more work resolving the vagueness CON raised than CON's answers did to resolve PRO's cultivation-timing pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.1-A4.2 answer to CON's Q1/Q2 pressure, offering a concrete functional test (payment + procedure promotion + audience signals + safe harbor) for age-gating, versus CON's B4 rebuttal that this test remains too vague and will still cause overcautious gating—this exchange most directly decided whether the "workable boundary" problem was resolved, and PRO's answer held up better through the closings.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully proving that clinical consent arrives too late to prevent the psychological harm of cultivated insecurity. Side B also effectively neutralized Side A's 'leakage' argument by framing it as a perfection fallacy, demonstrating that stopping deliberate algorithmic amplification is still worthwhile even if organic exposure remains.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2 effectively dismantled Side A's reliance on clinical consent by pointing out that a parent blocking a procedure does not undo years of algorithmic persuasion teaching a minor that normal features are defects.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won the core clash on scope and timing. It narrowed "influencer marketing" to paid procedure promotion with objective audience signals and offered a safe-harbor limiting principle, directly answering CON's overbreadth charge. It also showed why existing sponsorship labels and clinic consent are insufficient for pre-treatment harm: they don't convey medical risk and don't undo cultivated insecurity. CON (A) relied on leakage and existing law but never solved Q2 - what prevents repeated targeting before treatment - and its warning-as-furniture and repost arguments were turned by PRO's harm-reduction framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 2's A1/A2 – defining triggers (non-incidental under-18 analytics, youth targeting, known-minor exclusion) and functional commercial-purpose test – which collapsed A's central overbreadth/vagueness route and made the age-gate workable.
