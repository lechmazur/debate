### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Both sides ran disciplined, well-grounded cases, but PRO (Side B) held the stronger live route to victory after the full exchange. PRO's core security logic—that ownership confers physical control no contractual mitigation can replicate during a crisis, and that a presumption correctly shifts the burden before an asset changes hands rather than gambling on post-closing unwinding—was never squarely neutralized. CON's best live argument, the early-stage capital gap (specifically that sovereign wealth funds and export-credit agencies fund late-stage, de-risked projects, not the upfront exploration billions), was genuine and PRO's "de-risking attracts allied capital" response remained somewhat theoretical. However, PRO won the crisis-scenario framing: CON's domestic-law and mitigation protections don't explain how they prevent a rival-linked owner from covertly degrading output or following quiet compliance directives, which is PRO's specific threat model. CON's "an unbuilt mine is worse than a compromised mine" frame is structurally strong but rests on mitigation holding up exactly when it is most stressed. PRO's "liability disguised as liquidity" line crystallized the asymmetry memorably. PRO also handled CON's sharpest pressure question (A3.1, pointing out the presumption only changes outcomes for ambiguous cases PRO claims are exempt) with a coherent answer—those ambiguous cases are precisely where intelligence is fragmented and the burden-flip matters most. CON never fully closed the loop on why domestic jurisdiction protects a rival-controlled facility from internal sabotage rather than just external embargo, leaving PRO's principal security argument standing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to A2 (A4.3 exchange): CON's specific point that sovereign wealth funds and export-credit agencies invest late-stage rather than early-stage exploration was the sharpest factual pressure in the debate. PRO's counter—"capital needs to be de-risked, not invented; allied investors step in when the competitive field is no longer distorted by adversarial subsidies"—was plausible enough to keep the route alive, but CON's specificity about funding stages gave it a durable edge on this sub-clash. This exchange most clearly illustrated the core unresolved tension: PRO's de-risking theory is structurally sound but under-specified, while CON's capital-gap critique is concrete but depends on mitigations surviving geopolitical stress.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B won by making the motion narrower and more security-focused: the presumption targets firms closely tied to rival states and flips the burden before control transfers. B also weighed false negatives better, arguing that adversarial ownership creates crisis leverage that mitigation cannot reliably fix.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's answer to the mitigation case was decisive: board observers, offtake clauses, and other paper safeguards are much weaker than preventing rival-linked ownership of critical nodes before a crisis.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side B won by successfully dismantling Side A's proposed alternative (allied capital) and turning Side A's impact (adversarial reliance). ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Both sides argued competently and engaged each other directly throughout. CON's scarcity argument—that a presumptive block kills projects by eliminating major capital sources, leaving defense contractors without inputs on fixed schedules—was genuine and consistently pressed. PRO's "managed tightness" response never fully answered why allied industrial policy can arrive fast enough for fixed-schedule procurement. However, PRO won enough of the decisive exchanges to carry the debate. First, PRO's remote-sabotage pressure question (software, chemicals, proprietary parts) exposed a real gap in CON's mitigation framework: CON's tech-escrow and data-controls answer was plausible but assumed near-perfect implementation against an adversary actively designed to retain leverage. Second, PRO's "already restricting exports" point materially neutralized CON's escalation argument—if downstream coercion is ongoing regardless, blocking upstream ownership does not uniquely trigger it. Third, PRO's "ownership is subjugation, not interdependence" reframe (B4.4) was the debate's sharpest original move and was not squarely answered: CON's concession-model framing assumed the rival firm needs market-access returns enough to comply, but PRO correctly noted the rival state can accept foregone returns in exchange for a crisis chokehold. Fourth, PRO's asymmetric cost-of-failure framing held across the exchange: in a national-security context, an irreversible strategic loss outweighs manageable supply friction. CON kept the scarcity argument live and the margin close, but PRO's compounding advantages on the security side of the ledger, particularly on remote sabotage and the interdependence reframe, are the decisive factors.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question about remote sabotage (software, chemicals, irreplaceable parts) was the sharpest exchange. It directly exposed that host-country territorial jurisdiction stops physical extraction but does not prevent an adversary-linked owner from making the facility operationally dependent on inputs or systems the rival controls. CON's tech-escrow and diversified-supplier response was a reasonable attempt but did not fully close the gap, since it depends on regulators perfectly anticipating and mandating every operational dependency before the adversary embeds them.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B won by making the cleaner national-security asymmetry: adversarial ownership of critical-mineral chokepoints creates an irreversible crisis vulnerability, while blocked deals create manageable financing friction. Side A’s scarcity argument was credible and well-developed, but it overclaimed that presumptive blocking guarantees shortages and leaned heavily on mitigation tools that Side B effectively challenged as unreliable against state-directed firms, remote operational dependencies, and crisis-time coercion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s most decisive exchange was the attack on mitigation: host-country courts, contracts, and board structures cannot reliably prevent a rival state from using ownership, software, parts, or supply-chain dependencies as a kill switch during a crisis.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): CON won by successfully demonstrating that physical territorial jurisdiction and enforceable mitigation (like tech escrows and stockpiles) can neutralize the threat of remote sabotage or supply interruption, making the risk manageable. PRO's admission that their policy would cause 'managed short-term tightness' allowed CON to effectively argue that PRO trades a contained risk for a guaranteed physical shortage that directly undermines defense readiness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's pressure question about remote sabotage was decisive, specifically detailing how tech escrow, localized data controls, and diversified reagent stockpiles mechanically neutralize the threat of a rival state turning off extraction software or withholding chemicals.
