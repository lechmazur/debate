### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): PRO built and defended two durable comparative advantages. First, psychological commitment forms before the consultation: by the time a patient sits in the clinic chair, advertising has already framed her expectations—and CON's repeated counter that "ads generate inquiries, not deposits" is technically narrow and ignores PRO's actual mechanism (psychological, not only financial, entrenchment). CON never demonstrated that pre-visit optimism is harmless or that consultation-stage disclosure fully reverses it. Second, PRO's "imperfect statistics beat calculated silence" comparison was established early and never squarely defeated. CON argued that age-based rates would be misread, but never showed that misreading is worse than the current zero-data baseline, which allows a 40-year-old and a 28-year-old to receive identical aspirational imagery with no basis for self-differentiation. PRO's Q2 in the pressure round exposed a genuine internal tension in CON's position: you cannot simultaneously call age-based rates "false precision" and defend a regime that displays no quantitative data at all. CON's Rebuttal 2 answer—"zero data correctly routes patients to the exam room"—is a reframe but doesn't resolve that tension. CON's strongest live points—the misreading risk and gaming concern—were real, but PRO answered the gaming concern with standardized regulatory definitions, and the misreading risk was weighed against the current harm rather than defeating PRO's comparison. CON's alternative (consultation reform, cooling-off periods) is coherent but arrives after the mechanism PRO identifies has already operated. PRO maintained cleaner comparative framing across all rounds and the closing crystallized the key asymmetry rather than recycling earlier prose.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 in the Pressure Questions round was the most decisive exchange: "You claim age statistics create 'false precision,' yet defend ads that currently display zero quantitative data—which form of omission better serves informed decision-making?" This exposed a genuine contradiction in CON's position and forced CON's Rebuttal 2 into a defensive reframe ("zero data correctly routes to exam room") that never actually resolved the false-equivalence problem PRO identified—identical imagery for women of vastly different ages communicates no age-based differentiation at all, which PRO argued is worse than imperfect statistics.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate focused on the proposition’s core consumer-protection question: if ads sell reassurance and “control,” material age-based limits and uncertainty should appear at the same early touchpoint. Side A had a coherent objection that ads cannot individualize prognosis and that consultation reforms are better, but this often became a strawman of the motion as requiring a billboard-style personal medical chart. Side B persuasively answered that age is still a necessary baseline, uncertainty can be standardized, and downstream consent does not fully cure upstream expectation-setting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was against Side A’s “age statistics are reductive” claim: individual ovarian reserve and eggs banked matter, but they do not erase the need for a prominent age-based baseline, and omission lets identical aspirational ads create false equivalence between very different age groups.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): Side B won by successfully demonstrating that the harms of misleading advertising—specifically psychological commitment and chronological delays in conception—occur upstream of the consultation room. Side A's proposed downstream fixes (cooling-off periods) failed to address these irreversible upstream harms, and Side B effectively proved that providing the dominant predictive baseline (age) is vastly superior to the status quo's "calculated silence."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they contrasted CON's demand for statistical perfection against the status quo's "weaponized silence" and false equivalence, definitively winning the "partial data vs. zero data" clash.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): PRO wins on the strength of three arguments that survive the full exchange. First, the anchor-and-sunk-cost mechanism: PRO argues that advertising sets the psychological and financial anchor before any consultation occurs, and CON's response—that women update beliefs during mandatory consultations—understates cognitive bias and ignores real pre-consultation costs. CON never actually dismantles this; it just reasserts that women are capable agents, which is a strawman of PRO's claim. Second, the "selectively true" loophole: PRO's explanation that pooling ages is legally defensible yet functionally deceptive is sharp and specific, and CON's counter ("enforce existing law") never explains how current standards would catch technically accurate but misleading aggregate headlines. Third, a structural advantage PRO largely leaves unexploited but CON fails to notice: the motion explicitly requires disclosing uncertainty alongside age-based rates, which directly addresses CON's strongest objection that individual variation (AMH, ovarian reserve) makes age alone misleading. CON repeatedly argues that prominent age bands suppress individual complexity, but the motion's "uncertainty" component would express precisely that distribution. CON's paternalism and false-precision arguments are real concerns but never outweigh the core harm PRO identifies. PRO also handles its pressure questions more directly, framing the "selectively true" loophole cleanly in A2. CON's closing mostly recycles earlier points without sharpening the comparative choice, while PRO's closing effectively turns the "false precision" charge back on CON. Overall PRO builds the cleaner, more adaptive case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 in Rebuttal 2: explaining that existing law is reactive and legally porous because pooled age data is "selectively true"—technically accurate but functionally deceptive—is the sharpest argumentative move in the debate. It directly answers CON's pressure question about why new mandates are needed rather than enforcing current standards, and CON never provides a credible mechanism by which existing law would actually catch this specific deceptive practice.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B won by making the cleaner comparative case: age is the strongest and only pre-consultation variable most viewers know, so prominent age-based rates correct a specific advertising distortion before emotional, financial, and time investment occurs. Side A had a plausible concern about individual variation and false precision, but repeatedly overstated the mandate as requiring dominating actuarial clutter and leaned too heavily on consultations and existing law without adequately answering B's loophole point about selectively true pooled averages. B also framed autonomy more persuasively as requiring accurate initial anchors rather than later correction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that individual variation does not justify hiding the strongest known predictor, and that current false-ad rules miss legally true but functionally misleading pooled success rates, most directly undermined A's core defense.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B won by successfully proving that the status quo of "pooled" success rates is technically legal but functionally deceptive, neutralizing Side A's reliance on existing truth-in-advertising laws. Side B also provided a much more realistic mechanism for consumer behavior, explaining how advertising sets psychological anchors and sunk costs that a later consultation cannot simply erase.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's explanation in Rebuttal 2 that current advertising laws are porous because pooled averages are "selectively true" cleanly dismantled Side A's argument that existing regulations are sufficient.
