### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A (PRO) won the three most decisive clashes. First, the contractor/co-author dispute: PRO consistently showed that religious orders did not merely execute state blueprints—they recruited their own personnel, supplied theological justification, and ran daily operations under their own norms. CON's best answer was that withdrawal would have required defying compulsory attendance laws, but since the orders voluntarily entered and sustained participation for generations, this is thin. The "couldn't leave" story does not survive the obvious retort that they could have refused from the outset. Second, the institutional continuity argument: PRO's analogy to corporations, universities, and governments that carry obligations through complete membership turnover was never adequately rebutted. CON's distinction—that religious orders serve living beneficiaries rather than shareholders—describes purpose, not legal continuity, and does not defeat the liability principle. Third, the immunity problem: PRO's Q2 exposed that CON's asset-protection logic would insulate every wealthy institution from every historical accounting, a reductio CON never escaped. CON's A2 offered a limiting principle (causation + continuity + core mission) that sounded nearly identical to PRO's own standard, undermining the practical difference between the frameworks. CON's strongest ground—that asset diversion harms present-day beneficiaries and that the state is the primary obligor—was real, but PRO answered correctly: the state's liability does not extinguish a co-architect's obligation, and proportional calibration through negotiated or court-supervised settlements is how complex institutional liability already works. PRO's closing crystallized the stakes memorably ("permanent immunity by another name"), while CON's closing repeated earlier points without adding comparative pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second pressure question—asking which institution that caused mass harm and still holds assets could ever be required to pay under CON's framework—was the most decisive exchange. CON's answer in Rebuttal 2 proposed a limiting principle that mirrored PRO's own standard without acknowledging the concession, revealing that CON's framework either converged with PRO's or provided no workable immunity test at all. PRO then crystallized this as "an argument that makes accountability impossible has answered the wrong question," which effectively ended the theoretical dispute over whether the principle is coherent.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by demonstrating that religious orders were co-authors of the residential-school system, not mere state contractors, and by exposing that Side B's framework would grant permanent immunity to any institution with ongoing beneficiaries. A defused B's slippery-slope and asset-diversion concerns with clear limiting principles and proportionality standards, while B never squarely answered A's pressure about voluntariness or escaped the false dichotomy of state-versus-church responsibility. Because A explicitly endorsed state payment alongside institutional contribution, B's repeated claim that church liability lets the state escape was largely a strawman that weakened B's closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's dismantling of the contractor analogy in Rebuttal 1, showing that religious orders supplied independent moral architecture, recruited their own personnel, and voluntarily sustained the schools for generations. This exchange undermined the foundation of B's case and remained live through the close.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by successfully dismantling the contractor framing and establishing religious orders as co-architects with independent moral and operational agency. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B's structural critique—that the proposition binds only religious orders while the state that designed, funded, and supervised the policy faces no stated obligation—was the decisive clash, and it was never fully answered. PRO's central concession in A1 of Rebuttal 2, "nothing prevents the state from also paying its share," was explicitly identified by CON as revealing that aspiration is not architecture: once institutional assets flow in, political pressure for state payment collapses. CON's Q1 exposed this weakness cleanly, and PRO's answer deepened the problem rather than resolving it. PRO's underlying case—operational control plus retained assets creates independent obligation—is genuinely strong and was never fully dismantled; the motion's own language ("played central roles") substantially undercuts CON's "mere implementers" framing. But PRO's short, underutilized closing (122 of 220 words) left CON's strongest rebuttal about the political escape valve standing without adequate response, and PRO never sharply distinguished "contributing alongside the state" from "substituting for the state," which was the live burden. CON also deployed the more memorable framing throughout, notably the "aspiration is not architecture" line and the "accounting misdirection" close. The result is a competitive but real edge for CON on execution and crystallization of the key structural problem, even though PRO's moral core remained intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's exploitation of PRO's A1 response in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing was the most decisive exchange. PRO answered B's Q1 ("what prevents the state from treating institutional contributions as its own discharge?") by saying "nothing prevents the state from also paying its share." CON immediately identified this as a structural concession—"aspiration is not architecture"—and used it to anchor the closing argument that the proposition creates a political escape valve for the state.
