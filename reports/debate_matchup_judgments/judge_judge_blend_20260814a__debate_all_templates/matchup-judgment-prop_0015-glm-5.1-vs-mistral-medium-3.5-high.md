### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) built and maintained the stronger path to victory across the full debate. The decisive structural flaw in PRO's case was its self-defeating admission in Rebuttal 1: claiming that Arkansas Medicaid recipients transitioned to employer coverage while simultaneously conceding that bureaucracies "don't track every success." CON exploited this contradiction from Rebuttal 1 onward with the framing that PRO was conflating reduced enrollment with reduced dependency—"vanishing from a database isn't escaping need, it's becoming invisible." PRO never resolved this; in Rebuttal 2, PRO's A1 answer gestured toward "separate datasets" and "documented" employment rises without addressing the specific tracking gap it had already conceded. On exemptions, PRO repeatedly asserted they "function effectively in practice" with "objective criteria" while also conceding eligible individuals lose benefits, which it dismissed as "temporary paperwork delays." CON's answer—that weeks without diabetes medication or food is real harm, not friction, and that the gap between eligible-on-paper and protected-in-practice is the central mechanism by which vulnerable people fall through—was never squarely neutralized. CON's pressure questions were sharper, directly targeting the two live vulnerabilities. PRO's questions attempted a burden flip but CON answered them directly with the measurable data point (rising uninsured rates, increased ER use) and forced PRO to defend the tracking concession. PRO's closing was notably brief and largely repetitive, conceding that exemption failures are "implementation issues to fix"—a significant concession that strengthened CON's weighing frame. CON closed with the cleaner comparative: measurable harm versus unproven benefit. The TANF deep poverty argument (dependency shifted to shelters and emergency rooms) was raised early and never adequately answered by PRO, who only noted employment among single mothers rose without addressing the rising deep-poverty data. CON's epistemic discipline—using the Arkansas case with appropriate uncertainty while pointing to measurable indicators—was stronger than PRO's confident assertions of documented transitions it then admitted weren't tracked.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal exchange was CON's Rebuttal 1 response to PRO's Arkansas claim. When PRO asserted thousands transitioned to employer coverage but admitted bureaucracies don't track every success, CON identified this as the "accounting fraud at the heart of this debate"—you cannot claim documented transitions while relying on undocumented assumptions. This contradiction remained live for the rest of the debate. PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer pointed to "separate datasets" and rising employment but never resolved the specific logical flaw: the transition claim was unsupported, and the measurable data (rising uninsured rates, increased ER use) pointed in the opposite direction. CON's formulation "conflating invisibility with independence" crystallized the stakes and PRO had no clean answer.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built a more durable comparative case across the full debate. Three things decided it. First, PRO's "fixable vs. corrosive" framing held up under pressure: implementation failures like missed mailings can be remediated; the mental-health decline, skill erosion, and intergenerational poverty produced by indefinite dependency cannot be administratively waived away. CON never substantively engaged this damage argument—calling the benefits "speculative" without disputing the causal mechanism. Second, PRO's B4.3 move—that paperwork burdens endemic to all safety-net programs (SNAP recertification, Medicaid renewals, housing applications) preexist work requirements—directly cut the heart out of CON's narrative that requirements specifically conjure new vulnerabilities. CON's closing gesture ("friction is the feature") asserted but did not answer this structural point. Third, PRO's pressure questions were sharper: Q1 forced CON to reveal whether its objection was to any work expectation or just implementation, and Q2 exposed the expanding definition of "genuinely cannot comply." CON's answers (even with navigators it still fails, pointing to the 18,000 figure) were responsive but never resolved the definitional problem PRO identified. CON's strongest sustained contribution—the Arkansas example and "mechanism of exclusion" framing—was real and vivid, but PRO consistently reframed it as a solvable administration problem rather than a policy indictment. CON's most lasting point, that states implementing cynically won't invest in the fixes PRO proposes, was answered plausibly by federal incentives and accountability, even if imperfectly. The net result: PRO answered the live comparison (which harm is larger and which is fixable) more cleanly throughout, while CON's case grew increasingly reliant on implementation pessimism without defeating PRO's core normative and intergenerational argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B4.3 rebuttal—that the paperwork burdens CON identifies (stable housing, internet, literacy) are endemic to every safety-net program and preexist work requirements entirely—was the most decisive exchange. It shifted the frame from "work requirements create new targeting of the vulnerable" to "work requirements extend the same bureaucratic structure already governing recipients' lives, while adding an employment expectation for able adults." CON never directly answered this, which left its core narrative—that the mechanism of harm is unique to work requirements—substantially undermined.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A won because it made the proposition hinge on actual compliance failures rather than idealized exemptions, and used the Arkansas example to show concrete harm without corresponding proof of employment gains. Side B had a coherent fixable-friction versus corrosive-dependency frame, but relied too heavily on asserted benefits from work norms and never sufficiently proved that work requirements actually reduce dependency more than they remove aid from vulnerable people. A’s comparison of concrete, immediate coverage and food-access harms against speculative dependency reduction was cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was answering B’s “paperwork is just a glitch” argument by reframing inability to navigate reporting, internet, mail, homelessness, or mental illness as precisely an inability to comply under the policy, not an unrelated administrative inconvenience.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully isolating the core comparison: fixable administrative friction versus the structural, corrosive harm of long-term dependency. A’s distinction between policy logic and implementation flaws, capped by the strong point that bureaucratic burdens preexist work requirements across the safety net, neutralized B’s primary mechanism of harm. B argued compellingly that real-world vulnerability makes compliance impossible, but repeatedly conflated paperwork struggles with genuine inability to work and failed to adequately weigh against A’s case on dependency’s intergenerational and psychological damage. A’s comparative weighing was cleaner and more responsive to the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 ([A4.3]) effectively dismantled B’s pressure by noting that paperwork and stability demands already govern SNAP/Medicaid renewals, meaning work requirements don’t create a new category of vulnerability. This forced B into a defensive posture and crystallized A’s “fixable friction vs. corrosive dependency” weighing mechanism.
