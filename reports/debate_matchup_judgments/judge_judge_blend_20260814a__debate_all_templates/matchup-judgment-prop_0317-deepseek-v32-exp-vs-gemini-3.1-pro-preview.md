### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): CON built the stronger cumulative case across the exchange. Three compounding weaknesses hurt PRO. First, PRO's opening used only 216 of 340 words, leaving the core argument underdeveloped while CON packed in multiple parallel lines (legal definition, parental controls, proportionality, analogies). Second, and most damaging, PRO's Rebuttal 1 attempt to distinguish loot boxes from physical trading cards by noting cards have a secondary market directly backfired: it conceded that physical cards are actually closer to the legal definition of gambling than loot boxes, and CON exploited this precisely in [B2.1], creating an internal inconsistency that persisted through closing. Third, CON's "statutory fantasy" argument—that no legal framework for "children's gambling" exists, so classifying loot boxes as gambling necessarily triggers mandatory adult-only age gates rather than mere spending limits—was consistently pressed across Rebuttal 1, Pressure Questions, Rebuttal 2, and Closing, and PRO never adequately answered it. PRO's Rebuttal 2 response ([A4.3]) simply asserted that gambling regulation doesn't mean total ban and compared it to alcohol/tobacco, but never explained what the actual regulatory mechanism would look like or pointed to any jurisdiction applying gambling-style protections to minors without an outright ban. PRO's emotional framing ("slot machine in the bedroom") and moral argument about predatory design were rhetorically effective and gave PRO a real live route, but CON's proportionality and legal-definition arguments were more consistently grounded and not squarely neutralized. The margin is clear but competitive: PRO's core policy harm argument has genuine force and CON's "total ban" claim was somewhat overstated, but PRO's execution costs and the baseball card backfire prevented a closer result.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [B2.1] turn against PRO's baseball card rebuttal was the most decisive exchange. PRO had tried to distinguish loot boxes by noting physical trading cards can be traded or sold for real money. CON correctly flipped this: if secondary market value is what matters, PRO just admitted physical cards are closer to true gambling than loot boxes, which are account-locked and have zero real-world financial value. This simultaneously defended CON's analogy, damaged PRO's framing of loot boxes as uniquely harmful, and reinforced the legal definition argument in one move. The inconsistency it exposed persisted without resolution through closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): Side B won by making the better comparative case that the paid random reward mechanism and child vulnerability justify gambling-style safeguards despite the lack of cash-out value. Side A had a strong legal-definition and overreach argument, but leaned too heavily on asserted total-ban consequences and an overly simple parental-controls solution. Side B more persuasively framed those as loopholes and burden-shifting rather than adequate protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's attack on parental controls as reactive burden-shifting, paired with the claim that account-locked rewards are an evasion rather than proof of safety, was the most decisive exchange.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): CON wins by exposing a fatal statutory tension in PRO's case: classifying loot boxes as gambling under existing frameworks would trigger mandatory 18+/21+ age gates and casino-level licensing, effectively banning minors from mainstream games—an outcome PRO refuses to defend. While PRO effectively establishes psychological harm and predatory design, they never squarely answer CON's reductio on trading cards and claw machines, nor do they articulate a coherent legal mechanism for "regulated gambling" by children. CON's alternative of platform-level parental controls, while contestable, provides a proportionate solution that avoids this legal sledgehammer, and CON's turn on the baseball-card analogy severely damages PRO's definitional case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal turn (A2.1) showing that physical trading cards—PRO's own analogy—are actually closer to traditional gambling because they possess transferable real-world value, whereas locked digital loot-box items strictly lack it, thereby undermining PRO's attempt to claim loot boxes are uniquely dangerous.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B wins by decisively exposing a fatal practical flaw in Side A's regulatory mechanism. While A built a compelling, rhetorically polished case around psychological harm and minor vulnerability, A repeatedly claimed that classifying loot boxes as gambling would simply add "safeguards" like spending limits for minors. B correctly identified that gambling frameworks are inherently exclusionary and legally mandate strict 18+/21+ age gates, meaning A's proposal would functionally ban minors from these games rather than protect them within them. B's "statutory fantasy" framing, combined with a workable alternative (platform-level parental controls) and effective turns of A's analogies, gave B a cleaner, more legally grounded path to victory. A's moral urgency was strong, but could not overcome the practical incoherence of applying adult casino laws to children's entertainment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 dismantling of A's regulatory proposal. B directly answered the pressure questions and delivered the decisive blow: "There is no statutory framework for 'children's gambling.' Under actual state and federal law, the moment a product is legally classified as gambling, it triggers mandatory 18+ or 21+ age gates... You cannot have a gambling product legally accessible to a twelve-year-old with a 'spending limit.'" This squarely neutralized A's core solution, forced A onto defensive ground, and reframed the entire debate around legal reality versus aspirational harm prevention.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON)): Side A built and maintained the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Its core structural argument—that variable-ratio reinforcement tied to real-money wagering for near-worthless items constitutes functional gambling regardless of the "always win something" technicality—held up under pressure and was never squarely neutralized. Crucially, Side A's A4.2 turn was the most decisive moment in the debate: by pointing out that enforcing a minor-specific spending cap requires the same age-verification infrastructure as a gambling age-gate, it converted Side B's own proposed remedy into evidence for PRO's position. Side B conceded throughout that loot boxes are exploitative and require regulatory intervention, which effectively ceded the normative heart of the motion and left them arguing only about remedy design. Side B's strongest argument—that gambling law technically requires the possibility of receiving nothing of value—was real but undercut by the motion's normative framing ("should be regulated as"), which asks what protection minors deserve, not whether loot boxes already satisfy current statutory definitions. Side B's compliance nightmare argument, its best practical objection, was cleanly neutralized by A4.2. Side B never resolved the core tension: if spending caps require age verification and platform-level enforcement, then the claimed compliance gap between its proposal and Side A's disappears. Side A's rhetoric ("casino in the bedroom," "budgeting a child's slot machine") was also more memorable and substantively grounded than Side B's more abstract framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.2 response to Side B's Q2 was the most decisive exchange. Side A demonstrated that enforcing a minor-specific spending cap inherently requires the same age-verification infrastructure as a gambling age-gate, directly turning Side B's "single point of control" argument against it. This neutralized the compliance nightmare objection while simultaneously showing that Side B's preferred remedy is simply a weaker version of the same enforcement mechanism—one that caps harm rather than preventing behavioral conditioning altogether.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): B won by making the central harm clearer: randomized paid rewards aimed at minors combine financial wagering with casino-style reinforcement, so ordinary disclosure or budgeting does not answer the addiction/conditioning concern. A had a plausible alternative-regulation case and a useful implementation distinction, but over-relied on a narrow “nothing of value” definition and framed harm mainly as bankruptcy, which B effectively exposed as too limited for minors and addiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was against A’s spending-cap alternative: B argued that a cap merely budgets a child’s slot-machine-like use while leaving variable-ratio conditioning intact, and also turned A’s compliance objection by noting that minor-specific caps still require age verification.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) won because it demonstrated that the gambling label is a legal misfit for loot boxes—they always confer an item, so stretching the definition to subjective disappointment creates unworkable overbreadth—and showed that platform-level spending caps and transparency directly prevent the financial exploitation of minors without the collateral damage and enforcement chaos of reclassification. Side B’s psychological equivalence argument was potent and its dismantling of the trading-card analogy was damaging, but it never fully reconciled the core definitional mismatch or proved that gambling law was superior to CON’s targeted, workable alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 answer (A2) distinguishing a single-point app-store payment control from the vastly broader compliance burden of age-verifying and reclassifying internal game mechanics across thousands of titles; this defused Side B’s enforcement-symmetry argument and preserved the practical superiority of CON’s remedy.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing the spending cap alternative as both practically contradictory and psychologically inadequate. A sharply exposed that CON’s proposed caps require the exact same age-verification infrastructure CON dismissed as a “compliance nightmare,” neutralizing CON’s pragmatism argument. Furthermore, A’s rebuttal that “addiction is a neurological hijacking, not an accounting error” decisively dismantled B’s claim that capping spending contains behavioral conditioning. While B made a coherent legal distinction about always receiving an item, A effectively framed this as a corporate loophole that ignores the functional reality of variable-ratio reinforcement on minors. A’s rhetoric was sharper, their answers to pressure were more damaging, and they maintained a clearer, more compelling path to victory throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) directly answering the pressure questions. A turned B’s compliance argument against them by noting that enforcing spending caps inherently requires the same age-verification systems B claimed were too burdensome, while simultaneously reframing the spending cap as “budgeting a child’s slot machine usage,” which crystallized the psychological harm clash and left B with no clean defensive route.
