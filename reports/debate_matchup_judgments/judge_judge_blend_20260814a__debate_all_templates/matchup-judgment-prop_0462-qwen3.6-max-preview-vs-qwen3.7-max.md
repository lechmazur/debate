### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Both sides ran competent, structurally coherent cases, making this close. PRO's strongest live argument—that tracking sorts by early advantage rather than ability, structurally locking out the majority of disadvantaged students—was genuinely powerful and only partially answered by CON's "better identification" pivot, which partly conceded the problem while stepping outside the debate's framing. However, CON's core mechanical argument (math is strictly cumulative; prerequisite gaps cannot be bridged by simultaneous grade-level exposure) was consistently deployed from opening through closing and was never adequately neutralized. PRO asserted that tutoring would be "proactive and integrated," but never explained mechanically how a student with multi-year prerequisite deficits accesses grade-level algebra without first filling those gaps—meaning the heterogeneous lesson still exposes the gap before the tutor can address it, which is exactly CON's "structurally reactive" critique. CON's pressure question Q2—asking what mechanism actually lets advanced students access content beyond grade level in a heterogeneous room—exposed a real hole, and PRO's A2 ("depth and enrichment, peer discourse") deflected rather than answered: it describes enriched grade-level work, not acceleration. CON's staffing arithmetic critique (intensive tutoring requires dramatically lower student-staff ratios than standard classes; reallocation of parallel-track FTEs doesn't bridge that gap) was repeatedly made and PRO's reallocation story was plausible in outline but never resolved the ratio math. The sustainability concern—tutoring collapses when budgets tighten, leaving a slowed core with no safety net—was a durable threat PRO never neutralized with a concrete institutional mechanism. PRO's equity framing is rhetorically stronger and CON's "lower tracks as prerequisite bridges with flexible promotion" (A2) is empirically optimistic given how static such placements tend to be in practice. Still, CON's practical objections cut to the viability of the proposed model itself: if the tutoring is structurally reactive and fiscally unsustainable, PRO's equity goal is also unfulfilled. CON's mechanical case slightly outweighed PRO's equity case after the full exchange, giving CON a razor-thin but real edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question Q2—asking what concrete mechanism allows a math-ready student to access content beyond grade level in a heterogeneous classroom—exposed the clearest gap in PRO's model. PRO's A2 response ("depth and enrichment, peer-led mathematical discourse") described enriched participation in grade-level content rather than genuine acceleration, confirming CON's recurring claim that the heterogeneous model structurally caps high achievers. This exchange left the most important live mechanical tension in PRO's proposal unresolved.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): B wins by making the tutoring and pacing burden decisive. A built a plausible equity case against biased tracking, but never fully answered B's core objection that math readiness gaps make one grade-level classroom either slowed for advanced students or inaccessible for struggling students. A's staffing answer, that existing tracking resources can be redeployed into high-dosage tutoring, was asserted more than demonstrated and was vulnerable to B's ratio comparison. B also blunted A's equity route by arguing for better identification and flexible advancement rather than abolition of public acceleration.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was the response to A's tutoring model: tracking uses ordinary class ratios, while intensive tutoring requires small groups, so eliminating tracks risks leaving only a slowed heterogeneous class once tutoring proves unsustainable.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side B won by successfully attacking the mechanical viability of Side A's model. Side B persuasively argued that the cumulative nature of math makes heterogeneous pacing impossible without causing confusion, and exposed the mathematical flaw in Side A's staffing reallocation (standard class ratios cannot cleanly convert to high-dosage small-group tutoring without ballooning budgets). Side A's defense of "depth and enrichment" essentially conceded that the core class could not provide acceleration, cementing Side B's claim that the model caps achievement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 effectively dismantled Side A's resource reallocation argument by pointing out the simple arithmetic of staffing ratios: tracking uses standard class ratios, while intensive tutoring requires small groups, meaning a 1:1 swap of FTEs is impossible without new funding.
