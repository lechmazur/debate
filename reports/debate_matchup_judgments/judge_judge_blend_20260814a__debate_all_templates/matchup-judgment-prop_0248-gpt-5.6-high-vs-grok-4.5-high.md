### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and executed the pressure-question format correctly with A1:/A2: labels. CON's liberty/consent and administrability arguments were forceful and consistent, but its answers to PRO's pressure questions ultimately reasserted rather than resolved the core tensions: on taxation, CON never explained why general progressive taxes settle the specific reciprocal duty to one's own parent rather than merely shifting cost onto unrelated low-income taxpayers; on borderline cases, CON's fallback was to openly embrace "blanket immunity" even for affluent children of loving, non-abusive parents, which PRO then exploited decisively in rebuttal and closing as the debate's starkest injustice. PRO, by contrast, met CON's consent objection with a solid non-consent-duty analogy (taxes, support obligations), and answered CON's vagueness pressure with a concrete structural fix (protected baseline for housing, dependents, disability, retirement; only disposable capacity assessed; a substantial-misconduct defense), which blunted much of CON's administrability attack even if it didn't fully eliminate it. PRO's case did suffer an execution cost from having its opening clipped for length, and CON's litigation/family-history concerns remained a live, unresolved practical worry. But on balance PRO more successfully neutralized the opponent's sharpest pressure and forced a costly concession, giving it the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1/Q2 pressure questions forcing CON to explicitly bite the bullet on "blanket immunity even for affluent children of adequate parents," which CON's answers confirmed rather than escaped, and which PRO then weaponized in its final rebuttal and closing as the decisive unfairness.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively turning Side B's proposed safeguards into Side A's primary harms, arguing powerfully that means-testing and misconduct exceptions inherently require the invasive state scrutiny into private finances and childhood histories that Side A warned against. Side A also successfully framed progressive taxation as a systemic solution to the free-rider problem that avoids micro-level family litigation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing argument that "PRO's safeguards are not limits on that intrusion; they *are* the intrusion" was the most devastating and decisive conceptual move in the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): PRO wins by sustaining a clearer comparative burden: under CON’s rule, a low-income public must subsidize care for wealthy parents while their raised, affluent children preserve discretionary wealth. CON never squarely answers why general taxation should fully replace a bounded, means-tested familial duty when reciprocity is present and the child has disposable means. CON’s administrative and liberty objections are real but largely repeat the same coercion frame without addressing the specific unfairness PRO identifies. PRO also more effectively handles pressure: A’s Q1 exposes a distributive gap in CON’s reasoning, and CON’s answer in B4.1 merely asserts that progressive taxation already extracts from the affluent without explaining why the specific child should be exempt from contributing to their own parent’s care before strangers are taxed. The misconduct exception is indeed vague, but PRO successfully narrows the issue to substantial repudiation and shows that hard cases warrant safeguards rather than blanket immunity. A’s clipped opening is a minor execution cost, but the core case remains intelligible through later speeches, and PRO’s closing crystallizes the decisive asymmetry: CON must defend letting capable children externalize essential parental care onto the public.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A3.1 (Q1) and A2.2: PRO’s challenge that progressive taxation forces low-income strangers to subsidize wealthy children’s parents, which CON never adequately resolves.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly, but A's equal-citizenship/equal-elder-protection point proved the more durable line of attack. A repeatedly forced B to confront the "genetic luck" problem: two identical taxpayers face radically different liability, and two identically needy elders get different security, purely based on whether a solvent child exists. B's calibration answer (residual capacity after securing the child's own dependents) actually concedes A's dilemma-either the duty is capped low enough to be trivial for solvency purposes, or high enough to threaten savings/family security-and B never resolved that tension in Rebuttal 2 or Closing. A's causation-based rebuttal to the "status/benefit" analogy (minority support flows from adults creating dependency, not from unchosen benefit) also directly undercut B's central reciprocity analogy without B offering a comparably sharp reply beyond restating the analogy. B's free-riding framing assumes childhood is a "private investment/debt" that must be repaid, but never independently established that premise beyond assertion; A explicitly named this as the load-bearing but unproven assumption. On pressure questions, A's ceiling/equal-citizenship questions landed harder and exposed a real unresolved tension, while B's questions were answered adequately by A (causation vs. benefit, and public care remains available regardless). B's closing was also clipped for length, a real execution cost, whereas A delivered a clean, complete closing that crystallized the equal-citizenship clash effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1 in Pressure Questions ("what principled ceiling prevents this claim from consuming retirement savings") followed by B's calibration answer, which conceded the dilemma A had set up: either the duty is bounded and thus too weak to solve the stated solvency problem, or unbounded and threatens family security—B never escaped this fork.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a solid victory by dismantling Side B's core mechanism of "reciprocal debt." Side A effectively distinguished child support (based on causation/creating dependency) from elder care, and demonstrated that Side B's reliance on biological status fails to measure the actual "prior benefit" it claims to tax.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal comprehensively neutralized Side B's pressure questions by proving that minority support stems from causation rather than unchosen benefit, and showing that biology is an arbitrary proxy for "prior benefit" since a devoted stepchild would owe nothing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that Side A's reciprocity principle collapses into bare genealogy: a devoted stepchild may owe nothing while a biological child who received negligible care still faces liability, proving the duty tracks bloodline rather than actual benefit. B also crystallized a calibration dilemma (weak duties fail solvency; strong ones sacrifice equal citizenship and family security) and an equal-citizenship objection that A never adequately answered beyond restating the prior-benefit premise. B cleanly dissected A's analogies to inheritance and minority support, showing they rest on causation or post-death asset transfer, not reverse living dependency. A's closing was clipped for exceeding the word limit, leaving its crystallization incomplete, while B delivered a tight closing that summarized the unrebutted core objections. Across the exchange, B made the decisive clash clearer and left A with unresolved structural tensions in its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal (B4.4–B4.6), especially the stepchild/biological-child distinction and the calibration dilemma: if the duty is capped to protect the child's savings it cannot deliver promised solvency, but if it is strong enough to shift costs it threatens equal citizenship and family security—while childless elders still require the public system anyway.
