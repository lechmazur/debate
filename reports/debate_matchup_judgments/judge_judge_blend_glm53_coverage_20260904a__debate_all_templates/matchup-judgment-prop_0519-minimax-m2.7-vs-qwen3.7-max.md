### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B wins narrowly. A made a strong bootstrapping and coverage challenge, arguing that sectoral bargaining needs institutions fragmented industries lack. But B kept the stronger comparative claim: minimum-wage hikes only adjust an hourly number and can be offset through hours, schedules, and benefits, while sectoral mechanisms can set broader industry standards. B's state-extension and wage-board answers were imperfect but enough to blunt A's claim that sectoral bargaining necessarily leaves workers uncovered. A never supplied a comparable mechanism for protecting overall living standards, and repeated clipping slightly hurt execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was the key exchange: it answered A's institutional-capacity pressure with state-convened sectoral boards and reframed the political-capital issue as getting a more comprehensive return than a mere wage hike.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): Side B won by successfully exposing a fatal contradiction in Side A's case: Side A relied on state intervention (statutory extension and state-convened boards) to solve sectoral bargaining's coverage gaps, requiring the exact same political capital that Side A claimed made minimum-wage hikes impossible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal decisively neutralized Side A's coverage solution by pointing out that statutory extension is a political act requiring the same legislative majority as a wage hike, turning Side A's "gridlock" argument against them.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON won by sustaining a decisive coverage advantage: in fragmented service industries, sectoral bargaining cannot reach millions of workers at small franchisees, non-union shops, and peripheral contractors, whereas statutory minimum wages cover everyone. PRO's attempts to close this gap—statutory extension and state-convened wage boards—were either shown to require the same political capital as minimum-wage legislation or reframed by CON as state wage-setting rather than genuine sectoral bargaining. PRO's comprehensiveness and evasion arguments remained live but could not overcome the unresolved bootstrapping and coverage burden that CON pressed from opening to close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal demonstrating that PRO's New York Wage Board example constitutes state-set wage determination rather than genuine sectoral bargaining between organized social partners, and that scarce enforcement resources are more efficiently deployed through a universal statutory floor than through patchwork sectoral agreements that fragmented industries lack the capacity to monitor.
