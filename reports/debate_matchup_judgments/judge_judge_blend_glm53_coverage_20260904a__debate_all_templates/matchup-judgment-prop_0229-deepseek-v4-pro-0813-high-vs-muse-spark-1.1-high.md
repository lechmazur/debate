### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B more effectively exploited the debate's central tension: whether a uniform five-year phase-out can work across radically different state capacities. B's differentiation between Gulf rentier economies and fragile North African/failed states, backed by concrete plausible examples (Takaful and Karama's decade-long shortfall, Libya/Sudan/Yemen's absent registries), forced A into a concession in Rebuttal 2/Closing that price liberalization should be delayed "where transfers already reach poor households" — an admission that undercuts the proposition's own five-year universal timeline, which B explicitly flagged and A never fully repaired. B also sustained an unanswered credibility critique (visible price hikes now vs. unverifiable future cash) through to the closing. A's regressivity and fiscal-cost arguments were solid and never truly refuted on their own terms, and A's answer that cash lets fares/prices adjust while riders are compensated was a reasonable economic rebuttal, but it didn't resolve the capacity/sequencing contradiction B kept pressing. A's Rebuttal 1 was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost. On balance B did more to sharpen and control the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point [B4.6] — that A's "delay price hikes until transfers arrive" sequencing directly contradicts A's own five-year universal phase-out promise — was the most damaging unaddressed hit, exposing an internal inconsistency A's closing never resolved.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully proving that Side A's proposed mechanism (targeted cash in 5 years) would fail in the specified regions due to low state capacity and informal economies. Side B effectively weaponized the indirect costs of fuel (transport/food) and convincingly argued that cash registries in fragile states would result in "perfected gatekeeping," while also maintaining a strong, unrefuted point about Gulf economies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, pointing out that Side A's fallback position (only raise prices where transfers arrive) violates the 5-year phase-out mandate and demonstrating that cash registries in fragile states are more vulnerable to militia capture than a universal pump price.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by making the decisive clash clearer: once both sides accepted second-round harm through fares, food, and generators, PRO still needed cash transfers to protect that productive circuit under weak capacity within five years, and never fully did. CON’s budget-share vs liters reframing, Gulf feedstock split, and weak-state “no-application universality” answers kept more live routes open; PRO’s incidence and sequencing case stayed strong but was undercut by the clipped R1, capacity gaps, and tension between universal five-year phase-out and “raise prices only where transfers already work.”
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s A2.1–A2.3 / A4.1 line: conceding indirect harm does not rescue PRO, because household cash cannot keep microbuses, bakeries, and pumps viable when diesel rises systemically and delivery is imperfect.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases and engaged the pressure questions with correctly formatted A1/A2 answers. PRO's throughline was cleaner and more consistently defended: universal subsidies are a regressive, fiscally unstable status quo that must eventually be cut anyway, so a self-financing, announced five-year glide (raise price a little, recycle the disproportionate savings from heavy consumers into transfers first) converts an inevitable shock into a managed one. CON landed real hits — the capacity gap between Gulf and North African states, the risk to unregistered/near-poor households during the transition window, and especially the structural point that in diesel-dependent import economies fuel use isn't concentrated among the wealthy, undermining PRO's self-financing mechanism — but PRO's closing never squarely answered that diesel-dependency critique, while CON's own alternative ("conditional speed," possibly longer than five years) remained vague and was effectively neutralized by PRO's "deferred collapse" reframe. CON's opening was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, costing it part of its political-durability argument before the exchange even began. On balance PRO's mechanism and framing proved slightly more resilient across the exchange, though CON kept the debate genuinely close by exposing real unresolved tension around transition-period harm to informal and near-poor households.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that PRO's "self-financing" mechanism assumes fuel consumption is concentrated among wealthy motorists, which breaks down in diesel-dependent economies where fuel powers minibuses, irrigation, and freight — a structural challenge to PRO's core financing story that PRO's closing did not directly rebut.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully focusing on the vulnerabilities of the 5-year deadline for weak states. Side B's explanation of how state arrears function differently than liquid cash transfers (B4.1) perfectly neutralized Side A's affordability pressure, and B skillfully caught A's sequencing pivot in A4 to show that the poor would inevitably face price shocks before compensation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4's explanation of how weak states finance universal subsidies through state-company arrears, whereas targeted transfers require upfront liquid cash, decisively answered A's main affordability challenge.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B won by making the decisive clash clearer: thin fiscal buffers cannot sustain a far more expensive, regressive universal subsidy, so a managed five-year self-financing phase is safer than deferred collapse via arrears. A raised real capacity and exclusion risks and kept the deadline under pressure, but its conditional-no-clock alternative never answered how delay avoids overnight forced cuts, and the clipped opening plus weaker comparative weighing left B with the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's flip of A's thin-buffer point (pressure Q1 through B4.5/closing): if weak states cannot afford targeted transfers, they still less can afford universal waste hidden as company losses—that is deferred collapse, not stability.
