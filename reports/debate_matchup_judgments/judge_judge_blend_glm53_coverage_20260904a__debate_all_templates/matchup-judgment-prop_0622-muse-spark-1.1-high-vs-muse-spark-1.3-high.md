### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases—A on efficiency, progressivity, and the political economy of a growing dividend; B on structural bottlenecks (permitting, split landlord-tenant incentives, capital access for lumpy infrastructure) that a bare price signal cannot resolve. The decisive exchange was B's repeated pressure on concrete institutional failures: a carbon price cannot itself grant permits, coordinate multi-state transmission compacts, or give a renter with no capital access to an $18k heat pump. A's counters (declining asset value forces landlord upgrades; pooled subsidy dollars face the same permitting bottleneck) were clever but largely turned the critique back on B rather than fully dissolving it, and B's rejoinder that landlords facing lower rents more plausibly defer maintenance than invest was left standing. A's Rebuttal 1 was also clipped before finishing its argument, a real execution cost. Both closings crystallized the clash well, with B's recurring 'steel in the ground' framing giving a slightly sharper memorable contrast than A's shareholder framing. On balance B's case for why price alone under-delivers on access and coordination was less thoroughly neutralized than A's case was undermined, giving B a modest but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over the Cleveland landlord/tenant split incentive (A2.2 → B2.3/B2.5 → A4.3 → B4.3) was the most decisive clash: A's claim that falling rents force efficiency upgrades was met by B's plausible counter that landlords facing devaluation are more likely to defer maintenance and squeeze tenants, a point A never fully closed off.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A won because it maintained a cleaner comparative: a uniform price finds cheaper cuts everywhere, while subsidies pick winners and guarantee high cost per ton. A also turned B's infrastructure argument by showing that lumpy subsidized projects face the same permitting bottlenecks and create lobbies to keep carbon cheap, whereas dividends build a durable pro-price constituency. B's missing-alternative and fairness critiques were sharp but were partially neutralized by A's asset-value mechanism and the regressivity of subsidies flowing to capital owners. A's clipped Rebuttal 1 was a real execution cost, but not enough to erase its live routes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to B's permitting and provision arguments: showing that pooled revenue does not bypass land-rights vetoes, that subsidized factories lobby to keep carbon cheap, and that the dividend creates voters who profit from a higher price.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A built a more convincing and grounded case by focusing on the structural and physical bottlenecks of decarbonization, such as transmission permitting, landlord-tenant split incentives, and upfront capital constraints. Side A effectively demonstrated that a price signal alone cannot overcome these institutional barriers and that pooling revenue for targeted public investment is necessary to build viable alternatives. Side B's theoretical elegance was repeatedly undercut by Side A's sharp, practical rebuttals, and Side B struggled to explain how a carbon price alone solves physical infrastructure bottlenecks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the permitting and transmission bottleneck, clearly distinguishing between the motive created by a carbon price and the actual administrative and physical capacity required to build infrastructure, which only pooled public revenue can provide.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides executed the format cleanly, including proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 labeling, and both closings crystallized rather than widened the debate. The decisive difference was in the core mechanism clash: B's split-incentive argument (landlord/tenant, renter with no bus) and its historical grounding (feed-in tariffs and manufacturing subsidies preceding, not following, cost collapses in solar/batteries) pressed directly on A's central causal claim that a price signal alone summons supply-side infrastructure. A's answers to this pressure — "private capital does, drawn by profit," and vague claims that dividend checks "typically exceed" costs and buildings will "bleed value" until landlords act — were plausible but thinner and more assertion-heavy than B's concrete coordination-failure story. A's fairness/equity case (regressivity solved by dividends, subsidy capture by the wealthy, administrative friction) remained a real and largely unrebutted strength, but B successfully reframed it ("a quarterly offset vs. a permanent bill cut") rather than conceding it, blunting its decisiveness. On balance B did more to expose and sustain pressure on A's central mechanism, while A's strongest point was contained rather than defeated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over pressure questions on the renter/landlord split incentive (B3.1 and A's A1 answer at A4.1–A4.2, followed by B4.1's counter that the plan "guarantees the bus never comes") was the most decisive clash, testing whether a price-plus-dividend model can actually move supply-side actors without funding mechanisms, and B's rebuttal landed with more concrete force than A's response.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that carbon pricing alone cannot conjure the physical and industrial substitutes needed for decarbonization, while Side A never overcame the empirical gap between price signals and actual buildout. B’s concrete historical examples of subsidy-driven cost declines and the "pain without decline" frame gave B a cleaner, albeit narrow, path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s sustained attack on the supply-side gap—arguing that 100% rebates leave zero revenue for factories, grids, and retrofits, and that banks do not finance infrastructure on price promises alone—materially weakened A’s assumption that a credible price would summon alternatives automatically.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively exposing the structural flaw in Side B's model: rebating 100% of carbon revenue as dividends leaves zero public funding for the infrastructure, grid upgrades, and supply-side scale needed to make clean alternatives actually available. Side B's reliance on "price will conjure alternatives" and private capital failed to overcome Side A's concrete examples of coordination failures and split incentives (like landlords and grid queues). Side A's framing of "price pain without emissions decline" was highly effective and consistently applied.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it answered the pressure questions by turning Side B's "waitlist" argument against it ("you guarantee the bus never comes") and hammered the point that a 100% cash rebate starves the public investments required to build the substitutes that the price signal assumes will exist.
