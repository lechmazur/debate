### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a clearer baseline comparison (full cartel monopoly under prohibition versus partial predation under regulation) and directly neutralizing CON’s displacement and capacity claims in rebuttals and answers to pressure questions. Side B’s extortion and substitution arguments were sharp but failed to show why the status quo weakens crime more than the regulated alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s direct answers to B’s pressure questions (A4.1-A4.3) plus monopoly-versus-parasitism frame in closing, which cleanly resolved the live clash on revenue loss and state visibility.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won by establishing a cleaner comparative frame: under prohibition organized crime enjoys a full monopoly, whereas under national legalization it is reduced to a parasite on a visible, regulated market. CON’s infiltration and extortion arguments were blunted by PRO’s persistent reductio—cartels already extort legal sectors like avocados, yet no one argues for banning them—and by PRO’s exposure of CON’s tension in treating cannabis as simultaneously negligible and dangerously disruptive to remove. CON’s “visibility as vulnerability” and displacement-to-worse-violence points were evocative but never fully overcame the baseline comparison: monopoly is more power than parasitism. PRO’s closing crystallized this clash decisively, while CON’s attempts to distinguish cannabis from other extorted sectors felt like special pleading. The debate was competitive and well-argued on both sides, but PRO maintained the clearer live route to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s rebuttal exposing that CON’s extortion argument proves too much—if cartels infiltrating legal markets justifies prohibition, then avocados and limes should be illegal too—crippled CON’s core mechanism by showing the issue is state capacity, not legalization itself.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully grounding the debate in Latin America's institutional realities and turning PRO's core mechanism against them. While PRO argued that legalization moves cartels from "monopoly" to "parasite," CON sharply rebutted that this shift actually subsidizes syndicates by outsourcing operational risks and production costs to civilians, leaving cartels to extract risk-free profits via extortion. CON also cleanly resolved PRO's contradiction charge by distinguishing between strategic leadership revenue and local enforcer payroll, and won the decisive weighing clash: even if cartel accounting shrinks, provoking a shift from consensual drug sales to violent civilian predation fails the resolution's goal of weakening organized crime's threat. PRO's regulatory optimism was logically sound but less adapted to the weak-state context, making CON's comparative case more convincing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's "monopoly vs. parasite" framing was decisive. By arguing that legalization forces civilians to bear the financial and security burdens of building fixed, registered businesses while cartels simply use public registries as target lists for extortion, CON fundamentally undercut PRO's claim that visibility and regulation inherently weaken criminal power in high-conflict zones.
