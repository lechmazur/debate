### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive entitlement clash: PRO kept sliding from industrial technical copies and market displacement to automatic payment without establishing why copyright should cover statistical learning rather than expression. CON answered pressure directly with a function-based intermediate-copy line and the noninfringement competition point, then used administrability and entry-cost rebuttals to show PRO's "imperfect collective schemes" and deferrals still tax without linking value or harm. PRO remained strong on holdouts, litigation lottery, and practical certainty-plus-payment framing, but never recovered the circular market-creation problem or the unworkable who/how-much allocation. The motion fails if the new royalty is not owed; CON made that the cleaner vote path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.6/A4.3–A4.5: market displacement does not manufacture a copyright entitlement, and PRO's rate menu assumes the disputed royalty.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won the entitlement and administrability clash. PRO asserted upstream copies must be paid, but CON's functional test for intermediate transformative copying answered A's line-drawing pressure, and showed market shrinkage alone isn't a copyright injury. PRO's answer to how to split billions of untraceable contributions remained a menu of incompatible proxies, which CON exposed as arbitrary transfers to intermediaries that tax entry. PRO's entry-barrier flip failed because clarifying lawful analytical training gives certainty without a mandatory pre-revenue toll, while a compulsory fee + compliance entrenches incumbents.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2 exposing that per-work, per-dataset, and revenue-share are incompatible proxies and that PRO's 'imperfect beats zero' begs the question whether any royalty is owed, paired with B4 functional boundary for lawful analytical copies.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully challenging the foundational premise of Side B's case: that technical copying for analytical purposes inherently triggers a copyright compensation right. Side A effectively distinguished between intermediate/transformative copying (like search indexing or human learning) and expressive distribution, and dismantled Side B's music analogy. Side A also provided a cleaner, more principled answer to the pressure questions, offering a functional test for lawful training, whereas Side B's answers on administrability relied on a "menu of guesses" that Side A convincingly showed would be arbitrary and favor incumbents. Side A's closing crystallization ("Imperfect payment beats zero only if payment was owed in the first place") perfectly captured the decisive clash and exposed the circularity in Side B's market displacement argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which dismantled Side B's "imperfect distribution beats no distribution" argument by pointing out that arbitrary transfers to intermediaries funded by smaller innovators can be worse than no royalty, and that a budgetable burden remains an unjustified toll if no protected expression is actually exploited.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive policy clash by keeping the decisive comparison on forced sale rather than institutional convenience. PRO built a coherent mass-licensing case and answered rate/unknown-owner pressure cleanly, but never neutralized the live training/output bargain critique or why non-monetary refusal should be extinguished. CON made the motion easier to reject: escrow-after-ingestion is acquisition, standardized fees price culture as raw material, and voluntary pools preserve real consent without treating foundation-scale ingestion as an entitlement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's training/output separation attack (especially A2.7/A5.2): a flat ingestion fee plus later output litigation is a bad forced trade, not compensation for displacement.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): PRO won the institutional comparison. It framed the clash as mass-use market failure and showed why opt-in creates holdouts and favors incumbents, while a rate based on observable ingestion plus audits/escrow and preserved output liability delivers lawful access plus payment. CON's strongest point—right to say no—was blunted by PRO's distinction between expression and style/voice and by CON's own answer that startups should just omit unreachable works, which concedes PRO's scale and competition argument. PRO also answered both pressure questions directly with A1/A2.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2/A4 flip on unmeasurable contribution and startup fixed costs — arguing that individual negotiation prices bargaining power not causal contribution, and that audits are lighter than millions of negotiations/litigation — which undercut CON's core fairness and pro-startup claims.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B won by demonstrating that Side A's opt-in alternative is practically unworkable at foundation-model scale and inherently favors massive incumbents who can afford exclusive catalogs. Side B's compulsory licensing scheme offered a scalable, legally grounded mechanism that balances access and compensation. Furthermore, Side B handled the pressure questions much more effectively, providing concrete mechanisms for rate-setting and orphan works, whereas Side A's "just omit them" answer inadvertently conceded the practical limitations and anti-competitive realities of its own model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to Side A's pressure question answers, sharply pointing out that A's opt-in model practically excludes startups, forces freelancers to independently police datasets, and ultimately concentrates power among large catalog owners, thereby exposing the real-world flaws in A's preferred regime.
