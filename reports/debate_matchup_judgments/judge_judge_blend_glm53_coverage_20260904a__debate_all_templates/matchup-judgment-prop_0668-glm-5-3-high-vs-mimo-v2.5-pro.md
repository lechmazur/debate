CON's (A) strongest mechanism—that bans just push buyers to unconditioned Chinese/Russian suppliers—was met by PRO's seller-responsibility framing and a plausible "torture equipment" analogy, and PRO consistently pressed the asymmetric-harm weighing (lost sale vs. irreversible persecution) that CON never squarely rebutted, only asserted a counter-mechanism without evidence. The pressure round was pivotal: PRO's pointed question about whether CON could cite any case where end-use conditions actually stopped misuse went unanswered with a real case—CON simply deflected by turning the question back on PRO. Worse, CON's labeled "A2" answer to PRO's gun-dealer analogy never actually engaged the analogy at all, a real execution failure under the format rules. PRO, by contrast, gave substantive if imperfect answers to CON's own questions (disputing the "spied on either way" equivalence, offering existing designation mechanisms). PRO's closing crystallized this asymmetry sharply ("Twice, they pivoted"), while CON's closing was cut off mid-sentence, further weakening its final crystallization. CON's substitution and category-workability arguments remain live and reasonably strong, keeping this from being a blowout, but PRO's tighter rebuttal discipline and cleaner exploitation of the pressure round give it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question demanding a concrete case where end-use conditions stopped misuse, and CON's failure to answer it (both in that round and in the follow-up "A1"), combined with CON's non-answer to the gun-dealer analogy, was the most decisive exchange—it exposed the empirical hollowness of CON's leverage claim at the exact moment it was most load-bearing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): CON (A) built the more resilient comparative case. Its core mechanism—conditional licensing preserves ongoing leverage (revocation, audits, whistleblowers) while a categorical ban simply hands the market to unaccountable suppliers—was defended cleanly through the exchange. A's sharpest moments were tactical reversals: turning PRO's missile analogy into evidence that licensing, not banning, is the actual global norm, and turning PRO's "suppliers aren't interchangeable" point into an argument for engagement-based leverage rather than against it. In the pressure round and second rebuttal, A also exposed a structural symmetry PRO never fully answered: the same governments PRO admits "lacked will" to enforce licensing are the ones who would administer and selectively waive a categorical ban, making the "bright line" argument less decisive than PRO claimed. PRO (B) had a strong, morally coherent opening and used the Pegasus example effectively as recurring evidence of regulatory failure, and its concession that allies should also face bans was an honest, consistency-preserving move. But PRO's closing never directly rebutted A's waiver-bending point, repeating the "sale itself is the violation" framing without engaging the mechanism by which bans get quietly exempted. PRO also suffered three separate word-limit clippings (rebuttal 1, rebuttal 2, and closing), cutting off argument development at key moments, while A was clipped only once. Combined with A having the last word and using it to reassert the unanswered symmetry argument, this gives A a clear, though not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's reversal of PRO's "suppliers aren't interchangeable" argument (A2.4/A4.3)—turning it into proof that engagement retains leverage rather than proof that a ban would meaningfully degrade capability—combined with A's final closing point that the same governments PRO trusts to enforce a ban routinely grant waivers, which PRO's clipped closing never directly answered.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by masterfully co-opting Side A's strongest points. When A argued that regulators lack the political will to revoke licenses, B successfully pointed out that those same regulators would be in charge of enforcing a ban, making the political failure symmetric. B then broke the symmetry by showing that a licensing regime at least leaves a paper trail and retains incumbent leverage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 provided an excellent answer to A's dilemma, accepting that cutting access degrades capability in order to prove that democracies should maintain the contract precisely so they hold the kill switch, rather than abandoning the market to untrackable suppliers.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): CON built the stronger comparative case by framing the debate around harm reduction to dissidents rather than moral comfort for exporters. While PRO effectively exposed the failures of existing licensing and the moral weight of complicity, CON absorbed the regulatory-capture critique with automatic triggers, transparency mandates, and protected whistleblower channels, then turned the symmetry: the same politically unwilling governments must administer PRO's ban, bending bright lines invisibly without the audit trails that licensing creates. CON's "detection ecosystem" argument—that Pegasus was exposed because Western supply chains leave contractual and technical traces, whereas pushing regimes to no-strings suppliers destroys the leak pathways that protect dissidents—gave CON a live, unanswered route to victory. PRO's missiles analogy was cleanly turned to support conditional control, and CON closed with a sharp comfort-versus-protection frame that crystallized the weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's turn of PRO's missiles analogy in Rebuttal 1 (B2.1–B2.2), showing that democracies license rather than categorically ban arms sales, so PRO's own comparison supported conditional control over prohibition.
