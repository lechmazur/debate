CON’s alternative—preferences, aid, and targeted adjustments—kept the productive channel open while changing incentives. PRO’s clipped opening and visible retreat from thick standards (carbon prices, collective bargaining) to thin floors weakened their causal story, and they never squarely answered CON’s concrete mechanism about small producers failing the documentation test.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal defanged PRO’s leverage trap by distinguishing between a gate that diverts trade and a premium-plus-capacity channel that keeps the factory standing while changing the margin, directly undermining PRO’s claim that enforceable teeth are the only missing ingredient.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A consistently maintained the upper hand by framing the alternative as an unworkable double standard and effectively trapping Side B on the poverty impacts. Side B's attempt to weaponize Side A's "phased timelines" as a concession was rhetorically sharp, but A had already successfully distinguished a timeline with a mandatory floor from B's voluntary ladder.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 defense of incomplete enforcement (A4.3-A4.4) was excellent, successfully arguing that while all customs rules leak, an imperfectly enforced condition still raises the cost of cheating and beats a chapter no one can invoke.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that the mirror-clause mechanism is in practice a unilaterally defined certifier's bar—covering inspections, collective bargaining, and carbon registries—that structurally excludes developing partners who lack compliance capacity, producing exclusion without reform. CON's ladder-versus-lock frame sharpened the comparative choice, and PRO's concession that weak infrastructure deserves phased timelines fed directly into CON's argument that graduated, incentive-based access is the superior path. PRO's tax-on-the-compliant frame was rhetorically strong but never adequately answered the rerouting-and-laundering problem or the ambiguity of Europe-defined labor standards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4), which demonstrated that the minimum floor is in practice a certifier's bar far beyond forced labor, introduced the Generalized Scheme of Preferences as a concrete ladder alternative, and established that incomplete enforcement of a rigid gate creates laundering and added emissions while incomplete enforcement of graduated incentives retains leverage and partial improvement.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): PRO won the central comparison: privileged market access must carry the floor Europe already pays, or domestic rules become a tax on the compliant and a subsidy at the dock. A’s leverage paradox – if EU market is essential it bites, if disposable the poverty lockout collapses – plus the narrowing of “minimum” to forced labor / systematic denial / carbon dumping (not wages) neutralized CON’s strongest poverty and exclusion claims. Phased timelines answered capacity gaps and the imperfect-enforcement defense (all customs rules leak) held, while CON’s ladder alternative conceded conditionality works and its closing was clipped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R1/R2 leverage paradox and A1/A2 in R2 – cheap legal labor survives, silenced workers and unrestricted dumping do not – directly answering B’s low-wage vs violation and who-defines / laundering pressure questions.
