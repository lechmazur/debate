### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B ran the cleaner analytical case throughout. Its core framing — that compensation tracks value delivered, not zip code — was consistently more principled than Side A's cost-of-living premise, which B correctly identified as circular: "inflated" only works if you've already accepted that geography determines pay. B's most decisive blow was the coffee-analogy rebuttal in Rebuttal 2: coffee costs more in Manhattan because its inputs are locally sourced, but a remote worker's output isn't produced from local inputs — the code doesn't change with the worker's zip code, and neither does the cost of producing it. This directly destroyed one of PRO's most concrete analogies. B also consistently won the internal equity clash: A's two-developers-in-Kansas example was colorful, but B's answer — that the just resolution to unequal pay for equal work is to raise the lower salary, not cut the higher — was never cleanly answered. A conceded the two-way adjustment in Rebuttal 2 but never explained why the correct direction is downward rather than upward. B's pressure question about the "only flows downward" asymmetry landed, and A's answer, while honest, didn't resolve the underlying burden. B also effectively tagged A's sustainability argument as a hostage argument and defended this framing across multiple rounds. The closing "same work, same pay — that's the line that doesn't move" crystallized the debate's central clash cleanly. Side A had genuine strengths (gentrification, purchasing-power parity, "borderless market" contradiction in Q1) but left too many key pressures unanswered and repeatedly asserted without mechanism (payroll savings → more hires). B won the majority of the live clashes by the final round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal to the coffee analogy [B4.5–B4.6]: PRO argued a coffee costs more in Manhattan due to local economic realities, so labor should too. B's response — that coffee costs more because its inputs are locally sourced, whereas a remote worker's output is not produced from local inputs and neither is the cost of producing it — was the sharpest, most direct exchange in the debate. It flipped PRO's own concrete analogy and left the geographic-inputs premise of PRO's whole framework without grounding.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B built the stronger path by consistently anchoring pay to identical output/value, turning the internal-equity clash by advocating raising local pay rather than cutting relocated pay, exposing the circular "inflated" framing, and showing PRO's borderless-market premise contradicts their coastal-default demand. Side A landed solid local-market and gentrification points but saw them neutralized or reversed; their pressure-question answers were direct yet failed to resolve the core inconsistency. Later rounds narrowed effectively for B while A repeated equity claims without new comparative force.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3 reversal of gentrification (surplus flows to locals vs. shareholders) plus B2.1/4.1 on borderless but non-bottomless national clearing rate.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by consistently outmaneuvering PRO on the core economic clash. CON successfully reframed the relevant labor market as national rather than geographic, cleanly answered PRO's pressure questions by distinguishing HQ collaboration premiums from individual output value, and dismantled PRO's coffee analogy by noting remote output lacks locally sourced inputs. CON also effectively turned PRO's offshoring warning into a concession that the framework is pure labor arbitrage. PRO argued competently on internal equity and gentrification, but relied on assertions and analogies that CON neutralized with sharper causal reasoning and tighter framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's coffee analogy and market-baseline question: distinguishing "borderless" from "bottomless" to show national skill clearing rates don't collapse to rural averages, and pointing out that digital output isn't priced on local inputs, which severed PRO's core pricing mechanism.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A (PRO) built the stronger technical case and made the more decisive argumentative moves across the exchange. PRO's core framework—that wages are set by market-clearing rates and outside options, not by output alone—is more accurate as a description of how labor markets actually function, and PRO defended it consistently. The most decisive exchange came in Rebuttal 2 [A4.5], where PRO exposed an internal contradiction in CON's case: CON simultaneously claims pay should track individual output and that competitors will pay a "national rate," but a going rate is itself a market construct, not an output measure—which proves PRO's framework rather than CON's. CON never answered this. PRO's pressure Q1—asking why many remote-first companies themselves use location-based pay bands—was similarly unanswered; CON dismissed it as "corporate opportunism" without engaging the empirical challenge it posed to the "borderless national market" narrative. CON's strongest moves were rhetorical: the "geographic illusion," "corporate clawback," and $500K-value-for-$150K-salary arguments are emotionally resonant and land cleanly. But CON's foundational "equal output = equal pay" slogan describes no actual compensation system, and CON never resolved how the national-rate claim coexists with its pure-output logic. PRO's closing effectively crystallized this contradiction. The gap is clear but competitive because CON's rhetoric is genuinely effective and the "who owns the upside of relocation" framing is a live and compelling moral intuition that PRO only partially neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 move at [A4.5]: catching CON's internal contradiction between invoking "individual output" as the pay metric and simultaneously invoking a "national rate" competitors will pay. PRO correctly noted that a going rate is a market construct, not an output measure—which confirms PRO's framework and undermines CON's. CON never answered this in either subsequent speech.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by establishing labor-market clearing prices as the operative framework, exposing CON's internal contradiction between "output only" and "national rate," and showing how location-adjusted pay sustains remote access at scale; CON's responses stay repetitive and fail to neutralize the market-dynamics rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.5) directly answers both pressure questions and crystallizes that CON's own language concedes market constructs over pure output.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully exposing a core tension in Side A's framework: A argued pay should strictly track individual output, yet simultaneously invoked a "national market rate" to justify retention. B sharply noted that if pay truly equaled output, no "rate" would exist, proving wages are set by market dynamics and outside options, not just value generation. B also effectively dismantled A's personal-overhead analogies by distinguishing them from labor market competition, and provided a more economically grounded model of how compensation actually clears. A's rhetoric was punchy but relied on an idealized compensation model that B consistently outmaneuvered through direct structural rebuttals and clearer weighing of sustainability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.5] identifying the contradiction between Side A's "pay equals output" premise and their reliance on a "national market rate," proving that wages are inherently market constructs rather than pure value accounting.
