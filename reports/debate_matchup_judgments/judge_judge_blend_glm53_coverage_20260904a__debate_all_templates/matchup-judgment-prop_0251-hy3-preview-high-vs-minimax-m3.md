### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won on the decisive clash: the distinction between "mandatory temperature-triggered graduated controls" and "mandatory stop-work." CON correctly and persistently showed that California's high-heat standards activate specific employer obligations—shade, water, rest cycles—at temperature thresholds, but do not halt operations. PRO repeatedly claimed California proved mandatory triggers align with stop-work protection, but never answered CON's clarification that mandatory controls and mandatory shutdowns are different policies. This confusion ran through PRO's entire case and was never resolved. CON also landed the sharper blow on the wage-replacement problem: PRO's A2 response simply asserted that "the employer or the state" must bear the cost of halted shifts, but offered no mechanism, no fund, and no precedent. CON's closing correctly called this an empty promise and reinforced the informal-economy harm, which PRO never adequately answered. PRO's strongest point—the power imbalance making voluntary controls fail—was met by CON's reasonable counter that stronger enforcement plus whistleblower protections addresses that problem without confiscating wages. Both sides had turns clipped for exceeding word limits, balancing that execution cost. PRO's human-stakes framing was rhetorically effective and the power-imbalance argument is real, but PRO kept mischaracterizing the California model and left the wage-replacement hole wide open. CON offered a coherent alternative—mandatory, penalty-backed, graduated controls—that achieved PRO's stated goals without the costs PRO could not address, making CON the cleaner path to a verdict.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 [A4.1–A4.2] directly and precisely distinguished "mandatory controls triggered by temperature" from "mandatory stop-work," correcting PRO's repeated misreading of California's standard and showing that CON's own proposal already satisfies the "mandatory" requirement PRO was demanding—while avoiding the wage-loss harm PRO never solved.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): A won because it made the key distinction clearer: mandatory heat protections do not necessarily require mandatory stop-work. A’s alternative of enforceable graduated controls answered the safety concern while preserving a strong comparative disadvantage against B: wage loss, informalization, and weak implementation details for who pays during stoppages. B persuasively established that heat is dangerous and that voluntary measures fail, but often proved the need for mandatory regulation rather than the specific need for stop-work. B’s late answer that employers or the state would bear the cost was too underdeveloped to overcome A’s pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 was decisive: A directly answered B’s questions by distinguishing California-style mandatory triggers for controls from mandatory shutdowns, then explained how fines, liability, and OSHA-style enforcement could give graduated controls real teeth without the wage harms of stop-work.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B wins by cleanly separating "mandatory temperature-triggered controls" from "mandatory stop-work," showing the motion's specific remedy is unnecessarily blunt and harmful to vulnerable workers. Side A built a strong moral case around power imbalances and employer noncompliance, but repeatedly conflated temperature triggers with full work stoppages and failed to answer the wage-loss/informal-economy pressure with a concrete funding mechanism. Side B's California example effectively demonstrated that graduated controls can be mandatory and penalizable without halting work, undercutting PRO's core necessity claim. CON's rebuttals were sharper, directly weighed the economic tradeoffs, and turned PRO's power-imbalance argument back on them by advocating whistleblower protections and fines rather than shift confiscation. PRO's rhetoric was compelling but could not overcome the practical vulnerabilities CON exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.1-B4.5] decisively clarified that California's model uses temperature thresholds to mandate specific controls, not stop-work, and answered PRO's enforcement critique by pointing to standard OSHA-style penalties and whistleblower protections. This surgically removed PRO's conflation of "mandatory triggers" with "mandatory shutdowns" and framed stop-work as wage confiscation rather than safety, shifting the comparative weighing firmly to CON.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO built a stronger and more durable case across all five rounds. Its opening framed the core structural argument cleanly: where workers cannot freely refuse dangerous conditions, the risk cannot be left to the party profiting from the work continuing. The historical parallels to asbestos, silica, and machine guarding established that the labor-rights frame is not novel radicalism but the standard response to occupational hazard. These parallels were never convincingly answered—CON's counter that "better enforcement of existing standards" would suffice was directly undercut by PRO's pressure question on the live body count occurring under those same standards. CON's best individual moment was the sharp observation in Rebuttal 2 that California's heat standard does not include mandatory stop-work, which genuinely destabilized PRO's use of California as direct evidence for stop-work innovation. However, this point came late, was confined to one application (the perverse-incentives claim), and left the broader structural argument—power imbalance, the race-to-the-bottom dynamic, the failure of voluntary frameworks across all comparable hazards—entirely untouched. PRO answered both pressure questions with the required A1/A2 format and with substantive responses: the WBGT technical answer addressed the site-specific-variables objection, and the wage-continuation answer was grounded in real analogues (California heat recovery pay, Spain contract continuity). CON's answers to PRO's questions were weaker: the "better enforcement" answer to the body-count question is philosophically the same solution that has already been failing, and the response on California merely narrowed the evidentiary claim without rebuilding the underlying normative case. CON's opening was also clipped for length—a real execution cost at the most important moment for establishing the core narrative. PRO's "who pays for a destroyed kidney versus who pays for a hot afternoon" comparison crystallized the weighing issue memorably and fairly. CON never produced a comparably memorable frame that resolved the fundamental power-imbalance problem at the heart of the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 [B2.4] directly answering the "general duty clauses already exist" argument was the most decisive single exchange. PRO identified that heat is the only major occupational killer without a dedicated federal standard, explained why general duty clauses produce almost no enforcement in practice (heat damage is hard to photograph, causation is contested, workers don't complain before they collapse), and reframed "subsidiarity" as leaving the call to the party that profits from doing nothing. CON never escaped this logic—its consistent fallback to "better enforcement of existing standards" required it to defend the very framework PRO had just shown was structurally incapable of protecting workers from heat.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): B won by making the core labor-rights clash clearer: extreme heat is a workplace hazard where individual refusal is not meaningful because of employer power and economic coercion. A had useful pressure on income loss, rigidity, and California not proving stop-work mandates specifically, but leaned too heavily on a repeated strawman of universal shutdown and offered only vague better enforcement as an alternative to rules it conceded are failing. B answered the main pressure with tiered WBGT-style thresholds, wage/job protection, and the analogy to other mandatory safety standards, while giving the cleaner reason why costs should be internalized by employers rather than workers bodies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal was most decisive: it reframed mandatory stop-work as calibrated graduated protections rather than a binary summer shutdown, then turned A's flexibility argument into employer discretion that predictably externalizes harm onto low-wage workers.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing extreme heat as a systemic power imbalance that flexible or voluntarily enforced standards cannot resolve. A’s explanation of why general duty clauses fail for heat (delayed symptoms, causation disputes, worker fear of retaliation) decisively undercut B’s reliance on “better enforcement.” A also handled pressure cleanly, clarifying that thresholds are tiered floors (WBGT) and that wage continuation aligns with standard labor-rights hazard internalization. While B raised valid concerns about hourly income loss and correctly noted California’s rules lack full stop-work mandates, B’s “perverse incentive” claim was logically vulnerable and repeatedly countered by A’s historical analogies to other safety regulations. A’s sharper weighing (long-term bodily harm vs. short-term scheduling costs) and more direct rebuttals secured the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 directly answering the pressure questions. A dismantled the “arbitrary threshold” claim by explaining WBGT metrics, reframed wage continuation as standard hazard internalization, and turned B’s “perverse incentive” argument by showing how safety mandates historically drive compliance markets rather than strangle them. This exchange crystallized the clash and neutralized B’s strongest practical objections.
