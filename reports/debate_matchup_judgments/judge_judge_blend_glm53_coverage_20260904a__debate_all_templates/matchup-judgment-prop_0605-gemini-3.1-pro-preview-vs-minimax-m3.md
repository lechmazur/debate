CON's clipped speeches and reliance on vague warnings about broker markets—without showing they would be worse than current kafala abuses—left their middle path looking insufficient.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's reframing of CON's recruitment-cost argument as a defense of indentured servitude [A2.5], which shifted the burden and morally delegitimized CON's economic defense of employer-controlled residency.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A wins by securing the structural prerequisite clash. While Side B offered a strong technocratic case centered on sequencing, institutional capacity, and real-world reform examples (Qatar/Saudi), it never fully neutralized A's core mechanism: enforcement is functionally impossible when the abuser controls the victim's legal residency. ### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive debate by making the practical replacement burden decisive. A had the stronger moral framing on coercion and abuse, but leaned on shaky comparisons to Singapore/Korea and did not convincingly answer how low-wage workers would retain housing and welfare during job transitions at Gulf demographic scale. B’s defense of targeted reform plus employer-linked logistics gave a more concrete reason not to adopt the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was most decisive: it directly answered A’s fiscal and SME questions, flipped the Singapore example by arguing low-wage portability is restricted there, and pressed the housing affordability problem against A’s private-rental answer.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by exposing a persistent circularity in CON's defense: every structural benefit of kafala (employer housing, repatriation, SME recruitment viability) depends on legally trapping workers, which is precisely the abuse the motion targets. A also gave cleaner answers to the pressure questions, showing that employer levies and housing accounts can replace coercive sponsorship without the predicted catastrophe, and turned Qatar's actual reforms into evidence that portability is already working. B's demographic-scale argument and Singapore objection were formidable, but never escaped the frame that they were defending bondage because the logistics of bondage would otherwise collapse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's repeated demonstration that CON's structural necessities (employer housing, SME recruitment costs, repatriation) were circular—each justified kafala by citing benefits that exist only because kafala forbids worker mobility—defanged B's practical objections and recast them as admissions that the system runs on immobilized labor.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): CON wins by successfully distinguishing the Gulf's unique demographic scale and grounding the debate in logistical reality. While PRO offered sharp rhetorical attacks on the structural incentives of sponsorship, CON decisively undercut PRO's core comparative example by noting Singapore explicitly restricts job portability for low-wage permit holders. CON also effectively argued that severing the employer link destroys the only guaranteed housing safety net for low-wage workers who cannot afford private rent, making PRO's transition plan unworkable. PRO's reliance on sovereign wealth to fund welfare for a foreign supermajority remained hand-wavy against CON's structural framing of sponsorship as a decentralized logistics framework. CON's targeted reform alternative proved more feasible and better weighed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Turn 8 response dismantling PRO's Singapore comparison. By pointing out that Singapore restricts portability for low-wage workers and requires employer consent for transfers, CON directly neutralized PRO's primary empirical model. Coupled with the practical argument that low-wage migrants cannot afford private rent, this rebuttal collapsed PRO's feasibility case and forced PRO into an ungrounded reliance on sovereign wealth.
