### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built the more durable throughline: bonds are a financial receipt, not a physical remedy, and calibration either makes the bond a pay-to-pollute fee (via surety) or a crushing barrier, while jurisdictional leakage undercuts universal enforcement. PRO's strongest move—introducing surety underwriting to solve the calibration trap—was turned against it by CON, who showed the surety model shrinks operator "skin in the game" to a negotiable premium, exactly the pay-to-pollute outcome CON warned about. The pressure round exposed a genuine tension in PRO's case: strict liability (A1) formally retracted PRO's opening claim that bonds don't punish "bad luck," and PRO's answer to the desperate-burn problem (A2)—that reckless attempts also forfeit—left the cornered operator with nothing to lose, which CON correctly noted cuts in CON's favor rather than resolving it. PRO's best surviving point was the bankruptcy/insolvency leverage problem, which CON answered adequately but not airtight (fleet-wide revocation plus an ad hoc "risk pool" that itself resembles a financial backstop). On balance CON's "prevention happens before launch, bonds pay after the cascade" framing was sharper, more consistently defended, and better exploited PRO's concessions, though CON loses a small execution point for a rebuttal that was clipped for exceeding the word limit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's surety-bond fix ("the bond shrinks to a premium... pay-to-pollute fee wearing a suit") combined with the Pressure Questions exchange on strict liability and the desperate-burn incentive, which forced PRO to concede strict liability and offer an answer to the burn scenario that CON then flipped to reinforce its own point.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): A narrowly won because its core ex ante incentive and bankruptcy-prefunding mechanisms survived. B compellingly exposed A's reversal on punishing unavoidable failures, but repeatedly treated licensing and bonds as substitutes and inaccurately framed an upfront bond as acting only after failure. B also never fully answered why risk-priced surety and retained liability would not improve design incentives alongside licensing. The clipped rebuttal was a minor execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's challenge to B's calibration argument was decisive: surety avoids immobilizing the bond's full value while risk-based premiums and indemnity preserve incentives. B's reply that the premium is harmless ex ante but the exposure severe after failure did not explain why rational operators and underwriters would ignore that expected liability during design.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won the prevention-timing clash. PRO conceded strict liability in B4.1, retracting B2.2's promise not to punish bad luck, and never resolved the desperate-burn perverse incentive. A's surety analysis – small premium during design vs full exposure at end-of-life, leaving a cornered operator with nothing to lose – answered B's contradiction charge and showed bonds act after harm while licensing bites before launch. PRO's bankruptcy and market-access points were partially answered by risk-pool and fleet revocation, and cash-can't-retrieve remained live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.2 / A2.1 + A2.5 – A turned B's pay-to-pollute vs existential gamble charge into the inverted-incentive defect: soft when design discipline should bite, savage when quiet passivation is needed.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): B built a cumulative, adaptive case around a single decisive asymmetry: standards and mandates evaporate the moment a company goes bankrupt or a satellite dies at an altitude drag never reaches, while a pre-funded bond survives exactly that moment. B's pressure questions (arbitrage cuts both ways; GEO/high-orbit satellites have no drag-based fix) landed hard, and A's answers effectively conceded the gap ("no policy does" physically stop debris) without explaining how technical mandates bind an insolvent owner or a satellite that dies outside self-clearing orbits. A's rebuttals largely repeated the "physics not finance" framing round after round rather than closing that hole, and its closing was recap-only, never confronting the insolvency/enforcement asymmetry B had made central. B's own turns were clipped twice for length, a real execution cost, but the core arguments were already delivered before the cuts, and B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("A plan is a promise. A bond is money.") in a way A's closing did not answer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Questions (arbitrage symmetry and the GEO/high-orbit gap) combined with A's answer conceding "no policy does" physically stop debris — this exposed that A's technical-standards remedy has the same enforcement/coverage gaps it accuses bonds of having, and A never recovered from that concession in Rebuttal 2 or Closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that bonds create ex ante incentives for redundancy and reserved disposal fuel while also preserving resources after insolvency. Side B repeatedly argued that cash cannot physically stop debris, but this did not answer A's incentive mechanism or explain who bears residual failures under technical standards. Arbitrage was B's strongest objection, yet A showed that national engineering mandates face similar evasion and proposed market access as an enforcement lever. A's two clipped rebuttals were execution costs, but its comparative case remained clearer and more complete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange answered the remediation dilemma: bonds do their main work before failure by changing safety investment, while forfeited funds remain better than collecting nothing from an insolvent operator. B largely repeated that money cannot alter physics and never neutralized this preventive incentive.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): PRO won the central comparative clash. CON's arbitrage claim was turned: if domestic bonds drive flags of convenience, so do domestic 5-year/passivation rules. PRO's pooling + prevention answer resolved the cost dilemma and the kinetic-energy question (cash funds tracking/removal and incentivizes ex ante compliance, just like insurance) while CON never answered who bears cost at 800km/GEO or when the firm is bankrupt. A plan vs money framing survived.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2/B4 response that CON's arbitrage argument is self-defeating plus port-state market-access enforcement, and that pooling/prevention means most bonds are never forfeited, which directly neutralized CON's affordable vs bankrupting dilemma.
