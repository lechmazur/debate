### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B exposed and sustained pressure on the central hole in A's case: A's justification for aggressive surcharges depended on peak revenue somehow sustaining broader affordable access, yet A repeatedly conceded ("no pricing rule ensures mass access") that this transfer is unguaranteed, while B's harm mechanism (immediate exclusion of inflexible families/groups at the moment of peak cultural relevance) remained direct and concrete. A's fallback move — treating both the harm and the benefit as equally uncertain — is a fair rhetorical parry but does not actually close the asymmetry B kept highlighting: a surcharge is a certain barrier, while its downstream cultural benefit is speculative. B's closing crystallized this with the "occupancy vs breadth" distinction, a clean and memorable frame that A's closing did not squarely rebut, instead repeating the whole-schedule framing without new leverage. A's rebuttals were solid and format-compliant throughout, and its "freezing prices doesn't create seats" point was a good structural counter, but it never fully neutralized B's mechanism critique. B loses a small amount of credit for having Rebuttal 1 clipped for exceeding the word limit, an execution cost, but the substantive content delivered before the cut was already complete and decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) directly answering CON's pressure questions — showing that rejecting surcharges keeps ability-to-pay from being the gatekeeper and that CON's revenue-to-access link is merely asserted, not guaranteed — was the decisive exchange, since CON's own Rebuttal 2 conceded "no pricing rule ensures mass access" without closing the resulting asymmetry.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins by effectively linking the specific mechanics of dynamic pricing (unpredictability, group vetoes, wealth-based sorting) to the erosion of the "mass cultural habit." Side B argues well that venues need revenue to survive and that scarcity excludes people regardless of price, but Side A successfully counters that sorting by wealth at peak cultural moments fundamentally changes cinema from an accessible ritual to a luxury, which B's revenue arguments fail to resolve.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction in Rebuttal 2 and Closing that a sold-out room measures occupancy, not breadth, cleanly neutralizing Side B's argument that full peak screenings still represent mass participation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A wins by keeping the debate focused on the motion's core: whether aggressive dynamic pricing undermines cinema-going as a mass cultural habit. B ably defends revenue and venue viability, but never fully bridges the gap between peak surcharges and preserved mass access, effectively conceding that no mechanism guarantees affordable seats will result. A successfully reframes the decisive comparison as a certain, immediate pricing barrier versus a speculative, unguaranteed subsidy story. A also neutralizes B's strongest point about sold-out screenings by distinguishing wealth-based exclusion from first-come planning and by emphasizing that stable off-peak discounts and memberships can manage demand without aggressive surcharges. In the closing, A crystallizes that a full room at a surge price is not the same as a mass habit, while B largely recaps without resolving the mechanism problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal answering B’s pressure questions: A distinguishes first-come planning from wealth-based gatekeeping at sold-out screenings and shows that stable discounts can manage demand without aggressive surcharges, establishing the decisive "certain barrier versus contingent benefit" frame.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent economic cases, but A's "blunt instrument" critique of B's proposed fixed-day/time rates—that a single stable weekend rate cannot simultaneously price a blockbuster and a quiet release correctly—was raised in Rebuttal 1 and never squarely answered by B, even when B tried to operationalize its "aggressive vs. differentiated" threshold in Rebuttal 2. B's framing (theaters must defend the "aggressive component" specifically) was a smart burden-shift and its equity point (willingness-to-pay displacing budget-constrained regulars) landed, but A neutralized much of that by noting speed-based rationing is equally arbitrary and that individual displacement doesn't establish aggregate erosion of the habit. A's reframing of "mass habit" as abundant, recurring access rather than equal access to one contested screening gave the judge a cleaner lens for weighing the clash, and A answered both pressure questions with plausible mechanisms (competitive incentive to discount, guessing-error costs of frozen rates) that B's side left comparatively under-defended. The clipped opening cost A some polish, but the substantive exchange in later rounds compensated. This is a close, competitive debate, with A having the slightly more resolving path through the central pricing-threshold dispute.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point that a single stable weekend rate is necessarily blunt—overpricing ordinary films or underpricing blockbusters—was never adequately resolved by B's later attempt (Rebuttal 2) to define "durable posted rates by day and time" as the workable threshold, leaving a live gap in B's core distinction.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully decoupling the benefits of off-peak discounts from the harms of peak surcharges, forcing Side B to defend the exclusionary nature of the latter. A's framing that preserving a mass habit requires keeping an 'affordable route' open was highly persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's argument in Rebuttal 2 that allocation by booking speed still leaves an affordable route open, whereas dynamic surcharges close it entirely regardless of planning.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): PRO won by exposing a false choice at the heart of CON's case. CON argued that aggressive demand-responsive pricing was necessary to fund deep off-peak discounts and keep theaters viable, but PRO showed that ordinary posted pricing with stable discounts and matinee rates already fills quiet seats without requiring unpredictable peak surcharges. Since an empty seat has near-zero marginal cost, theaters do not need extra peak extraction to make off-peak bargains sustainable. PRO also landed the decisive cultural comparison: occupied seats do not equal preserved habits if schedule-constrained regulars are systematically replaced by wealthier buyers on popular nights. CON treated these displaced patrons as isolated exceptions, but PRO demonstrated they are central to cinema as a mass cultural practice. CON's opening was clipped, and its mechanism remained more asserted than proven, while PRO's rebuttals maintained epistemic discipline and sharpened the clash effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's sustained attack on the necessity claim—showing that stable advertised discounts can fill off-peak seats without aggressive peak surcharges, because the marginal cost of an empty seat is minimal and theaters already have incentive to discount them. This neutralized CON's primary path to victory and left CON defending revenue extraction as a virtue rather than proving it was indispensable for broad participation.
