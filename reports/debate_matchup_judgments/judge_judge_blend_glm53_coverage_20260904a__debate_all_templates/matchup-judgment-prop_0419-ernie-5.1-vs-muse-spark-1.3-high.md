### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides opened with coherent, well-organized cases, but B built a sharper, more sustained mechanism-driven attack. B exploited A's own concession that bots already handle routine cases (turning A's "bots for trivial, humans for edge cases" into "then why mandate it?"), and introduced the "magnet" argument — that a legally guaranteed fast human line removes the friction that currently filters trivial requests, flooding queues and burying the complex cases A claims to protect. This point was pressed hard in rebuttal and again via Pressure Q1, and A's answer (A4.1, "no one wants a human to process a three-second cancel") never actually explained why exhausted, non-technical customers wouldn't simply press zero once a guarantee exists — the core mechanism went essentially unrebutted. B also successfully flipped the dark-pattern narrative (retention departments, not bots, invented the trap) and reframed the debate around "results vs. method," a framing A never dislodged. A's closing pivot to "verification/accountability" was met by B's closing line that "verification is a receipt, not a body," directly countering A's last framing with the debate's final word. A's case remained emotionally resonant and reasonably grounded but lacked a comparably decisive rebuttal to B's queue-flooding and incentive-cost arguments, leaving B with the cleaner, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's "magnet" argument (B2.2–B2.3, reinforced by Pressure Q1 in B3.1) — showing that a guaranteed fast human lane removes the friction currently filtering trivial requests, flooding queues and undermining the very protection A claims to offer — was the most damaging exchange, and A's answer in A4.1 never squarely closed the gap.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A wins by demonstrating that mandating a human fast lane would create the very bottlenecks it aims to solve (induced demand flooding the queue) while diverting resources from more effective automated solutions. Side B argues well for the intrinsic value of human judgment, but struggles to overcome A's practical critiques regarding zero-sum budgets for small businesses and the reality that human retention agents are often the source of dark patterns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal brilliantly exposes the dilemma in B's model: if the fast lane has no friction, it gets flooded and fails; if it requires 'failed bot attempts' first, it preserves the exact chatbot trap the motion opposes.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively exposing the practical contradictions in Side B's proposed mandate. Side A effectively argued that a guaranteed fast human path either requires friction (which recreates the trap the proposition seeks to avoid) or creates a flooded queue where everyone presses zero, burying the complex cases Side B claims to protect. Side A's results versus method framing was consistently applied and highly persuasive, particularly on the safety clash where Side A demonstrated that automated, scalable containment is superior to human hold lines during emergency spikes. Side B's reliance on layers and triage failed to overcome Side A's sharp rebuttals regarding resource trade-offs and queueing realities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's reliance on airline-style triage. Side A pointed out that airline triage only works because of the friction of failed bot attempts; thus, codifying a no-friction fast path would flood the queue, while keeping the friction would violate the spirit of the anti-trap mandate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): A built a tighter, more consistent case: bots stay for easy cases, the mandate only bans the dead-end maze, and the human off-ramp targets the exact moment firms have both money and power at stake (billing authority, cancellation exits, safety triage). Critically, A explicitly pre-empted B's core cost/efficiency objection by clarifying the mandate is a narrow escalation right, not a universal human front door—and B never squarely engaged that clarification, instead repeating in Rebuttal 2 that the rule "forces every billing inquiry through a human lane," a mischaracterization A had already called out. A's answers to B's pressure questions (dedicated safety lane vs. B's invented "universal queue"; observability vs. proving deceptive intent) were more directly responsive than B's answers to A's questions, which mostly reasserted prior claims rather than resolving the tension. B's late "access vs. resolution" reframe was conceptually useful but arrived too late and undeveloped to overturn the accumulated damage, and B's closing was clipped for exceeding the word limit, costing it the final crystallization of its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's clarification in [A2.5]/[A4.3] that the mandate bans only dead-end loops, not tiered bot-first systems, directly undercut B's central efficiency/cost argument, which continued to assume a universal human front door even after this correction (most visible in [B4.1]).
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by consistently dismantling Side A's core arguments. Side B successfully argued that bots are intentionally designed to lack authority for refunds and cancellations, framing them as an 'exhaustion tax' on consumers. Side B also cleanly neutralized Side A's 'universal queue' and 'enforcement' arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4) brilliantly turned Side A's concession about bots lacking authority against them, proving that the 'efficiency' of bots is actually just a filter to exhaust consumers who cost the company money.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by grounding the debate in the practical reality of chatbot design, effectively distinguishing between a bot's ability to "look up" a charge versus its deliberate lack of authority to "reverse" or cancel one. This dismantled Side A's core efficiency argument. Side B also successfully reframed the cost debate by highlighting the existing "phantom charge tax" borne by consumers who give up, and easily swatted Side A's strawman arguments regarding universal safety queues. Side A relied on theoretical efficiency and outcome-based regulation but failed to overcome Side B's sharp procedural and practical rebuttals. Side A's closing was also slightly clipped, serving as a minor execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, specifically the argument that an "efficient" bot is only efficient when it wants to be because it is deliberately denied the authority to reverse charges, and the clarification that a dedicated safety lane satisfies the mandate without forcing panicked callers into a universal billing queue.
