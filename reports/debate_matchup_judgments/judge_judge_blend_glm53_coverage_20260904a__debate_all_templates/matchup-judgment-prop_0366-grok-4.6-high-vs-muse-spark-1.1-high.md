### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly and followed format well, but CON (A) built the more complete causal chain: theatrical revenue funds both future films and the accessibility tracks PRO wants, and simultaneous release risks cannibalizing that revenue via replacement ratios and pristine-file piracy. A also successfully turned PRO's own framing by pointing out that day-one premium home rental imposes its own barriers (broadband, cost, quiet space), so simultaneous release is not obviously more universally accessible, just a different exclusion. PRO (B) landed a genuinely strong point that COVID data was confounded and that "wait 30-45 days" still excludes people during the culturally decisive month, but never rebutted A's arithmetic mechanism with a comparable causal model—B's core response ("cannibalization is assumed math, not a law") is assertion rather than argument, and B never directly answered A's distinction between camrip leaks and pristine day-one masters in the final rounds. A's structural fix (accessible theaters plus a shortened window) also directly answers the "compromise is possible" question better than B's "both rooms can open" claim, which glosses over how the economics of that dual system would actually work. The Pressure Questions and answers were handled competently and honestly by both sides with proper A1/A2 formatting, but A's final rebuttal and closing left B's core economic rebuttal unanswered, giving A the cleaner path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response distinguishing a "shaky cam" leak from a pristine day-one studio master file (never squarely rebutted by B afterward) combined with A's turn that a $25 broadband-dependent home rental imposes its own accessibility barriers, undercutting B's central "first-class access" framing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): A won a competitive debate by giving the stronger comparative compromise: a short theatrical window followed by guaranteed robust home access. B compellingly established that theaters have intrinsic accessibility barriers and exposed A's occasional false-dilemma framing, but never adequately answered the central revenue mechanism. Merely noting that home sales generate revenue did not show they would replace multiple tickets, concessions, or losses from source-quality piracy. A's arithmetic was too categorical, but its funding and theater-survival risk remained live and outweighed B's case against a 30–45 day delay.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal most decisively distinguished ordinary camrips from an opening-night studio-quality file and pressed that home revenue may produce a smaller total pot. B answered that both rooms could remain open, but did not establish why cannibalization and piracy would not undermine that coexistence.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by establishing a firm economic mechanism (per-household rentals replacing per-head ticket sales, plus pristine day-one piracy) that Side A never adequately dismantled. Side B successfully flipped the access argument by demonstrating how Side A's model could shut down theaters and defund the very accessibility features Side A championed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's explanation of how day-one pristine source file leaks are functionally different from existing camrip leaks decisively shut down Side A's attempt to dismiss the piracy threat.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON (A) did the more decisive work in the exchange. B's opening structural claim—that theaters cannot by design offer rewind, independent captioning volume, or pause, so simultaneous release is the only equal access—was powerful, but A's key move was distinguishing "access as control" (comprehension) from "access as timing" (avoiding spoilers/memes), which undercut B's equation of any delay with exclusion. A's pressure question forced B to concede the motion only requires "more" simultaneous releases, not zero-wait absolutism, and A then exposed the resulting contradiction with B's earlier claim that "you cannot justify any wait" when rooms lack control [B4.2 vs B4.1]. B's closing ("they admitted it") reused A's honest concession that rooms can't become remotes, but never resolved the comprehension/timing distinction or the "more vs absolutist" tension A exposed, leaving that pressure point live and unanswered. A's economic and substitution arguments (switched vs new dollars, thinner mid-budget slate) were somewhat speculative but plausible and never fully dismantled by B, who leaned on an imperfect Spotify/concert analogy that A directly rebutted. Overall A gave the cleaner, more internally consistent path to a decision, while B's case, though emotionally resonant, carried an unresolved contradiction into the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 catching that B's answer to A's pressure question ("motion only asks for more, not zero-wait forever") directly contradicts B's earlier and later claims that "any wait" without full control is unjustified exclusion — a contradiction B's closing never resolved.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B narrowly won by showing that theaters structurally cannot provide the user control some disabled viewers need and that delayed access excludes them from the cultural moment. A effectively separated accessible files from simultaneous timing and exposed B’s weak treatment of substitution, but its claims of widespread theater closure and diminished film production remained largely asserted. A also repeatedly treated the proposal for more simultaneous releases as nearly universal, making its economic objection broader than the motion required.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure question about pause, rewind, and independent description forced A to concede that theaters cannot supply equal control. A sharply replied that this warrants a better, faster home file rather than day-and-date release, but never fully neutralized B’s argument that delayed access still denies equal participation when the film is culturally current.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by cleanly separating the functional need for an accessible file from the demand for opening-weekend timing, successfully arguing that while comprehension is an access right, immediate cultural participation (memes) is not. Side A failed to overcome Side B's economic substitution arguments regarding the survival of physical theaters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 masterfully trapped Side A's logic by pointing out that if any wait is unjustified due to lack of control, no window survives, and brilliantly summarized the clash: "A missed caption is a comprehension problem. A missed meme is not."
