### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): B offered the clearer limiting principle: serious, government-caused or controlled hazards involving knowledge or deliberate indifference. Its analogy to other open-textured constitutional standards substantially answered A's line-drawing objection. A correctly argued that concealment impedes statutory and constitutional remedies alike and identified a possible private injunction, which B unfairly reduced to post-harm damages. Still, A never showed that judicial review created intolerable consequences; one Pennsylvania decision over decades did not establish the predicted systemic flood. B therefore preserved both a plausible enforcement benefit and a manageable model of the right.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was its answer to the judicial-governance objection: courts can adjudicate extreme, state-caused dangers using familiar causation and culpability filters without setting comprehensive pollution standards. A's lone Pennsylvania example showed judicial intervention, but not the widespread regulatory collapse A needed to prove.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by making the decisive clash clearer and harder for CON to escape: when government knowingly poisons (Flint), existing statutes/torts either depend on the wrongdoer's discretion or arrive too late, so the child lacks a real floor. PRO's pressure questions and valve exchange forced that concession; CON's later Robinson Township turn was bluntly reframed as ordinary judicial review after decades without the predicted flood. CON's manageability and positive-duty worries remained live but secondary after PRO cabined the right to state-created extremes with existing filters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2/closing on the valve: EPA chose not to act and tort comes after poisoning, so CON's "claim on paper" is no enforceable right.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built a much stronger path to victory by anchoring the debate on the structural enforcement gap when the government itself is the polluter or regulator. Side A's framing of statutory protections as a "courtesy" rather than a "right" when dependent on the wrongdoer's discretion was highly persuasive and largely unanswered. Side A also brilliantly neutralized Side B's "flood of litigation" argument by forcing Side B to name examples during pressure questions, and then turning Side B's single example (Robinson Township) into proof that fifty years of constitutional environmental rights produces ordinary judicial output rather than a catastrophe. Side B's arguments were competent but failed to overcome the moral and structural force of Side A's case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing response to Side B's Robinson Township example, pointing out that a single well-known case over fifty years disproves the "flood" narrative and demonstrates that the right functions as ordinary judicial review.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won the central thin-or-thick dilemma. Side B alternated between a narrow survival floor that added little to existing law and a broader health standard requiring courts to choose thresholds, budgets, and infrastructure remedies. Side A also gave the stronger mechanism comparison: declarations do not themselves create enforcement capacity, while the same right can empower project-blocking litigation. Side B never convincingly resolved that tradeoff, and three clipped turns imposed an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it used Side B’s own answers to show the right expanding from an extreme livability floor into review of pollution above health guidance and judicial alteration of zoning. This revived the competence and tradeoff objections while exposing the unsupported claim that environmental litigation would reliably facilitate rather than obstruct clean infrastructure.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won by locking PRO into a live thin/thick dilemma that never resolved: the survival floor bought little beyond existing tort/life rights and could not do the anti-backsliding work claimed, while every thicker application (twice guidance, asthma thresholds, forced zoning for transmission) restored the polycentric slider and capacity/NIMBY problems. PRO's answers oscillated and analogies (patents, housing) failed to show the sword reliably swings for clean infrastructure; CON's capacity and "blocker files first" weighing stayed cleaner across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 + Rebuttal 2 sequence forcing PRO to shrink the right to "extinguish livability" then expand it again under pressure questions, exposing the slider and empty-decree problems.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B masterfully deployed a "thin vs. thick" dilemma that Side A never fully escaped. When Side A argued for a thin survival floor, Side B effectively demonstrated that existing statutes already cover this and that constitutional declarations do not solve the underlying fiscal and administrative capacity issues, using Flint as a prime example. When Side A expanded the right to compel green infrastructure and strike down local zoning, Side B exposed the lack of precedent and the polycentric nightmare of judges siting transmission lines. Side B's closing crystallization of the budgetary trade-offs and the "blocker files first" dynamic was decisive, logically rigorous, and rhetorically brilliant.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side A's claim that a court decree magically creates administrative capacity ("A declaration adds none of the three") and exposed the structural flaw in relocating complex budgetary trade-offs to a judge who only sees a single plaintiff and a single harm.
