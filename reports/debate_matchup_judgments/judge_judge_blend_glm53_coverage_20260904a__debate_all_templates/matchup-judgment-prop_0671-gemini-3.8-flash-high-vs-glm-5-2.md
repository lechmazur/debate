### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by preserving the crucial distinction between device-light and device-free. Side B compellingly explained how adaptive tools can help independent and vulnerable learners, but that only justified targeted use, which A’s policy expressly permits. B never showed why those benefits require devices to be the early-grade default, while leaving A’s handwriting, attention, and paper-comprehension advantages substantially unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest response was that B’s classroom-capacity argument establishes a case for selective adaptive interventions, not against a paper-first default. B’s image of twenty children abandoned to worksheets was forceful, but A answered with rotating stations and exposed the false choice between continuous teacher attention and universal device use.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by locking the decision rule to developmental foundations plus comparative outcomes, not feature inventories. A kept the motion intact as device-light rather than device-free, flipped equity toward language-rich adult attention over early screens, and repeatedly forced B to convert adaptive-app mechanisms into superior early literacy results. B’s best live route—the independent-work bottleneck when a teacher pulls a small group—was real and damaging, but it never fully established that default screens outperform paper-centered practice overall, so the transition-cliff and digital-divide claims stayed underweighted against A’s cleaner crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure-to-Rebuttal-2/closing line: features (pronunciation, adaptivity) are not outcomes, so B’s equity case collapses if apps mainly displace the human, language-rich attention affluent kids already get.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side A won by grounding the debate in the practical reality of early-grade classrooms. While Side B argued for the developmental benefits of paper, Side A effectively exposed the structural bottleneck of a paper-first default: when a teacher is working with a small group, the remaining students are left with static materials that cannot provide immediate corrective feedback. Side A's emphasis on adaptive tools for diverse learners (ELLs, dyslexic students) and the distinction between passive home screen time and academic digital literacy dismantled Side B's equity and transition arguments. Side B's reliance on "features vs. outcomes" was a solid rhetorical pivot, but it failed to overcome Side A's concrete demonstration of how paper-first policies disadvantage vulnerable students in real-world classroom constraints.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's "peer spelling checks" and "partner reading" alternatives by pointing out that six-year-olds cannot correct phonological mistakes, thereby proving that static paper leaves struggling readers in unassisted guesswork.
