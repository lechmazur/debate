### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A built a tighter causal story (proliferation/normalization/retaliation) anchored to a concrete, largely undisputed example (NSO/Pegasus reportedly reaching US officials), and used it to argue that export controls squeeze a genuine bottleneck of sophisticated vendors serving mid-tier abusers. The pivotal moment was B's concession in [B2.2] that the most dangerous adversaries already run in-house programs—A's pressure questions and closing exploited this concession cleanly, showing that CON's "leverage" argument only protects a market that supplies precisely the abusive mid-tier buyers generating blowback. B's answers (allies/unstable services need the channel, late visibility still enables sanctions) were reasonable but didn't fully neutralize the bind; B kept reasserting the diffusion/"code not missiles" mechanism without directly rebutting A's counter-analogy that other dual-use export regimes (encryption, dual-use tech) also face determined buyers yet still reduce net supply and cost. A's "crime scene, not control" framing landed harder than B's late rejoinder ("at least has suspects"), which borrowed A's own metaphor rather than independently refuting it. Both sides argued within the rules with plausible, ungrounded-in-fake-citation reasoning, but A's progression across rounds—question, concession, closing—was cleaner and harder to escape.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question Q1 [A3.1], forcing B to admit the export market's value rests only on arming mid-tier abusers once the "in-house programs" concession [B2.2] was on the table—an admission A then rode to victory through Rebuttal 2 and Closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by successfully narrowing the debate through Side A's concession that the most dangerous adversaries already maintain in-house programs. This admission allowed PRO to show that CON's arguments for visibility and leverage applied only to mid-tier buyers—precisely the actors generating blowback. PRO convincingly argued that strict export controls would reduce total harm by bottlenecking supply to these mid-tier actors, while leaving core government intelligence capabilities unaffected. CON's displacement argument remained live but was materially weakened by PRO's comparative framing and the NSO example, where visibility demonstrably failed to prevent abuse. PRO's rhetoric was sharper and its closing crystallization more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's exploitation of Side A's concession that the most dangerous adversaries already run in-house programs, which PRO used to collapse CON's leverage argument to the mid-tier blowback zone and render the case for preserving exports self-defeating.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO won the bottleneck vs displacement clash. By using CON's concession that dangerous adversaries already have in-house programs, PRO narrowed the trade to mid-tier buyers who can't build Pegasus-grade tools and drive blowback, showed visibility without recall failed for Americans, and kept intelligence options via gov-controlled tools. CON's shift-to-black-market response didn't explain why losing accountable sales to abusers hurts security.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2/A4 turn of [B2.2] - if top adversaries build their own, the export market only arms mid-tier abusers, so visibility and allied-interoperability defenses become arguments for arming the source of blowback, not for leverage over adversaries.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): The decisive clash was whether strict export controls actually shrink the global spyware ecosystem or merely relocate it to unregulated jurisdictions. CON built a coherent economic mechanism (constant authoritarian demand, mobile talent, chokepoints that only bind Western-oriented firms) and reinforced it with a plausible crypto-export-control analogy, then sharpened it in rebuttal by noting that spyware maintenance/support requires people, not fixed infrastructure ("those people can board a plane"), directly undercutting PRO's key distinction. CON also successfully reframed the burden of proof, forcing PRO to defend a speculative causal chain against a "certain, mechanical" cost. PRO's opening was well organized (blowback, abuse, gray-market feeding adversaries) and it correctly conceded the intelligence tradeoff as a strategic move, but its rebuttals relied on hand-wavy assertions about chokepoints (dollars, cloud, visas) that CON repeatedly showed only apply to firms still oriented toward Western markets—an objection PRO never squarely resolved. Under pressure, PRO admitted there is "no magic threshold" for when strictness bites, which CON exploited effectively ("the threshold doesn't exist—just a default flip"). CON's closing crystallized the asymmetry cleanly: certain, mechanical losses versus a benefit chain with every link contested. PRO's closing repeated the ecosystem-shrinking theory without new mechanism to answer the relocation problem. B's opening was clipped for length, a real execution cost, but the substantive argument had already landed before the cut.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that "the exploit is knowledge, not hardware... those people can board a plane" directly dismantled PRO's central claim that regulation bites because spyware needs ongoing maintenance/support tied to Western infrastructure, and PRO never adequately recovered from this in the closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON won by exposing that PRO never met the burden of showing strict regulation would actually reduce global spyware supply rather than simply relocate it offshore. CON's pressure questions and second rebuttal sharply highlighted a paradox in PRO's case: strict rules drive vendors beyond the reach of the audits, sanctions, and cloud/financial chokepoints PRO relied on to claim leverage. CON's "business plan, not a sanction" framing and evidence-asymmetry critique (documented abuse vs invisible lost collection) gave a cleaner comparative reason to reject the proposition as speculative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which collapsed PRO's ecosystem-shrinking claim by showing that relocating vendors converts known, leverageable suppliers into invisible, unleverageable ones, and that higher prices and zero compliance overhead offshore enrich the illicit market rather than shrink it.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): PRO won the lifecycle vs relocation clash. CON's core hydraulic claim - market simply moves - was met by A's specific mechanism that spyware needs updates, hosting, payments, and talent, so strict "deny unless verified" degrades tools and raises costs even offshore. CON conceded offshore makes abuse invisible rather than safer and never showed why certain visibility loss outweighs documented blowback. Clipped B opening also hurt execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2/A4 turning relocation into weakening: fewer maintained copies, fragmented support, loss of legal cover reduces blowback more than it costs visibility, directly undercutting B1.2/B2.3.
