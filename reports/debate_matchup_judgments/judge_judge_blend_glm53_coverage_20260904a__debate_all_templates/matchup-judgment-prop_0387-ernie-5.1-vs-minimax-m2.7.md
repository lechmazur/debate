### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): PRO built a more durable case across the debate and never lost control of the central framing: voluntary unions and industry-specific institutions only protect players who can already organize, leaving the most vulnerable—minors, unaffiliated players, workers in small organizations—with no recourse. CON had genuine counter-arguments (enforcement gaps don't disappear by adding rules, the FIFA model is itself broken, consolidation risk) but never escaped the core problem PRO hammered consistently. CON's "industry-specific institutions" alternative repeatedly raised the question of who enforces them, and CON's own answer in Rebuttal 2—specialized mediators with collective bargaining rights—is itself a regulated structure, blunting the distinction from PRO's proposal. PRO answered both pressure questions in proper A1/A2 format and pivoted them into strengths: national law plus multilateral coordination addresses jurisdiction; tiered compliance still sets a floor even for small teams. CON's A1 produced the debate's sharpest individual moment—"if Brazilian law already exists and doesn't protect that minor, adding more rules doesn't fix an enforcement gap"—but PRO's closing crystallized why it doesn't defeat the motion: esports-specific regulation fills a gap general labor law doesn't cover by design. Two clipped speeches (Opening and Closing) are real execution costs for CON; the Closing in particular was cut at a decisive moment. PRO's closing was concise and on-point. The margin is clear but not dominant because CON's enforcement-gap argument and FIFA critique were genuine, not just noise.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q1 (the São Paulo minor) was the decisive exchange. CON had asked which existing body could mandate cross-border compliance. PRO answered by reframing: no single body needs to, because national authorities already enforce within their borders and the ILO model shows how to coordinate minimum standards across jurisdictions. More importantly, PRO pivoted to show that CON's voluntary alternative is worst precisely where enforcement is hardest—small organizations with no union presence—turning CON's enforcement objection back against their own case.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate tied to the proposition: stronger labor protections are needed because the most vulnerable players cannot bargain for themselves. Side A raised real concerns about costs, jurisdiction, and bureaucratic overreach, but its alternative of unions, mediators, salary floors, and collective bargaining often sounded like stronger labor protections under another name. Side B most effectively pressed that voluntary or industry-led systems protect only players with leverage and leave minors and fringe players exposed. Side A never fully answered who gives its mediators authority or how its model protects the 16-year-old with no union access. Side A also suffered some execution cost from clipped turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s pressure and second rebuttal were decisive: it forced Side A to explain how voluntary industry institutions would protect non-unionized minors and then argued that only mandatory floors apply to actors with no bargaining power.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): PRO wins by successfully anchoring the debate on the protection of vulnerable players (minors, non-unionized rookies) who lack the bargaining power required for CON's union-based alternative. PRO effectively navigated the jurisdiction clash by clarifying that national labor authorities, not a mythical global bureaucracy, would enforce standards, and used tiered compliance to mitigate CON's small-team concerns. CON landed strong points on regulatory adaptability and correctly noted that adding rules does not automatically fix enforcement gaps, but their alternative ultimately relied on the same legal recognition and court enforcement they criticized in PRO's model. PRO's closing crystallized the core stakes more cleanly, and CON's clipped opening and closing incurred a slight execution cost per the protocol.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 directly answered CON's pressure on jurisdiction and tiered compliance by clarifying that national authorities enforce locally and that tiered systems still mandate a substantive floor. This effectively neutralized CON's "loophole" and "global bureaucracy" attacks while turning the focus back to CON's lack of enforcement mechanisms for non-unionized minors, securing PRO's comparative advantage on the motion's core harms.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger cumulative case. Its structural-coercion argument—that industry-wide identical non-compete terms, information asymmetry, and monopolistic tournament access undermine genuine voluntariness—was never squarely neutralized. CON's market-discipline defense required orgs to actually self-correct, yet CON could not show this had happened; its own rebuttal conceded orgs do not collapse, only "lose players," which begs the question of whether replacement talent supply makes discipline illusory. The Hollywood comparison flip in B2.4 was the debate's sharpest single exchange: CON introduced it to normalize punitive clauses, PRO pivoted it into proof that entertainment labor only improved after workers organized—directly undermining CON's "market sorts it out" thesis. PRO's crystallization in B4.6–B4.7 ("market discipline is a hope, not a system") was the clearest weighing sentence in the debate and went unanswered in CON's closing. CON's strongest live argument—compliance costs pricing out small orgs and young talent—was partially answered by PRO's point that the status quo already prices out players through burnout and career-ending non-competes. CON's closing mostly recycled earlier arguments without adapting to that evolved clash, and its repeated "top players have six-figure leverage" framing never recovered from PRO's outlier rejoinder. Both sides incurred clipping penalties, roughly canceling out. Overall, PRO's argument chain stayed internally coherent and progressively tightened; CON's rested on a market-discipline premise that accumulated more holes as the debate progressed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Hollywood comparison flip [B2.4]: CON introduced entertainment-industry non-compete norms to normalize esports contract terms; PRO turned this into evidence for the proposition by noting that Hollywood's "standard" terms only became tolerable after SAG-AFTRA and the Writers Guild organized—proving that the industries that fought for protections got them, while esports, which hasn't, remains unprotected. This rebuttal materially damaged CON's normalization argument and was never cleanly answered.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side B won by making the central clash clearer: esports players, especially minors and non-stars, lack meaningful bargaining power, so market consent and reputation are inadequate safeguards. Side A had a plausible cost and consolidation concern, but relied heavily on top-player outliers, voluntary-signing logic, and market discipline without showing that those mechanisms actually protect vulnerable players. Side B's responses on non-competes, information asymmetry, and structural coercion were more persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was against market discipline: it argued that persistent punitive terms, sponsor silence, and continued recruitment by abusive organizations show reputation does not correct the abuse, directly undercutting Side A's main alternative to labor protections.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): PRO wins by decisively dismantling CON's core market-discipline mechanism. When pressed to name an organization that collapsed from labor abuse, CON conceded the point and pivoted to a weaker talent-flight standard that PRO had already neutralized by highlighting industry-wide standardization of non-competes and sponsor indifference to contract ethics. PRO's framing of structural coercion versus meaningful consent, combined with the sharp reversal of CON's Hollywood comparison, created a cleaner comparative path. CON's compliance-cost argument was competent but ultimately outweighed by PRO's closing risk-shift analysis and tighter execution under pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 directly answered CON's pressure questions by turning the persistence of non-compete clauses into proof of market failure, and reframed parental consent and housing as dependency-creating rather than protective. This crystallized the clash into system versus hope and left CON's market-discipline case functionally dead.
