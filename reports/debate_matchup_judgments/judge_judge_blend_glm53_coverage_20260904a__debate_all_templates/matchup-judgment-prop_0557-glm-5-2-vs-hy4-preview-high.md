### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON (A) more successfully exploited a load-bearing tension in PRO's argument. PRO's central claim—that a test score is "independent of what her family could buy" and the "one measure" that "surfaces talent"—collided with PRO's own concession that "nothing is wealth-neutral" and that tests are shaped by coaching and retakes. CON repeatedly forced this contradiction into view (turning "ladder" into "toll," turning the "voice" argument into proof that scores are optional and marginal, and closing with the vivid "floor is a trapdoor" line), and PRO's closing never squarely reconciled the tension, instead re-asserting the disputed premise. PRO's strongest original move—the self-selection/withholding argument in Rebuttal 2—was answered adequately by CON's point that test-optional already gives students the choice to submit a helpful score without penalizing those with unhelpful ones. CON's proposed alternative (contextual data, course-taking patterns) is imperfect but was defended more consistently than PRO's insistence that tests are a family-wealth-proof capability signal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's answer in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.2), turning PRO's "voice" and "ladder" framing into evidence that scores are marginal and purchasable, combined with the closing line "the wealthy 3.7 student can buy a higher number," most directly exposed the unresolved contradiction in PRO's case between "nothing is wealth-neutral" and "the test is independent of what family could buy."
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that test-optional admissions preserve A's claimed benefit—a strong score surfacing hidden talent—without forcing every applicant to bear the costs and risks of testing. A persuasively established that transcripts vary and contextual review can be difficult at scale, but never adequately proved that these problems require a mandate rather than optional submission, better advising, and scalable context. B also weighed the remaining tradeoff effectively against public universities' access and mobility mission.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that a helpful score can already be submitted under test-optional policy decisively weakened A's “ladder” and “voice” framing. A's answer—that disadvantaged students may mistakenly withhold useful scores—was plausible but did not establish why mandatory testing is preferable to advising them about submission.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO won a competitive debate by making the live choice easier: at public-university scale, CON’s “scalable context” alternative collapses exactly where profiles/rank vanish (under-resourced schools), leaving transcripts that encode school offerings rather than comparable student readiness. PRO flipped CON’s two-3.7s example into a ladder case, answered pressure cleanly (number-plus-context vs reconstructing GPA from scratch), and crystallized the asymmetry in closing. CON’s mission/deterrence and “voice without mandate” lines stayed live and sharp, especially on purchasable prep, but never operationalized a cross-school anchor once common tools disappear, so PRO’s reinstatement path survived as the clearer decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 A1/A2: contextualizing one score is tractable; rebuilding what a 3.7 means without profiles is not—and tests can show ability when AP access is missing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON (A) landed the decisive structural blow: PRO's defense of reinstating tests depended repeatedly on infrastructure and policy reforms (free in-school testing, automatic waivers, publicly funded prep, capped bands) that the motion itself does not guarantee, since a university "reinstating a requirement" does not thereby acquire the power or obligation to fund and administer those reforms. A pressed this in Rebuttal 1, sharpened it in Pressure Questions, and closed the loop devastatingly in the final speech: those same safeguards (bands, published weight, pairing with records) are equally available under test-optional policy, so the mandate's unique contribution is only the prep-gap burden, not any unique fairness gain. B never squarely answered why reinstatement specifically compels these reforms rather than merely being compatible with them, instead repeating "the mandate forces the university to supply testing for all" as an assertion. A also successfully inverted B's core "auditability/accountability" argument by noting that a test's opacity (proprietary scoring, prep-sensitivity) is outside university control, whereas contextual GPA review is at least improvable by policy the university does control — turning B's own transparency framing against it. B's opening was strong and the "privatized vs. universalized gaming" framing was clever, but it was not adequately defended once A exposed the reform-dependency problem, and B's closing recycled the same framing without resolving it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point that PRO's safeguards (free testing, waivers, publicly funded prep) are "reforms to testing infrastructure, not powers a public university controls by reinstating a requirement," extended through Pressure Questions and crystallized in A's closing ("those safeguards are available under test-optional too... the requirement itself adds nothing except the mandate") was the single most damaging and unanswered exchange of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by isolating what the requirement uniquely changes. Side A established real benefits from comparability, prediction, and reduced submission games, but repeatedly relied on added safeguards such as universal administration, public preparation, waivers, and limited score weighting. Side B persuasively argued that many safeguards are also available under test-optional admissions, while mandatory submission uniquely makes unequal test preparation and access unavoidable. Side A also never fully justified treating every required score as transparent evidence rather than another income-sensitive input.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s closing response was decisive: publishing bands, capping score weight, and contextualizing records do not require mandatory testing, whereas the mandate uniquely removes the escape hatch for strong students lacking test preparation. This directly narrowed the debate to the incremental effect of reinstatement rather than the abstract usefulness of scores.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won by converting the live clash into a clean motion burden: PRO’s best defenses (free admin, automatic waivers, funded prep, score bands rather than hard cutoffs) are attached reforms, not effects of reinstating the requirement as it exists. PRO kept a strong auditability/gaming frame and answered A1/A2 structure, but never squared how a mandate uniquely improves fairness once those external conditions are stripped away. CON’s closings crystallized that those same safeguards can exist under test-optional, while the mandate uniquely universalizes the prep gap—making the comparative path easier to decide for A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s repeated motion-scope rebuttal (esp. A2.1/A4.4 into A5.2): PRO’s protections are post-hoc reforms, not powers the requirement itself grants; the mandate’s distinctive addition is unavoidable prep-gap filtering.
