Furthermore, Side A brilliantly turned the supermajority override into a lose-lose proposition: if the veto is truly binding, the commissioner wields unaccountable political power; if it is overridable, Side B's own premise of inherent legislative myopia dictates that short-termist majorities will simply logroll to bypass the veto. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): A built a sustained, distinctive throughline — a veto is structurally subtractive and cannot itself create the long-horizon investment that both sides agree democracies chronically underproduce. This point was raised in the opening, sharpened in rebuttals (the NIMBY-ally claim was cleanly dismantled by noting a veto can kill a passed bill but cannot force a blocked one through), and never fully neutralized: B's answer that "the threat of veto forces redesign" was asserted via analogy (ECHR) rather than demonstrated as a mechanism for generating missing investment. A also pressed a real dilemma on the override mechanism (toothless vs. gridlock-inducing) that B never directly answered. B's central analogy to courts and central banks was rhetorically effective and forced A to articulate a real distinction (codified text, living plaintiffs, narrow quantifiable mandates vs. contested, unbounded future-welfare tradeoffs), but B's closing rebuttal to that distinction ("only historical familiarity") did not substantively rebut it. B also suffered two format/execution costs from being clipped for length in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing, cutting off its final crystallization. Both pressure-question rounds were sharp and well-answered with proper A1/A2 formatting, but A's answers (naming non-binding alternatives like advisory climate bodies) directly rebutted B's "status quo defense" charge, while B's answers, though clever, relied on analogy more than mechanism. Overall A provided the cleaner, more sustained comparative case and suffered no execution penalties.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal on the NIMBY-ally claim (A2.3–A2.4) and the "veto cannot build" framing sustained into A4.3, which exposed that B's own diagnosis (chronic underinvestment) is never actually solved by a purely subtractive institutional tool — a point B never fully closed off.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): CON won the core trade-off: PRO conceded the disease is chronic underinvestment / non-production of long-horizon projects [B4.3], but its cure is a strictly subtractive veto that cannot build. CON's distinction between rights adjudication (codified text, precedent, living plaintiffs) and open-ended fiscal/climate balancing held up, while PRO's court/central-bank analogy never survived A2.1/A4.2. PRO's redesign argument and NIMBY-as-ally claim [B2.6] was sharply reversed by A2.3 that a veto can kill a fast-tracked rail or housing bill but cannot force a shovel when parliament stalls. CON also answered pressure with concrete non-veto alternatives (scoring, UK Climate Change Committee, capital trusts) while PRO's replies were clipped for length, an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1/2 on the veto being subtraction-only — it can block the rare bold project but cannot compel action when short-termism produces inaction — which directly neutralized PRO's B2.6 claim that a future commissioner would help build housing/infrastructure.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by decisively proving that a veto is a strictly subtractive tool that cannot solve the chronic underinvestment PRO identified, and by effectively distinguishing a future-generations commissioner from constitutional courts. Side B's court analogy was their strongest point, but Side A's rebuttal regarding the difference between rights adjudication and macroeconomic trade-offs was highly persuasive. Side A's rhetorical framing ("you cannot build the future with a veto") crystallized the clash perfectly, while Side B struggled to overcome the mechanical limitations of a veto in addressing state capacity and infrastructure development.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, where they exposed the core contradiction in PRO's case: PRO diagnosed chronic underinvestment as the primary crisis, yet prescribed a strictly subtractive veto that can only block the rare long-horizon projects that actually pass, without possessing any mechanism to compel or build new investment.
