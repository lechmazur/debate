### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Both sides argued competently with grounded, plausible examples and no fake citations, but PRO (B) built a cleaner, more durable central frame—permanent knowledge/language death versus corrigible friction—and sustained it under pressure. B's rebuttals repeatedly turned CON's own evidence against it (Polynesian navigation as proof of gatekeeping, not rescue; Hebrew/Welsh/Catalan as proof survival requires extraordinary resistance; the Jakarta teenager as the mechanism of domain loss rather than a counterexample). B's pressure questions were sharper and exposed a real tension in CON's position (why must the "neutral standard" be English specifically, and doesn't "network effects" just describe monopoly entrenchmentrather than justify it), and B's answers to CON's own questions (A1/A2 format observed by both sides) reframed "widening access" as functionally equivalent to reducing dominance, which CON never fully dislodged. CON (A) mounted a genuinely strong late move—that PRO's own remedies retain English as the connective layer, suggesting the harm is implementation unfairness rather than dominance itself—but this came mostly in the closing and was only partially pre-empted by PRO, leaving it live but not decisive enough to overturn PRO's more consistently defended asymmetry framing across the whole debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 turn of A's own examples (Polynesian navigation, Indigenous fire management, the Jakarta teenager) from evidence of English's rescuing bridge into evidence of English's gatekeeping power was the most damaging single exchange, forcing CON to spend its next speech defending coexistence cases (Hebrew/Welsh/Catalan) that B then used in Pressure Questions to show survival requires extraordinary resistance to dominance, not proof of harmless contact.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by consistently separating a shared second language from English dominance. Side A established substantial coordination and visibility benefits, but often defended the need for a common interface rather than proving that concentrated English-language gatekeeping is necessary for those benefits. Side B more effectively linked dominance to domain loss, unequal admission costs, and epistemic filtering, then weighed these irreversible harms against connectivity that could persist through multilingual publication and translation. Side A exposed real uncertainty about the scalability of that alternative, keeping the debate competitive, but did not fully neutralize B’s distinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response to the Jakarta example was the sharpest exchange: speaking Javanese at home does not answer the claim that languages die as prestigious domains such as science, law, and code move elsewhere. This directly turned Side A’s coexistence example into support for B’s domain-loss mechanism.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): CON built the cleaner comparative path: dominance creates real frictions, but the live alternative is longer chains and starved regional routes rather than flourishing pluralism, so non-anglophone knowledge is not safer without the shared channel. PRO landed sharper turns on monopoly vs bridge and permanence, yet never established a viable counterfactual that outweighs isolation risk; CON's access-reform framing and flipped permanence weighing (obscurity kills faster) resolved the motion for B.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Both sides built serious, well-constructed cases, but CON exploited the comparative structure of the motion more effectively. From the opening, CON insisted that "more harm than good" requires a real comparator, and repeatedly forced PRO to specify what a lower-dominance world would look like. PRO's eventual answer in Rebuttal 2 (translated abstracts, multilingual journals, regional networks) was directly undercut by CON's observation that these mechanisms still coordinate through English — leaving PRO's alternative-world claim thin. PRO's strongest and most original line — distinguishing circulation/reach from authority/certification ("decorative rather than decisive") — was a genuine pressure point, but CON's rebuttal ("authority follows circulation," with Bollywood, K-pop institutes, and untranslated Chinese fantasy vocabulary entering global discourse) gave a plausible, concrete counter that PRO's closing did not fully dislodge, mostly reasserting the same distinction rather than answering the counterexamples. CON's pressure question about naming a realistic alternative was the most consequential exchange of the debate, and CON's follow-up exploited PRO's answer cleanly. PRO's own pressure questions were sharp and CON answered them adequately (A1/A2 format followed by both sides), but the net effect still favored CON's tighter comparative framing and more concrete rebuttal examples over PRO's more abstract "authority" claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's proposed alternative-world (translated abstracts, multilingual journals) — pointing out these mechanisms still coordinate through English as the pivot — was the most decisive moment, neutralizing PRO's answer to the comparative-burden pressure question.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by making the net comparison easier to resolve: English imposes real linguistic and status costs, but also enables access, collaboration, mobility, and cultural circulation at enormous scale. Side B persuasively distinguished circulation from authority and offered a reduced-dominance alternative, yet never sufficiently established why diminished interpretive control outweighs the conceded gains. Side A sometimes overstated the choice as English or isolation, but its comparative weighing remained stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest response reframed “conditional inclusion” as preferable to prior exclusion: the translation tax matters, but must be weighed against the authority, collaborators, and audiences that a shared language makes available. Side B’s content-versus-power distinction damaged some of A’s cultural examples, but did not fully defeat that net-benefit comparison.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B won by making the comparative burden decisive and keeping it live: "more harm than good" requires a realistic alternative, which A never supplied cleanly beyond English-plus-supplements that still pivot on English. A's epistemic-authority and diglossia case stayed sharp and consistent, but B's historical weighing, open-bridge access gains, shrinking costs, and "authority follows circulation" replies neutralized net-harm more effectively across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 reply that A's "reduced-dominance" counterfactual still runs on English as pivot, turning A's answer into a concession that isolation, not equality, is the true alternative.
