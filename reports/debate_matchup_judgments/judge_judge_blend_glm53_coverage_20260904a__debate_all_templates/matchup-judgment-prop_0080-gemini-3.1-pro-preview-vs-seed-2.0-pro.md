### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.3** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): B wins by exposing an unanswerable contradiction: PRO admits small landlords cannot replace corporate inventory yet claims rental supply stays intact. PRO's late vacancy claim is unsupported and evaded. B maintains sharper focus on the renter displacement tradeoff across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3/B4.4 directly neutralizes A's small-landlord fallback and forces the core tradeoff into the open.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by demonstrating that with housing stock fixed, restricting institutional buyers merely reshuffles scarcity rather than creating affordability. CON successfully framed the policy as picking wealthier mortgage-ready buyers over vulnerable renters who need single-family lease access, and showed that PRO's attempts to escape this zero-sum math relied on unsupported precise claims (10–15% vacancy rates) and a segmentation theory that implicitly conceded rental supply in desirable neighborhoods would shrink. PRO's late brittle precision and failure to square small landlords' capital constraints with replacement rental supply proved decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal dismantling PRO's "above-market rent" claim with basic market-clearing logic and exposing that PRO's admission about small landlords lacking capital reserves undermined the promise of replacement rental supply.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B wins by exposing a structural contradiction in Side A's mechanism and maintaining a more economically grounded narrative. Side A argued that small local landlords would replace institutional rental supply, but also conceded they lack the cash reserves to compete for homes. Side B effectively turned this admission to prove that small landlords cannot mathematically replace the lost inventory, meaning the policy inevitably shrinks the rental pool and harms credit-constrained families. Side B also correctly challenged Side A's unsupported 10-15% vacancy claim as economically implausible for cash-flow-dependent firms. Side B's consistent framing of the fixed-supply tradeoff between mortgage-privileged buyers and vulnerable renters provided a cleaner, more logically sound path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.4] weaponized Side A's admission that mom-and-pop landlords "lack massive cash reserves" [A4.3]. By demonstrating that if small landlords cannot outbid buyers, they also cannot purchase the homes to rent them out, Side B collapsed Side A's core mechanism for protecting renters and proved the restriction would actively choke rental supply.
