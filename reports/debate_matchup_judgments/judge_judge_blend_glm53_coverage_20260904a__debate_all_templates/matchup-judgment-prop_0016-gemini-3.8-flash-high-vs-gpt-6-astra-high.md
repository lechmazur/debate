### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-reasoned cases without resorting to fake precision. CON's most original move was distinguishing "necessary input" (housing) from "governing priority" (public order) and using the wheelchair-entrance example to argue cities have a nondelegable, immediate duty regardless of housing supply. But PRO exploited a genuine structural tension: CON's own framework requires "accessible emergency accommodation" and treats housing as an "operational necessity" for humane enforcement. PRO pressed this in Rebuttal 1 and drove it home in the Pressure Questions and Rebuttal 2 ("your entire framework is parasitical on our premise"), forcing CON into a concession (A1 in Reb2: enforcement is authorized once a placement exists) that CON then had to spend its final speeches reinterpreting rather than freely advancing new ground. CON's counter—that this concession shows public order requires an "independent decision" even when housing is available—is a fair rejoinder but doesn't fully restore primacy to policing, since PRO's model already welcomed enforcement as compatible with housing-led governance. PRO's closing crystallized the clash more decisively ("primacy belongs to the foundation, not the enforcement tool") than CON's closing, which mostly reasserted its distinction without fully dissolving the contradiction. Both sides answered pressure questions with proper A1/A2 formatting, so no execution penalty either way. On balance, PRO's tighter logical trap and slightly more memorable framing edge out CON's sophisticated but ultimately concessive position.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 (and its sharpening in Rebuttal 2, "your entire framework is parasitical on our premise") exposing that CON's own enforcement model presupposes housing/accommodation as a prerequisite, which CON could only partially rebut by distinguishing "necessary input" from "governing priority."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won because it exposed a structural dependency in Side B's case: CON acknowledged that displacement without a destination is failed policy and called accessible accommodation an "operational necessity" for humane enforcement. Side A leveraged these concessions to show that a genuinely order-led approach collapses into a housing-and-services approach in order to avoid cruelty, making public order a secondary enforcement tool rather than the primary frame. Side B offered a sharp conceptual distinction between governing priority and necessary resources, and scored with the wheelchair-access pressure question, but Side A absorbed that concession without abandoning its core thesis—arguing that narrow access enforcement is compatible with a housing-primary framework. By the final speeches, Side A had the cleaner, more durable path to victory: you cannot arrest a housing shortage out of existence, and CON's humane model could not function without first adopting PRO's infrastructure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (Turn 7), which argued that CON's own concessions made humane enforcement parasitical on a housing-and-services infrastructure already being in place, thereby undermining the claim that public order could be genuinely primary.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A built a highly sophisticated and logically rigorous case, successfully distinguishing between a necessary resource (housing) and a governing priority (public order).
