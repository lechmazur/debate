CON's best response ("if the concern is arousal, restrict all high-arousal gambling ads uniformly") is a reasonable proportionality objection, but it does not defeat the motion, which only asks for differential treatment, not an exclusive ban. PRO also gives concrete policy specifics when pressed in A1 (pre-watershed blackouts, in-play odds bans during play, tighter sponsorship disclosure) and correctly separates integrity monitoring funding from ad revenue when CON conflates them. CON's repeated Champions League outlier argument does not hold for the motion as a whole, since weekly major-league broadcasts also reach enormous audiences. PRO handles format correctly (A1/A2 labelling, pointed Q1/Q2) and its closing crystallises the live clash rather than widening it. The debate is close because CON lands real proportionality pressure, but PRO's coupling mechanism survives the exchange and provides a cleaner path to the motion.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins by a clear but competitive margin. The decisive surviving claims are: (1) the unique-context argument — event-specific ads tied to live emotional investment are meaningfully different from other gambling ads, and CON's "proves too much" counter is partially circular since PRO correctly distinguishes sports betting from generic live-event advertising; and (2) the children's normalization argument — live weekend afternoon sports sits outside the adult-only time windows that already govern other gambling ads, and CON never explains why watershed or content-classification rules would cover Saturday afternoon family viewing. CON's "existing tools" answer assumes tools designed for evening broadcasts apply to midday sports, which PRO correctly contests and CON never resolves. CON's sharpest asset was the migration concession — PRO acknowledged Australia's broadcast restrictions pushed bettors toward in-play and offshore channels where per-user harm is higher, and PRO's "mass entry point / net fewer new gamblers" response is speculative rather than demonstrated. CON correctly labels it as speculation and scores real points there. However, migration does not defeat the motion — it identifies a gap requiring parallel in-play rules, not a reason to leave the broadcast harm unaddressed. PRO's answer that restrictions can be a complement (not a replacement) for broader regulation survives the exchange. CON performed stronger round-by-round rebuttal work, but PRO's structural case on the proposition as written — that live sports broadcasts create a specific dual harm (adult impulsivity, child normalization) absent in other gambling ad contexts — was never fully neutralized, and that structural argument carries the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 catch of the migration concession: PRO had admitted in-play betting carries higher problem-gambling rates, and CON immediately turned this into "the policy PRO defends made gambling more dangerous, not less." This was the sharpest individual exchange of the debate, forcing PRO to spend Rebuttal 2 defending a net-population-effect theory that both sides acknowledged was speculative rather than documented.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B wins by aggressively leveraging Side A's own evidence regarding Australia's ad restrictions to show that the policy causes a migration to more dangerous in-play and offshore platforms. Side A is forced to concede the increased per-user harm and rely on a speculative claim about reduced overall volume, while suggesting parallel regulations that undermine their stance. Side B also managed word limits flawlessly, whereas Side A suffered from clipping in three major speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal, which brilliantly framed Side A's defense of the Australia migration as a damaging concession that the policy pushes users to platforms with higher rates of problem gambling.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO wins by establishing a cleaner weighing mechanism on the central clash: migration risk versus mass recruitment. CON effectively pressured PRO on the Australia migration evidence, arguing the policy pushes users to more dangerous in-play/offshore channels. PRO directly answered in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing by introducing a volume-versus-intensity tradeoff: cutting off the broadcast channel removes a mass entry point that recruits casual, first-time bettors, which reduces total new problem gamblers even if a smaller, already-engaged pool migrates to riskier platforms. This net-harm framework, combined with PRO's stronger defense of context-specific regulation (family viewing slots bypassing watersheds, real-time event exploitation impairing deliberation), gave PRO a more complete and actionable path to victory. CON's "speculative math" critique was sharp but did not neutralize PRO's causal story, and CON's alternative of relying on existing/improving tools felt less responsive to the unique contextual harms the motion targets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over the Australia migration evidence. CON weaponized PRO's concession that in-play betting carries higher problem-gambling rates to argue the policy backfires. PRO effectively parried by introducing a clear weighing mechanism (cutting off the mass broadcast entry point reduces total new problem gamblers, outweighing the smaller migrated pool), which crystallized the policy tradeoff and gave PRO the decisive comparative edge on net harm reduction.
