### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued vividly and stayed disciplined on evidence, but PRO (Side B) kept tighter fidelity to the motion's actual distinction between occasional backup and routine default, and its serve-and-return/contingency mechanism gave a clean, consistently applied metric. CON (Side A) built a compelling realistic scenario and the clever "divisibility of labor" move (device narrates, parent connects), but under pressure this collapsed into an unresolved tension: PRO's closing dilemma—that a parent present enough to do the "irreplaceable" emotional connecting is present enough to read, while a parent too depleted to read is probably too depleted for that emotional labor too—was never squarely answered. CON's best response (narration-as-performance is costlier than holding-and-murmuring) was asserted rather than demonstrated and didn't fully engage ordinary quiet reading. PRO also handled the "night four becomes night fifty" pressure by offering an actual line (device for specific nightly incapacity, human default resumes the next ordinary night), whereas CON's answer mostly reasserted that strain is constant without a clear boundary, which PRO exploited effectively in closing. This makes PRO's route to victory the more resolved one at the end of the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3-B4.4) dilemma—if the parent can do the harder relational work (connecting the story to the child's day, noticing fear), narration isn't the costly part, so the device is redundant; if she can't, the device's added words don't compensate for the missing contingent response—was the most damaging unanswered pressure point, directly undercutting CON's redundancy/hybrid model that the rest of the debate depended on.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A won by locking the motion to “routine default,” not rare strain, and by making contingency/serve-and-return the decisive metric. B’s best case—chronic exhaustion and a calm hybrid helper—was vivid and honest, but A repeatedly forced the live comparison: if a parent can still hold, listen, answer “why,” and connect the sad rabbit, narration is not the costly core; if not, machine-led nights thin the very response that builds language and security. B never fully escaped defending continuity of one-way narration against A’s clearer developmental good, nor the slide from allowed backup into standing machine-led routine. Pressure answers and the closing crystallize that cleaner path for A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s hybrid dilemma (esp. A4.3/A5.3 answering B’s energy split): a parent capable of the contingent human work B prizes can read; a parent too depleted for that leaves the essential loop missing—so routine AI lead is either redundant or thinned attachment, not enrichment by design.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully defended that early language and bedtime security require contingent human serve-and-return interaction, which routine AI narration cannot provide. CON's hybrid model—where AI handles narration while parents provide responsive care—was undermined by PRO's sharp dilemma showing that a parent depleted enough to need AI narration is unlikely to sustain the harder contingent response work, making the device either redundant or a contributor to passive caregiving. PRO maintained a clear distinction between allowed emergency backup and harmful routine default, and their framework better withstood the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO Rebuttal 2's 'redundant or passive' dilemma, which exposed that CON's hybrid model cannot reliably preserve the contingent responsiveness that CON claims is protected.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases without invented sourcing, but PRO (B) constructed a sharper logical trap and pressed it more effectively. PRO's core mechanism—that routine handover trains children to orient toward the tireless device rather than the tired parent, because "rituals run on defaults"—stayed consistent and was reinforced each round. The decisive moment was PRO's Q1 in Pressure Questions, which forced CON into an internal contradiction: CON's "safe" version of routine use requires an energetically engaged, pausing, cuddling parent every night, yet CON's own motivating example was an exhausted parent who cannot sustain that effort. PRO's Rebuttal 2 named this tension explicitly ("CON's defense needs both versions at once"), and CON's answer, while gesturing toward "even the worst case beats nothing," was clipped before it could fully resolve the inconsistency—an execution cost that mattered exactly where the debate turned. CON's recurring move of redefining "routine co-use" as not really "outsourcing" is clever but ultimately concedes PRO's framing: if the safe version isn't outsourcing, then routine outsourcing (as the motion describes) remains what PRO condemns. PRO's closing capitalized on this concession cleanly, while CON's closing mostly restated its reframe without repairing the contradiction. This makes PRO's path to victory clearer and less contested by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A2), pointing out CON cannot simultaneously justify device use via a collapsed/exhausted parent and promise that same parent will actively co-engage every night — a contradiction CON's clipped final rebuttal did not fully resolve.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the decisive comparison on defaults, orientation migration, and contingent interaction rather than raw word count. A’s strongest live route was that CON simultaneously needed an exhausted parent (to justify the tool against “nothing”) and an energetically co-leading parent (to keep the tool from absorbing joint attention)—an inconsistency B never resolved. B scored with a sharp overbreadth/redefinition move (safe co-use is “parenting with a prop,” so the motion sweeps too far) and a clean real-family baseline, but R2 was clipped, the audiobook retreat under-answered A’s simulative/conversation point, and B’s case kept sliding between total absence and idealized co-use. After pressure and closings, A left the clearer reason to affirm: routine handover rewrites who the child addresses and what bedtime expects, while occasional help does not.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 on the dual-parent bind and default flip ([A4.4]/[A4.5]): CON cannot justify routine use with depletion then promise nightly active pausing/repair as the safe norm.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by bifurcating routine device use into harmful total handover and benign co-use, then showing the proposition overbroadly bans both. PRO (Side B) proved total replacement is developmentally risky but could not show that routine co-use inevitably collapses into parental absence, nor could it answer why fixed-story co-use is harmful. A’s closing crystallized that B defeated only the extreme case, not the motion as written, and A effectively turned B’s “parenting with a prop” challenge into proof that the motion confuses tool-use with outsourcing. The clipped rebuttal was a moderate execution cost but did not obscure A’s cleaner comparative victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer to B3.1 in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.2), conceding that active co-use is “parenting with a prop” and using that concession to show the motion fails because it either bans harmless prop-use or only prohibits total handover—which both sides already agree is bad.
