### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by exposing that Side B treated a right to refuse after-hours contact as a mandatory communication curfew. A offered a credible welfare mechanism through protected recovery and explained how handoffs and genuine emergencies can be accommodated. B raised real concerns about workload compression and flexibility, but repeatedly asserted that rigid hours, surveillance, and operational paralysis were legally inevitable by conflating disconnect rights with separate rest-period rules. Neither side supplied strong empirical comparison, but A's model was more plausible and its weighing more responsive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's answer to the compliance argument was decisive: complaint-based enforcement and negotiated boundaries do not inherently require keystroke surveillance or prohibit voluntary evening work. Side B responded by invoking mandatory rest directives, but never established that right-to-disconnect laws themselves necessarily impose those rigid curfews.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won the central definitional clash. A reframed productivity in knowledge work as cognitive output vs B's velocity-equals-output, showed evening traffic is largely low-value noise, and answered B's strongest compliance-mechanics attack (rigid shifts, 11-hour rest) with France's negotiated/complaint-based model and that over-compliance is managerial choice, not legal inevitability. B's pipeline-halt and welfare-backfire arguments (compression, shadow work) were asserted as permanent losses but never shown to outweigh recoverable workflow costs vs durable burnout, leaving A's asymmetric weighing live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.4 directly answering B's Q1/Q2 on compression and liability, plus A5's velocity-as-proxy distinction that collapsed B's gridlock claim from output loss to process friction.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won by decisively exposing the regulatory mechanics of right-to-disconnect laws, demonstrating how strict liability for continuous rest periods forces employers to destroy the temporal flexibility (e.g., the 3 PM to 8 PM split shift) that knowledge workers rely on. Side B's dismissal of this as mere "managerial over-compliance" and its weak analogy to minimum wage enforcement failed to neutralize Side A's highly original and damaging flexibility-backfire argument. Side A also defended the productivity clash with concrete, plausible examples of global pipeline friction and SLA failures, whereas Side B relied on a more abstract distinction between velocity and cognitive output that did not fully account for time-sensitive knowledge work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it answered Side B's pressure question by explaining that enterprise liability for mandatory rest windows makes passive tolerance of off-hours work legally impossible, thereby collapsing Side B's claim that rigid curfews are just a voluntary managerial choice.
