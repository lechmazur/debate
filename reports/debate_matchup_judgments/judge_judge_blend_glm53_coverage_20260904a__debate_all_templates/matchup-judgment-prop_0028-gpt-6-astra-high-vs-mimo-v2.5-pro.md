### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully disaggregating the labor market to answer Side A's strongest historical weighing. Side B's structural argument about the asymmetry of reach—that raising thresholds is faster/broader than creating genuinely skilled jobs—gave them the clearer path to winning the comparative burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4) cleanly dismantled Side A's historical counterfactual by distinguishing between professional jobs (which were walled off) and ordinary employment (which wasn't, but now requires a degree toll).
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won because it established that the pre-expansion baseline was a wall of demographic exclusion, not a neutral market of accessible apprenticeships, and that PRO's screening framework treats any graduate benefit as arms-race evidence, making it unfalsifiable. CON leveraged PRO's concession that expansion opened professional access for women, minorities, and working-class students, showed that knowledge-sector growth and field-differentiated returns meet PRO's own falsifiability standard, and crystallized the debate with an unanswered "wall versus toll" frame: credential inflation is a real but bounded cost within a fundamentally expanded opportunity structure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 charge that PRO's screening theory is empirically unfalsifiable—relabeling every graduate benefit as screening—and that the pre-expansion world offered no scalable alternative path, forced PRO to concede transformative access gains while failing to prove those gains were outweighed by screening costs.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A wins by successfully splitting Side B's strongest argument. While B effectively highlights the historical exclusion (the "wall") and the demographic gains of university expansion, A correctly distinguishes between opening elite professional pathways and imposing unnecessary degree filters on ordinary employment. A's "decisive asymmetry" mechanism—that employers can raise screening thresholds much faster than economies create genuinely skilled work—provides a cleaner causal story for why mass expansion predominantly drove credential inflation. A also handles the pressure questions more rigorously, refusing to accept B's framing that inflation is merely "at the margins," and effectively neutralizes B's graduate wage premium argument as a measure of the non-graduate penalty rather than net opportunity. B's "wall vs. toll" analogy is rhetorically excellent, but analytically, A proves the toll is being levied on jobs that don't need it. B also suffers a minor execution cost from a clipped opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where A answers the pressure questions by establishing clear falsifiability criteria and sharply distinguishing between the expansion of elite professional training (which was progressive) and the imposition of degree requirements on ordinary employment (which is regressive), effectively neutralizing Side B's demographic inclusion argument.
