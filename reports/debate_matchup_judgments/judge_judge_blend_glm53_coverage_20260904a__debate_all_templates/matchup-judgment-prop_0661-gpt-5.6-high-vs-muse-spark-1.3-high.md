### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases without resorting to fake citations, and both handled the Pressure Questions format correctly with labeled A1/A2 answers. The decisive clash was Goodhart's law: CON argued that merely knowing which factors count (location, keystrokes, scanner gaps) is enough to game a system even without exact weights, illustrated with concrete, plausible mechanisms (mouse-jigglers, empty scans, "clean device" fraud). PRO's central counter—"if disclosure breaks the metric, it was a bad metric"—is a clever reframe but never actually neutralizes the practical harm, and CON exploited this directly in Rebuttal 2 by turning PRO's own "device anomalies trigger review" fraud-filter example into a demonstration that even PRO's tiered/materiality-based disclosure standard still hands adversaries exploitable information. CON also offered a genuine substitute regime (individualized post-decision notice plus rebuttal before finalization, backed by disparate-impact litigation and regulatory discovery) that answered much of PRO's due-process concern, though it leaves a real gap PRO correctly presses in closing: without any baseline disclosure that a system exists, it's unclear how outcome statistics or systemic patterns would ever surface for investigation. PRO's closing crystallized this well ("detection by the victim after the damage"), but by that point CON's sharper, more concrete rebuttal work—especially the self-own on the fraud-filter example—had done more damage to PRO's core compromise position than PRO had done to CON's alternative. The debate was close and both sides argued the actual motion rather than swapping it, but CON's rebuttals landed with more precision and left PRO's tiered-disclosure model looking structurally shakier than CON's individualized-notice-plus-litigation alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turn (A4.3), which used PRO's own fraud-filter example ("device anomalies trigger review") to show that even PRO's restricted "principal factors only" disclosure standard still lets sophisticated actors adapt (keep devices clean, steal another way), directly undercutting PRO's claim that their tiered disclosure avoids enabling gaming.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively collapsing Side B's distinction between disclosing 'factors' and 'thresholds,' using concrete examples (mouse-jigglers, empty scans) to prove that Goodhart's Law will ruin even sound metrics. Side A's alternative mechanism—individualized ex-post reasons before discipline finalizes—sufficiently answered Side B's due process concerns while avoiding the pitfalls of universal ex-ante disclosure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 perfectly leveraged Goodhart's Law to counter Side B's claim that fragile metrics deserve to be exposed, arguing that even honest measures stop measuring honesty once they become explicit targets.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by keeping a live alternative that answered PRO’s best due-process claims without the full mandate: individualized reasons plus data at the point of decision, plus existing disparate-impact and discovery tools for systemic patterns. PRO’s strongest frames—private law, silent scheduling/promotion harm, and asymmetry of risk—landed well, but the “if gaming breaks it, the metric was bad” reply never neutralized Goodhart. CON made factor-notice itself the cheat sheet (empty scans, mouse-jigglers, phrase theater) and showed PRO’s materiality line as litigation-prone over-disclosure. That left PRO needing either a disclosure so thin it fails oversight or thick enough to industrialize gaming. After full exchange, CON’s comparison was clearer: keep the ruler straight, fix bathroom-break errors case-by-case, rather than publish the test then call the breakage accountability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s rebuttal 1–2 on Goodhart: knowing factors alone teaches empty scans/jigglers, while an individualized “flagged absent at X + data + pre-final rebuttal” fixes the bathroom-break case without an ex ante blueprint.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases with plausible mechanisms and no fabricated authority, making this a genuinely close debate. B's opening was vivid and set a strong emotional and causal frame (hidden scores, laundered bias, silent errors), and its Pressure Questions exposed a real gap in CON's event-triggered model: workers may not know to invoke rights, and regulators lack an inventory to target audits. However, under that same pressure, B was forced to narrow "disclose to workers and regulators" into a tiered, functionally-scoped regime (factors-not-thresholds for workers, fuller records for regulators, security carve-outs for gaming risk). A seized on this shift effectively, arguing that the "short standardized notice" PRO promised cannot actually detect the disparate-impact harms PRO relies on, while the fuller version PRO concedes regulators need becomes a costly audit obligation rather than a simple mandate — exposing an unresolved dilemma at the heart of PRO's case that PRO never fully dissolved, mostly re-asserting that tiering was implicit in the motion rather than answering why it doesn't shrink the "universal" disclosure claim. A's counter-model (material-change or worker-request triggers plus targeted regulatory audits) directly answered B's Q1 by locating the trigger in the observable outcome (thinned shifts) rather than knowledge of the algorithm's existence, which meaningfully blunted B's central "chicken-and-egg" objection. A's opening being clipped is a real execution cost, but the lost content was substantially restated and expanded in later rebuttals, limiting the practical damage. On balance, A's rebuttals did more to destabilize B's live route to victory than B's rebuttals did to CON's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Turn 4 point ([A2.1]) that PRO's tiered, decision-linked disclosure "quietly abandons the blanket proposition" was the pivotal exchange: it forced PRO into defending a narrower, functionally-scoped version of disclosure for the rest of the debate, which A then used to argue that PRO's model is either too shallow to catch the harms PRO cites or too deep to be the light-touch mandate PRO promised.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by effectively trapping Side B in a dilemma between disclosure that is too shallow to expose bias or too deep to avoid gaming and compliance costs. A also cleanly points out that B relies on caveats and thresholds not found in the proposition to survive the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (A4.4 and A4.5) is decisive, specifically the 'call duration' example that proves some metrics are valid yet easily destroyed by optimization, and the epistemic check that B is reading unstated limitations into the prompt.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by making the decisive comparison clearest: blanket advance disclosure is neither necessary nor sufficient for accountability, while event-triggered explanation plus confidential, risk-based regulatory access better links visibility to actual harm. PRO's quiet-harms and precondition-of-knowledge pressure was real and memorable, but CON answered it with material-change/request triggers and retained-record audits, and repeatedly forced PRO into unstated tiers, carve-outs, and audience splits not in the motion. The unresolved too-shallow-or-too-costly dilemma, plus lingering gaming and scope problems, undercut the universal mandate as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained dilemma rebuttal (A2.7/A4.4–A4.6): PRO either gives notices too thin to detect accent/bias patterns or imposes continuous weighting/outcome reporting that creates the gaming, privacy, and compliance costs it denies—so the blanket “should be required” fails.
