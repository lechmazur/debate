### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that opt-in under industry power asymmetry becomes merely coerced paperwork—a "difference in paperwork, not kind"—while non-waivable studio duties would actually protect powerless performers. B cleanly turned A's concession that the opt-in checkbox would appear on casting forms, showing it fails A's own test for meaningful consent, and offered a stronger comparative framework.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing demolition of A's casting-form concession, showing that a "specific informed checkbox" under take-it-or-leave-it audition pressure is the same coerced document with better lighting, and that non-waivable entitlements survive coercion where waivable opt-in does not.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON wins the central power-asymmetry clash. PRO's case rested on take-it-or-leave-it boilerplate being non-consensual [B1.3], but CON forced PRO to concede studios could put the new opt-in on casting paperwork and prefer signers [B4.1]. A then showed visibility is not leverage and a coerced yes forecloses complaint, while a non-waivable disclosure+payment duty attached to the act of cloning survives coercion and delivers PRO's own goods (notice, residuals, sunset) without the anticommons and erasure cost. PRO never solved the coercion contradiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's R2 A3-A4 seizing on PRO's B4.1 admission - turning "the rule working" into proof opt-in becomes new boilerplate, then contrasting waivable consent with non-waivable entitlement via minimum-wage analogy.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively exposing the practical flaw in Side A's opt-in mechanism. When Side A conceded that studios could simply make the "specific yes" a condition of casting, Side B brilliantly reframed this as "new boilerplate" that coerces consent without giving day players actual leverage. Side B's alternative—non-waivable statutory entitlements akin to minimum wage or overtime—provided a much stronger, more realistic protection for vulnerable actors. Side B's rhetoric was also exceptionally sharp, memorable, and directly advanced the core clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's attack in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing on Side A's admission that studios could put the opt-in on casting paperwork, effectively turning a "veto" into a coerced signature that forecloses future complaints while changing nothing about the power dynamic.
