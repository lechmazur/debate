### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Both sides built coherent, competing frames—A's "privacy as the condition of freedom" versus B's "security as the precondition for any liberty at all." The decisive turn came in Rebuttal 2/Closing: pressed on how to stop the slippery slope of security-first logic, B conceded "No perfect principle stops that expansion," essentially admitting the structural weakness A had been building toward since Rebuttal 1's targeted-vs-mass distinction. A's closing exploited that concession cleanly, turning it into the debate's central reason to vote PRO. B's rhetoric was vivid and its pressure questions sharp, but its answers to A's pressure questions (especially "it only needs to stop one") sidestepped the actual efficacy critique rather than refuting it, and its final closing could only assert a moral line rather than resolve the conceded principle problem. A's case also used concrete, unremarkable background knowledge (PATRIOT Act, Investigatory Powers Act ratchet effects) responsibly rather than inventing statistics, giving it a grounding edge. A's repeated clipping is a real execution cost and kept the margin from being larger, but the substantive argumentative trajectory and the successful weaponizing of B's key concession give PRO the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Closing exploitation of B's Rebuttal 2 admission ("No perfect principle stops that expansion") was the most decisive exchange—B's own words validated A's irreversibility/slippery-slope argument, and B's attempted recovery ("there is a line every society draws") did not resolve the conceded structural problem.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by distinguishing legitimate targeted intrusions from indiscriminate surveillance and showing that Side B's strongest examples involved the former, which A already accepted. Side B never adequately established that bulk collection prevents security failures, instead asserting that saving even one life would suffice without addressing likelihood, proportionality, or systemic privacy costs. Its admission that no principle reliably stops expansion strengthened A's slippery-escalation concern. A's repeated clipping and somewhat sweeping claims narrowed the margin, but B's closing mischaracterized A as opposing searches of suspects despite A's explicit concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal was decisive: it showed that intercepting a suspect's messages, tracing an outbreak, and disrupting a specific plot support targeted security rather than security-first mass surveillance. Side B never repaired that mismatch and ultimately returned to a one-suspect example that A had already conceded.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by successfully positioning security as the absolute prerequisite for privacy. When Side B conceded that targeted privacy violations are acceptable to stop threats, Side A effectively used this to show that security is fundamentally the higher value. Furthermore, Side A introduced a decisive, unanswered mechanism: under-prioritizing security leads to catastrophic failures that trigger public panic, resulting in the sudden, total destruction of privacy anyway. Side B's performance was also hindered by severe word-count management issues, resulting in three clipped speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1, which brilliantly dismantled Side B's targeted-vs-mass distinction by arguing that such nuance "only exists if security already works," and warning that failing to compromise privacy now leads to panicky, unchecked mass surveillance later.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but A controlled the framing battle more effectively. A consistently pressed the "who gets the benefit of the doubt in the hard, uncertain call" framing and used a well-aimed pressure question (Q1: name a hard tradeoff where you'd pick privacy) to trap B: B's answer (always-on home cameras, justified only by "marginal" security gain) actually confirmed A's thesis that B only cedes to privacy when security stakes are trivial—A then explicitly weaponized this in Rebuttal 2 and Closing ("Our opponent will only ever side with privacy when the security gain is trivial"), a clean example of cross-round progression and exploitation of a live concession. B's case leaned on strong initial examples (cartels, warlords, the "floor" argument) but repeatedly mischaracterized A's position as absolutist even after A explicitly clarified the default-plus-narrow-exception framework, meaning several of B's rebuttals landed on a straw man rather than A's actual claim. B's execution was also hurt structurally: the opening, first rebuttal, and closing were all clipped for exceeding word limits, costing B potentially decisive content exactly where the debate's tension (bounded exceptions vs. default priority) needed resolving. A's speeches, while sometimes relying on generic historical gesture ("every regime that turned authoritarian"), were tighter, stayed within the clash, and closed with the sharpest, most memorable weighing point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.6) turning the "bounded exception vs. default" distinction into a clean tiebreaker principle, later validated when B's own Pressure Question answer (home cameras with "marginal" security benefit) proved A's claim that B only cedes to privacy on trivial stakes—explicitly exploited in A's Closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B won by exposing the instability in A’s proposed privacy default. A accepted warranted wiretaps and airport screening but largely defined these away as non-overrides, without clearly explaining why targeted surveillance can be trusted while other proportionate security measures cannot. B also supplied the stronger comparative account: security enables the practical exercise of freedom, while legal oversight can distinguish bounded protection from authoritarian surveillance. A’s systemic-abuse warning remained important, but relied on sweeping claims and did not fully answer B’s lawlessness or oversight comparison. B’s repeated clipping is an execution cost, though the visible arguments were sufficient.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions directly, gave a genuine case where privacy should win, and then showed that A’s accepted wiretaps rely on the same imperfect oversight institutions A invoked against broader security measures.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by successfully identifying and exploiting a fatal contradiction in Side B's case. When Side B conceded that exceptions like airport screening and warranted wiretaps are acceptable, Side A effectively demonstrated that in high-stakes conflicts, society consistently prioritizes security over privacy. Side A suffered execution penalties for being repeatedly clipped at the word limit, but their argumentative dominance on the core clash still secured a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 perfectly turned Side B's skepticism of democratic oversight against them by pointing out that Side B relies on those exact same mechanisms to justify the wiretaps they already accepted.
