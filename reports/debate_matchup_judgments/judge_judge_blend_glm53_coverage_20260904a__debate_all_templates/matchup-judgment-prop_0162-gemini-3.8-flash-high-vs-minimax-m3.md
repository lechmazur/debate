### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): The decisive thread of the debate was CON's attack on the word "prioritize." CON repeatedly forced PRO to admit that its own flagship examples (San Diego, LA) treat reuse as roughly 40% of supply, not a replacement for imports, which turned PRO's evidence against it. PRO never squarely rebutted this in the closing, instead retreating in Rebuttal 2 to defining "prioritize" as merely "marginal new capital allocation" — a weaker claim that concedes the strong version of its own resolution. CON also sustained a plausible, unanswered concentration/brine-disposal argument against closed-loop treatment, and used the pressure-question exchange effectively to expose that PRO's own cited cities keep imports as the backbone supply. PRO's opening (climate resilience, ecological/geopolitical costs, energy efficiency) was solid and its "you cannot pipe water that doesn't exist" line was a strong rhetorical moment, but PRO's closing failed to re-engage the "40% by 2035" definitional point that CON kept hammering, leaving CON with the cleaner, unresolved line of victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated use of PRO's own San Diego/LA examples (first in Rebuttal 1, reinforced in Pressure Questions Q2 and Closing) to show reuse functions as a minority supplement rather than a "priority," a point PRO never fully neutralized and effectively conceded by redefining "prioritize" as marginal capital allocation in Rebuttal 2.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won by correctly framing the motion as a choice about marginal investment in reuse versus importing more water, not reuse as a monopoly. Side B repeatedly attacked abandonment of existing imports, but A explicitly preserved current infrastructure and showed that B’s alternatives—protecting existing rights, banking water, and watershed restoration—did not establish where additional imported water would come from. B raised legitimate construction-time and emerging-contaminant risks, but neither outweighed A’s central scarcity and control comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer to B’s priority objection was decisive: existing aqueducts can carry cities during construction while new capital shifts toward reuse. B’s own retreat from expanding imports to stewarding current allocations reinforced A’s claim that it lacked a credible source of additional distant water.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by grounding the debate in the physical reality of water scarcity, effectively arguing that cities cannot prioritize importing more water because that surplus water no longer exists. Side B also successfully navigated Side A's strongest semantic trap regarding the definition of "prioritize" versus a "portfolio," clarifying that prioritizing means directing marginal capital to direct potable reuse rather than expanding depleted conveyance. Side A's pivot to stewarding existing rights effectively conceded the core premise that importing *more* water is unviable, leaving Side B with the cleaner, more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal to Side A's "portfolio vs. prioritize" semantic trap, clarifying that directing the vast majority of new capital expenditure to direct potable reuse (as in San Diego) is exactly what prioritizing over importing *more* water looks like in practice, while Side A's defense of existing imports conceded that new imports are physically impossible.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Both sides fought a tight, substantive debate, but B built the more resilient overall case. B's opening anchored the debate in concrete, plausible scarcity (Colorado River near dead pool, Delta collapse) and proven DPR technology (Orange County, Singapore, Windhoek), giving the debate a clear "the alternative is disappearing" frame that A never fully neutralized—A's closing still asserts cities should "diversify" conveyance without explaining how, when B's whole case is that the nearby basins are already tapped out. A landed the debate's sharpest single blow by exposing the incoherence in B's evaporation argument (you can't recycle water that evaporated), which genuinely damaged B's initial mass-balance framing. But B recovered by correctly noting the motion says "prioritize... over importing MORE," not eliminate existing supply, reframing DPR as covering marginal growth rather than replacing baseline imports—a point A's "parasitic pump" rebuttal never fully dismantled. On brine and buffer-safety, the exchange was closer to a wash: A kept pressure on inland disposal costs and outage risk, B offered plausible counters (engineered buffer tanks, comparable industrial brine handling) that were not decisively rebutted. In the end, B's closing crystallized the strongest live point in the round—"you cannot import water that does not exist"—while A's closing mostly re-asserted the mass-balance objection without answering where new imported water would actually come from. That unresolved gap tips a close, competitive debate to B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 catching B's evaporation/mass-balance confusion ("you cannot purify vapor that blew away") was the single sharpest moment, but B's Rebuttal 2 answer reframing the motion around "prioritize... over importing more" (retaining, not replacing, existing transfers) was the more decisive recovery, since A never cleanly answered where "diversified" new imports would come from once basins are conceded to be stressed.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A won by correctly locating the motion at the margin: cities can retain existing transfers and prioritize reuse over constructing additional import capacity. B sharply exposed A's mistaken suggestion that DPR recovers evaporated water, but then repeatedly treated prioritization as total independence from imports. That strawman weakened B's mass-balance, outage, and backup arguments. A also gave the clearer comparison between predictable local recovery and increasingly constrained, ecologically costly marginal imports, though some examples and claims were overstated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B decisively corrected A's hydrological error that reuse somehow converts outdoor consumptive losses back into supply. A nevertheless recovered by clarifying that the motion concerns new capacity, not abolishing existing imports; B never squarely answered why needing baseline inflow prevents DPR from being the better marginal priority.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully anchoring their case to the physical reality of the motion's alternative: importing more water from distant basins is physically and legally impossible because those basins (e.g., the Colorado River) are already at dead pool. Side B also decisively weaponized the word "more" in the motion to neutralize Side A's mass-balance argument, clarifying that prioritizing new DPR capacity does not mean abandoning existing imports. Side A delivered highly polished, theoretically sound arguments regarding hydrologic loops and brine disposal, but ultimately failed to solve the fatal flaw in their alternative or adapt to Side B's text-based framing of the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they explicitly used the motion's phrasing ("importing MORE water") to dismantle Side A's closed-loop mass-balance argument, proving that prioritizing new DPR capacity retains existing imports as backup, while importing more from depleted basins is physically impossible.
