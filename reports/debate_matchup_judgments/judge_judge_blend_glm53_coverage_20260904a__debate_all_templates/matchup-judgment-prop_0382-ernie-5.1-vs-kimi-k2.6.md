### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): PRO (Side A) holds the better path to victory, primarily by exploiting the proposition's own language. The motion says "once safety standards are met"—a phrase that explicitly reserves a distinct pre-market clearance phase—and then calls for "same basic terms." PRO's interpretation gives both halves of that sentence real meaning: tailored pre-market review first, then regulatory parity on the shelf. CON's reading—that "same basic terms" must include every stage—renders "once safety standards are met" either circular or meaningless, a structural weakness CON never addressed. PRO's sharpest pressure question (Q1: name a concrete hazard existing food-safety science cannot detect) exposed the core gap in CON's case, and CON's Rebuttal 2 answer confirmed it: the objection is about implementation fit and inspectorate training, not about scientifically undetectable hazards. That distinction matters, because PRO correctly argued that regulatory frameworks adapt to new production contexts without requiring entirely separate systems. CON's most effective move was the GM crops reversal in B2.2—turning PRO's own example against them by noting GM foods face distinct pre-market authorizations. PRO recovered in A4.3 by drawing the pre-market/shelf distinction, which is a genuine and meaningful one; a GM food on a supermarket shelf faces the same pathogen standards and residue limits as any other food. CON's closing tried to force a binary (either truly identical = inadequate, or substantively different = CON wins), but this binary is false: "same basic terms" in ordinary regulatory language does not mean mechanically identical rules in every operational detail. CON's "vat is not a barn" rhetoric was memorable and its ontological argument had real force, but it ultimately established an implementation challenge rather than a principled objection to regulatory parity. CON's rhetorical execution was slightly stronger throughout, but PRO's logical architecture—anchored in the proposition's own conditional structure—remained intact after the full exchange. PRO's clipped closing is a minor execution cost that does not reverse the outcome.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's B2.2 turning the GM crops analogy, paired with PRO's recovery in A4.3. CON correctly identified that GM foods face distinct pre-market steps that conventional crops never face, landing a real blow. PRO recovered by drawing the pre-market/shelf distinction: those extra steps govern how the product was made, not how it is policed as a food on the shelf, where the same pathogen standards and residue limits apply. PRO's recovery was slightly cleaner than CON's original turn because it aligned with the proposition's "once safety standards are met" language, which already contemplates a separate clearance phase before parity kicks in.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won by consistently proving that the physical differences between bioreactors and abattoirs necessitate bespoke regulatory rules. B effectively turned A's GM analogy, pointing out that GM foods require tailored pre-market and traceability rules, which contradicts the "same basic terms" resolution. Furthermore, A committed a major unforced error in the closing by claiming B wants the product regulated like conventional meat, completely mischaracterizing B's core argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which masterfully weaponized A's own GM crops analogy to prove that novel foods inherently require tailored oversight, defeating the "same basic terms" standard.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A won by exposing the internal contradiction in "same basic terms" via PRO's own GM analogy and pressure answers, showing distinct protocols and supply chains cannot be forced into one framework without evasion or inequity; B's responses were competent but repeatedly conceded ground on tailoring.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal 2 directly dismantled B's GM pivot and HACCP claims using pressure questions, forcing the decisive admission that tailored oversight contradicts the motion.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): PRO built and maintained a cleaner logical framework throughout. The central distinction PRO established—between a unified regulatory category with nested process-specific protocols and identical paperwork for every provision—was linguistically defensible, practically grounded in the dairy/brewing analogy, and never squarely answered by CON. CON's case suffered from two persistent structural errors: (1) repeatedly conflating food-safety classification with fiscal/subsidy/tax policy, a conflation PRO correctly identified as a category error in A2.4 and sharpened in A4.3 ("fiscal policy and safety classification are different statutes"); (2) inflating "same basic terms" to mean wholesale transplant of every FMIA provision, a reading that proved too much and was never defended on linguistic grounds. CON's strongest live argument—that the FMIA is carcass-centric and contains no provision for batch sterility testing—was genuine but ultimately self-defeating: it supports amending the framework to include bioreactor protocols, exactly what PRO proposes, rather than excluding cultivated meat altogether. CON's labeling argument was largely neutralized by PRO's point that existing frameworks already mandate process-specific disclosures (country of origin, antibiotic claims) within the same statutory category. CON also carried execution costs: the opening was clipped for exceeding the word limit, and the closing contained a misdirected reference to [A5.1] treating PRO's own argument as if it supported CON's position. PRO's pressure questions were sharper and PRO's A1:/A2: answers were more direct and responsive. CON's closing added little new crystallization and repeated the regulatory-structure argument without resolving the central clash PRO had exposed. The decisive rebuttal came in PRO's consistent counter that "same basic terms" is a legal classification question, not a demand for identical treatment across every fiscal, subsidy, and inspection protocol—a distinction CON never overcame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2.4–A2.6 cluster, continued through A4.3: PRO's identification that CON's cost/externality objections are a category error—the motion governs food-safety classification, not carbon pricing or subsidy regimes, which operate under separate statutes—stripped the most ambitious planks from CON's case and reframed the entire debate. CON never answered this distinction cleanly; instead, B4.3 doubled down on conflating the two, actually conceding PRO's point that fiscal differentiation is still available while claiming parity still bars it, an internal contradiction that damaged CON's credibility for the remainder of the debate.

A successfully proved that regulatory parity means equal classification with process-specific rules, not a literal copy-paste of slaughterhouse checklists or tax policy. B's case collapsed entirely in Rebuttal 2 when B accidentally restated A's argument against themselves regarding agricultural subsidies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3 decisively refutes B's tax/subsidy argument by pointing out that fiscal policy and safety classification are distinct statutes, brilliantly comparing it to organic vegetables and EVs.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO wins because it maintained the motion's narrow scope (food-safety classification only), directly answered pressure questions with concrete mechanisms (process labels, separate fiscal statutes), and repeatedly exposed CON's strawman of literal identical paperwork.
