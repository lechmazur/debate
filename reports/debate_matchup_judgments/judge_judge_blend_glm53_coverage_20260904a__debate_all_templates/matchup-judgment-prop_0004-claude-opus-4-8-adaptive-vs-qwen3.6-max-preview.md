### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the debate centered on the motion’s broad legal mandate and the non-compressible labor in essential and service sectors. Side A had a coherent productivity, retention, and coordination story, but it relied too heavily on asserting that turnover savings, latent caregivers, and reduced overtime would offset a recurring reduction in hours without proving that these mechanisms scale across “most” full-time employees. Side B’s patient-hours framing made the key burden clearer: if output requires presence, the missing hours must be covered by more workers, more overtime, or less service. Side A never fully answered where enough net new labor comes from or why costs would not rise substantially.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was the response to “restructuring” and retention: it argued that a 32-hour nurse still supplies fewer hours, retention slows losses but does not replace the missing coverage, and hiring enough base-pay workers confirms rather than escapes the cost problem.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): CON maintained a single coherent line throughout: the motion imposes a uniform rule on non-uniform work, and the core arithmetic (missing hours in essential services) was never neutralized. PRO's dynamic offsets (retention, overtime swaps) were directly countered on permanence versus one-time savings. CON's pressure questions and rebuttals kept the decisive clash live; PRO's responses adapted but did not resolve the burden on new supply.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2's direct mapping of "restructures" back onto the fork, plus B4's asymmetry point on waste savings vs. recurring wages, which closed PRO's main escape routes.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): CON built and defended a decisive fork: for presence-based labor—which describes most full-time employees in advanced economies—a 32-hour standard with unchanged pay forces either intensification, reduced coverage, or a structural ~25% increase in labor costs. PRO never escaped this arithmetic. Attempts to solve the gap through "retention" were shown to keep the same workers on fewer hours, not add coverage; "latent caregivers" were shown to be existing part-timers, not net-new supply; and "reallocating waste" was exposed as trading finite one-time savings for permanent recurring obligations. CON's refrain of a uniform rule on a non-uniform economy proved durable because PRO conceded by silence that voluntary adoption already captures the sectors where compression works, meaning the mandate binds hardest exactly where the model fails. PRO's case remained coherent for cognitive work but could not justify the universal statute as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained demolition of PRO's essential-services coverage mechanism—showing that retention preserves bodies but not hours, that reclassifying part-timers attaches full pay to existing hours without creating new coverage, and that overtime-threshold logic confirms the cost fork rather than escaping it. This exchange neutralized PRO's only plausible path to making the mandate work for "most" workers.
