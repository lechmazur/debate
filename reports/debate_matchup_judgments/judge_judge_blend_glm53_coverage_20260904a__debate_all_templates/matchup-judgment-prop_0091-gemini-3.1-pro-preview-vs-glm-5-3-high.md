### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. B better explained that owning the physical components does not itself prove that every enabled capability was purchased, while standardized hardware can reduce assembly complexity, lower entry prices, and preserve optional activation. A never established its claimed double payment, and its weight argument was weak outside the battery example. B overreached by suggesting the motion bans all tiering and incurred a clipped opening, but its consent, pricing, and consumer-choice case survived A’s largely asserted property-rights framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to double-dipping was decisive: uniform hardware can avoid costly production variants, so component presence does not establish overpayment. A’s answer substituted operating weight for proof that buyers paid twice, allowing B to narrow that burden and emphasize disclosed consumer choice.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A built and defended a clearer path to victory. A established that pre-installed hardware is a physical asset the owner maintains and transports, making recurring subscriptions for its activation rent-seeking rather than genuine service pricing. A directly answered B’s strongest challenge—the “horsepower problem”—by distinguishing genuinely smaller physical configurations from identical hardware held hostage by software locks, preserving the motion as written. A also turned B’s economic efficiency argument by showing the buyer pays twice: once through the base price and again through lifelong fuel and EV range penalties for hauling deactivated components. B mischaracterized A’s engagement in the closing and failed to fully neutralize the intuitive property-rights framing or the double-dipping mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal (Turn 7), where A reframed B’s challenge about “overpayment” by identifying the lifelong fuel and EV-range penalty of hauling deactivated hardware as the real consumer subsidy—then crystallized that automakers offload manufacturing costs onto owners while retaining control as “landlords” over bolted-in components.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully trapping Side A on the "horsepower problem." While Side A presented a highly persuasive and rhetorically powerful case against rent-seeking and the "dead weight" of dormant hardware, Side B exposed a fatal flaw in Side A's underlying principle. Side A argued that owners are entitled to the "exact same physical hardware" installed in the car, but when pressed, Side A could not cleanly distinguish between a software-locked heated seat and a software-locked battery or engine tune, which rely on the exact same physical cells or blocks. Side B convincingly demonstrated that Side A's rule would effectively ban all hardware-based tiered pricing, raising entry prices for consumers. Side B's analytical precision and sharp rebuttals in the later rounds secured the win, despite Side A's superior rhetorical flourish and a minor clipping penalty in B's opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's line-drawing in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, specifically pointing out that a software-limited battery uses the exact same physical cells, causing Side A's "exact same physical hardware" distinction to collapse and proving the motion would inadvertently ban standard tiered pricing.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by drawing the cleaner distinction between permanent onboard functionality and genuinely ongoing services, while preserving optional temporary plans and one-time unlocks. Side B made the affordability clash competitive and correctly challenged A's simplistic treatment of cross-subsidies, but relied too heavily on asserted pricing and the dubious claim that advanced sensors are useless without continuous cloud or machine-learning refinement. A also better connected the motion to ownership and vendor-shutdown risk.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing reversal of the console analogy was decisive: consoles may be subsidized through separately purchased games, but ownership of the console hardware itself does not expire. B's response equated installed automotive hardware with every game a console could run, which did not fit the proposition and weakened its central analogy.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by exposing that Side B's economic defense rested on a false choice between subsidized base models and affordable access, demonstrating through the turned console analogy that manufacturers can subsidize hardware while still granting permanent ownership of the physical device. A consistently pressed the property-rights frame that B never squarely answered—B explained why paywalls are profitable but never justified why that profitability should override the owner's claim to hardware already installed and paid for. A's pressure questions exposed cracks in B's subsidization logic, and A's closing crystallized the clash cleanly by showing that B's own best example refuted B's case. Side B's arguments shifted ground (from cheap standardization to expensive subsidization to cloud-dependency) without resolving their tension, and their affordability case was weakened by A's point that a forever fee is not clearly cheaper than a one-time unlock.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's inversion of Side B's console analogy in the second rebuttal and closing, showing that subsidized hardware works precisely when the buyer permanently owns the device—proving that recurring paywalls for installed hardware are unnecessary to the subsidized-price model.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by successfully defending the core principle of ownership and decisively dismantling Side B's economic and analogical arguments. Side B's attempt to use the video game console analogy to defend subsidized hardware backfired when Side A correctly pointed out that console hardware doesn't disable itself when a user stops buying software (games). Side A also effectively exposed the lack of a limiting principle in Side B's claim that all hardware features are actually "continuous services," properly distinguishing between static hardware (heated seats, motor output) and genuine ongoing services. While Side B's affordability and flexibility arguments were initially strong, Side A's clarification that the proposition targets the forced expiration of paid-for hardware neutralized them. Side A's closing crystallized the clash perfectly, securing a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing dismantling of the video game console analogy, pointing out that console makers recoup costs through software sales without disabling the physical hardware the consumer already owns, perfectly flipping Side B's own example to support the PRO case.
