### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won primarily by exploiting a genuine internal contradiction in Side B's case and by advancing a clean, consistent causal story throughout. Side A's Q1 pressure question exposed the logical tension between B's claim that landlords would leave units vacant rather than risk long-term tenants (implying financial cushion) and B's separate claim that these same owners face foreclosure without STR income (implying financial desperation). Side B's answer in B4.1 was plausible but strained — a mortgaged, financially stretched homeowner choosing zero income over a long-term tenant requires a more convincing account of the rental regulatory environment than CON provided. Side A also landed a durable distinction throughout: standard STR restrictions typically allow owner-occupants to home-share spare rooms while targeting whole-home, absentee-investor conversions. Side B tried to counter this with compliance-cost arguments in B4.2–B4.3, but this was an empirically weaker rebuttal that never actually challenged the structural logic. Side A's "immediate tourniquet" framing was memorable and answered the underbuilding objection cleanly — both sides agree on structural deficits, but A correctly argued that reclaiming existing built units provides immediate supply relief. Side B's case was genuinely competitive: the filtering-is-a-cruel-illusion retort, the tenant protection laws argument, and the hotel-profit-leakage claim each had real force. But the internal contradiction was never fully resolved, and Side A's closer comparison at A4.6 (absentee investors vs. foundational residents) crystallized the stakes more decisively. Neither side used invented sourcing or brittle fake precision, keeping both credible on epistemic grounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Q1 pressure question [A3.1] exposing the contradiction between B2.1 (landlords wealthy enough to leave units vacant) and B2.3 (same owners facing foreclosure without STR income) was the most decisive exchange. Side A then weaponized this in A4.3 by making it the central comparison. Side B's B4.1 answer — that a stretched owner might still prefer vacancy over long-term tenant risk — was logically possible but undercooked, leaving the contradiction standing as a live burden against CON's case for the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won by keeping the debate tied to the motion: restricting whole-home short-term rentals in tight neighborhoods returns housing supply, reduces eviction incentives, and limits displacement. Side B raised plausible concerns about underbuilding, tenant-law risk, local hosts, and neighborhood spending, but leaned too heavily on unsupported claims that units would commonly stay vacant or become second homes and that restrictions would bankrupt ordinary hosts. Side A more convincingly weighed residents as the year-round economic base and showed that tourist spending losses are marginal compared with preserving housing stock.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was pressing the tension between Side B’s claim that owners would absorb zero income by leaving units vacant and its claim that those same owners face foreclosure without short-term rental revenue, then pairing that with the point that many restrictions target full-home commercial operators rather than spare-room home-sharing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): PRO won by establishing a cleaner, more direct causal path to the proposition. The eviction-prevention mechanism—removing the financial incentive for landlords to convert tenant-occupied units into STRs—was concrete and largely unrefuted. PRO also effectively narrowed the income-harm debate by distinguishing absentee commercial operators from owner-occupants who can still home-share, blunting CON's middle-class-host argument. CON's responses that units become luxury leases or sit vacant due to tenant-protection risks were plausible but did not squarely defeat PRO's filtering logic or the immediate displacement reduction. CON's late regulatory-creep argument helped but could not overcome PRO's more focused comparative weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's distinction between commercial operators and owner-occupant room-sharing, which narrowed CON's income-harm route and held up despite CON's subsequent regulatory-creep response.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides were substantively competent and engaged throughout. The deciding factor is that Side B maintained a consistent, targeted attack on Side A's core mechanism—housing filtering—that PRO never fully neutralized. PRO's case depended on a chain: STR restrictions → units return to market → luxury renters absorb them → cascade relieves rents down the income ladder → working-class displacement stops. Side B's "luxury capture" critique (returned units go to wealthy buyers or peak-market renters, not displaced service workers) and the "immediacy" critique (filtering takes years; income loss from restrictions is today) were each introduced early and sustained across every rebuttal. PRO answered with marginal pricing in inelastic markets, which is theoretically sound, and with the investor-premium-deflation angle in Rebuttal 2, but neither fully escaped the luxury-tier-capture problem in high-demand neighborhoods. The cannibalization argument (tourists are already coming) was a real point but only partially addressed Side B's neighborhood-business mechanism. Side B's framing—"guaranteed immediate destruction vs. invisible trickle-down relief"—was sharper and better matched to the motion's comparative structure. Side A's housing-as-necessity framing was rhetorically compelling but depended on the same under-defended mechanism. The margin is real but not dominant: PRO's marginal pricing point and the commercial-vs-casual distinction kept this competitive throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's sustained filtering critique—first raised in the opening, sharpened in B2.1, applied to the pressure question answer in B4.1–B4.4, and crystallized in the closing—was the most decisive exchange. When PRO answered the pressure question by invoking "filtering," Side B immediately labeled it "trickle-down economics," argued it takes years and arrives at microscopic magnitudes in lower tiers, and contrasted it with the immediacy of income loss. PRO never produced a persuasive timeline or mechanism showing filtering would reach working-class renters fast enough to matter, leaving their main causal chain materially damaged for the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate. B made the strongest negative route by arguing that STR stock is small, premium, and slow to filter down, while the income and visitor-spending harms are immediate. But A more convincingly narrowed the motion to restrictions on commercial entire-home and absentee STRs, which blunted B's local-host income offense and made B's repeated ban framing feel overstated. A also answered the small-stock point with concentration and marginal pressure in tight neighborhoods, and gave a more plausible response that high-demand-city visitors are largely shifted to hotels rather than lost entirely. B's harms were engaging but often relied on exaggerated claims of instant neighborhood collapse, while A gave the cleaner comparative story that returned housing and reduced speculative conversion matter more than displaced tourist lodging patterns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's most decisive rebuttal was that restrictions can target commercial ghost inventory while preserving genuine home-sharing, directly undercutting B's resident-host income argument and exposing B's tendency to argue against a total ban rather than the motion's broader idea of restriction.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B wins because it successfully undermined Side A's core causal mechanism. A relied on housing filtering and marginal supply shifts to connect luxury short-term rental conversions to working-class displacement relief, but B persuasively established that filtering is too slow and weak to prevent immediate displacement. Meanwhile, B demonstrated concrete, immediate harms to resident incomes and neighborhood businesses that outweigh A's theoretical rent benefits. A never fully recovered from the filtering critique or resolved the tension between immediate economic destruction and delayed housing market effects.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained attack on PRO's reliance on housing filtering as a mechanism for working-class rent relief, culminating in the framing that "you cannot pay next month's rent with a theoretical decade-long cooling trend." This directly undermined PRO's ability to prove the "reduces displacement" half of the proposition.
