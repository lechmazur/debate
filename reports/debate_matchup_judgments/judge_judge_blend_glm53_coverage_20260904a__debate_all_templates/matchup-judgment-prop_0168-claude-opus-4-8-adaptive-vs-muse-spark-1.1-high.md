### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won because the proposition itself foregrounds science per dollar, and B consistently showed why life-support overhead, safety requirements, and limited human reach make robots comparatively superior. A's budget-pie and capability-loss objections were plausible but largely asserted, while its attempt to redefine exploration as human presence evaded the motion's central comparison. A also misread B's claim about crewed programs cannibalizing science and treated robotic sample return as a concession when platform cost remains central. The clipped closing was an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that shifting most does not mean eliminating crewed capability, combined with the comparison between limited human-accessible destinations and probes operating at Venus, Jupiter, and Enceladus, most directly answered A's wrecking-ball claim. B also effectively exposed that A never substantiated why a minority crewed allocation could not preserve development capacity.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by maintaining stronger logical consistency and factual grounding. Side A's late rebuttals relied heavily on a severe mischaracterization of Side B's historical timeline (claiming B admitted post-Apollo cuts delayed science, when B explicitly said those cuts birthed the flagships) and an illogical spin on sample return. Side B successfully defended their metric and highlighted the vast reaches of space humans cannot survive in.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A2 in Turn 7, which dismantles the "human as best instrument" argument by noting that lab instruments on Earth are what give returned rocks their ongoing value, perfectly setting up the case for cheap robotic sample return.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by proving that shifting most budgets would destroy the political economy that sustains total space funding and irreplaceable deep-space human capabilities. PRO established that robots deliver superior science per dollar, but never produced a credible example where gutting crewed programs preserved the total budget; CON effectively turned PRO's post-Apollo evidence to show that science actually struggled for scraps when human spaceflight funding collapsed. PRO's "keep 20-30%" response remained unsupported and unpersuasive against CON's argument that most is exactly the amount that breaks living capability, while CON's framing of the metric debate ("mission defines the yardstick") prevented PRO from making the efficiency comparison decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's turn on PRO's post-Apollo evidence in Rebuttal 2 (A4.3): after PRO cited the post-Apollo era as proof that science survived crewed cuts, CON showed that by PRO's own earlier logic this was precisely when flagship missions were delayed, descoped, and cancelled—making PRO's best historical example actually support CON's claim that the total pie shrinks and science fights for scraps when human spaceflight is gutted.
