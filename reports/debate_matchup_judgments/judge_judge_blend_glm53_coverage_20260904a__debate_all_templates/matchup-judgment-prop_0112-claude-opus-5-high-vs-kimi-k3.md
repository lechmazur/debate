### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the motion’s actual comparison live: systems that tie access to work status versus single-payer financing that does not. B’s strongest evidence (Germany/Japan/Netherlands multipayer models) was repeatedly turned into a concession that those systems work only after state backstops, mandates, and risk equalization sever the access-work link A condemned. B’s plurality/exit and dedicated-contribution firewall points were real but never fully escaped A’s reply that funding, not payer count, drives waits and that wage-based revenue also collapses in downturns. B’s clipped R2 was a real execution cost. Net: A made the decisive clash clearer and easier to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s collapse of B’s European examples (A2.1–A2.3 / A4.5): they succeed by untying access from work, so they abandon the substance of the system B is defending.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): PRO kept the debate anchored to the proposition's "tie access to work status" language. CON's best models (Germany/Japan) were turned in [A2.1-3] and [A3.1]/[A5.1] to prove PRO's point: they work because the state pays when work stops, neutralizing the tie. CON's structural monopoly critique lost force when PRO reframed wait-time differences as funding, not payer-count, and CON's clipped R2 incurred an execution cost while conceding universality as shared ground. Single-payer's one pool, one rulebook, and coverage that never asks where you work survived as the cleaner reason to prefer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1 turnaround of Germany/Japan/Netherlands – showing they succeed by refusing to tie access to work and that every "patch" is a step toward universal financing – directly collapsed CON's live route that employer-based universality is distinct and superior.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's core examples. When Side B cited Germany and the Netherlands as successful employer-based systems, Side A effectively demonstrated that these systems only achieve universality by severing the tie between access and work status—effectively making them universal systems with redundant administrative layers. Side A's closing argument brilliantly flipped Side B's "firewall" metaphor into a "fuse," highlighting the pro-cyclical nature of payroll taxes during recessions. Side B's clipped rebuttal and inability to defend the "exit" mechanism in a heavily regulated, risk-equalized multi-payer system sealed the victory for Side A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they exposed that Side B's "plural" model requires so many regulatory patches (state backstops, risk equalization, no-refusal mandates) that it functionally abandons the employer tie while retaining the administrative waste, and then dismantled the "payroll firewall" argument by pointing out that payroll revenues shrink during economic downturns.
