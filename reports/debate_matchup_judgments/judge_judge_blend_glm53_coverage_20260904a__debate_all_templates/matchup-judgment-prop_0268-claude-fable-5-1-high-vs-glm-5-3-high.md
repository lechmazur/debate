- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): B won by keeping the decisive clash live: manufacturers shrink to preserve sticker anchors precisely because grams and unit prices are not noticed at purchase, so existing disclosure fails the market’s own test.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s conversion of A’s price-point anchoring concession (A2.2 → B4.1/B5.1): firms choose shrink because the sticker is noticed and grams are not—existence of the strategy proves current disclosure fails.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won because it forced Side B to concede the behavioral reality that manufacturers shrink packages precisely because consumers anchor on headline prices and overlook quantity changes. Side A also showed that Side B's technical objections about baselines, mixed causation, and tag lag were solvable implementation details rather than reasons to keep shoppers in the dark, while Side B's scope-creep closing overstated how far Side A's clarifications actually mutated the motion. Side B never directly answered whether a well-defined shrinkflation mandate would be acceptable, leaving its core resistance unpersuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal, which caught the contradiction in Side B's claim that net-weight disclosures are already plain and obvious while simultaneously arguing that flagging changes would wallpaper stores, and leveraged the market's use of shrinkflation as proof that current disclosures fail.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won a close debate by making the decisive clash easier to judge: unit price already supplies the present-value comparison that decides the aisle purchase, while PRO’s flag is backward-looking and, by PRO’s own answer, is meant to trigger switching/resentment even when the shrunk pack can still be best per-unit value. CON also forced the “line of code” claim into conceded “substantially similar” adjudication and non-neutrality between two legitimate cost responses. PRO’s information-failure frame and inattention dilemma remained sharp, but never fully overcame that comparative weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON A4.5–A4.6 turning PRO’s B4.3 “switches/buys competitor” answer into an admission of resentment-driven distortion versus present unit-value choice.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that the proposed shrinkflation flag adds backward-looking grievance rather than decision-relevant information, while steadily expanding implementation costs from a "line of code" into complex regulatory adjudication. B answered pressure more cleanly, reframed the clash as "information improving the purchase versus provoking a reaction," and showed that unit pricing already answers the shopper's actual question. Side A's case remained live in principle but could not fully discharge the baseline burden or neutralize the "distortion by resentment" critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which captured Side A's admission that the flag changes behavior by making shoppers "switch, buys the competitor," reframed it as steering consumers to potentially worse purchases through resentment, and cleanly resolved the pressure questions by distinguishing scarce attention from zero attention.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive utility clash. PRO showed shrinkflation hides increases, but CON showed unit price already answers the purchase question "best value today" and the historical flag adds a backward-looking grievance. The admission in B4.3 that the flag's purpose is to make shoppers "switch" even when the shrunk brand is still cheaper per gram became distortion-by-resentment, plus scarce-attention dilution and implementation creep from "line of code" to "substantially similar" adjudication across tens of thousands of SKUs. Enforcing prominent, uniform unit pricing solves the real gap without the neutrality and gaming costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.6: no contradiction between scarcity and unit-price enforcement, neutrality between price and size routes, and turning B4.3 into proof that the flag steers to worse purchases via resentment while exposing the machinery cost conceded in B4.1.
