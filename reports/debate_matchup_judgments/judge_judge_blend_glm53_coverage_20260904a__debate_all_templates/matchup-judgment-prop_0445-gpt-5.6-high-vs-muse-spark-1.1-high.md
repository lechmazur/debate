### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases. A's "security attaches to the person, not the job" frame was clean and consistently pressed, especially the charge that CON's model abandons the 90% informal workforce. But B's case had the stronger institutional grounding and rebuttal trajectory: it anchored the sequencing critique in the real pattern of India's 2020 labour codes (flexibility passed, social security boards left largely inert), and it drew a genuinely useful distinction between employer-liable rights (verifiable in one factory, claimable without fiscal capacity) and treasury-financed universal benefits (requiring continuous budgets that shrink first in downturns). When A responded with a "statutory commencement condition" locking flexibility to operational protections, B's rebuttal that instantly-vesting flexibility creates a lobby no court will later unwind (since suspension would be chaotic) was a sharp, underrated political-economy point that A's closing did not squarely rebut — A instead generalized to "government may fail either way," which dodges the specific vesting/lock-in mechanism B raised. B's constructive alternative (expand social security additively) remained thinner than A's detailed portability mechanism, which is a real weakness, but B's critique did enough damage to A's central enforcement promise that A's proposal looked less credible by the end than B's defense of the status quo's narrower but more verifiable protections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's 2020-labour-code citation (B2.1) plus the "no court will suspend it once firms have invested" argument (B4.4) against A's statutory commencement-condition fix (A4.1) was the most decisive exchange; A's closing answered it only by generalizing to symmetric state-failure risk rather than confronting the lock-in dynamic directly.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side A won by consistently grounding the debate in the political and economic realities of a surplus-labor market with a narrow tax base. A's argument that courts would not practically suspend flexibility once implemented neutralized B's 'statutory lock' defense, and A convincingly showed that costless non-renewal bypasses anti-retaliation rights.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's dismantling of B's statutory commencement lock in A4.4, noting that once capital invests based on flexibility, no court will suspend it when funds dry up because doing so would cause legal chaos.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a persistent institutional-political economy trap: flexibility vests immediately and creates lobbying power, while universal portable protection for India's vast informal workforce requires fiscal and administrative capacity the state has repeatedly failed to deliver. CON's use of the 2020 labor codes and the narrow tax base grounded this skepticism, while its analysis of power asymmetries—showing that fixed-term workers will not enforce safety or wage claims when non-renewal is costless—undermined PRO's claim that portable benefits could substitute for job security. PRO's statutory commencement lock was theoretically tidy but could not overcome CON's demonstration that once firms invest assuming churn, suspension becomes politically and legally chaotic. PRO maintained a competitive frame, but CON sharpened the decisive clash more effectively across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's attack on PRO's synchronization defense using the 2020 labor codes example and the political economy argument that flexibility instantaneously vests while universal protection remains fiscally and administratively out of reach, which PRO's statutory lock could not convincingly answer.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides built plausible, well-grounded cases without fake citations, but B's rebuttals did more structural damage. B repeatedly turned A's own language against it ("jobs first is how you fund security" as a concession that the trade is speculative, not simultaneous), correctly identified that A's "double lock" answer to the funding pressure question actually undercuts A's core necessity claim (if general taxation can fund the floor first, flexibility is not needed to finance it), and exposed a genuine internal contradiction in A's anti-churn safeguards: if strictly enforced they reintroduce the rigidity A says is the problem, and if avoidable they fail to protect workers. A never resolved this dilemma in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, leaving B's strongest point live and unanswered at the end. A's opening and mechanism design (conditional commencement, portable benefits, contractor formalization) were compelling and specific, and its pressure questions were reasonable, but its closing mostly reasserted the trade rather than repairing the exposed contradiction. B also correctly followed the A1/A2 answer format and used the certain-loss-vs-speculative-gain framing consistently and effectively through to the final speech, giving it the cleaner path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point [B4.4], sharpened in Closing [B5.2]: A's anti-churn cap "either restores the rigidity PRO says suppresses hiring... or churn remains," a contradiction A never resolved.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing a core contradiction in Side B's case. When Side B introduced strict anti-churn rules in Rebuttal 2 to prevent abuse, Side A decisively pointed out that this recreates the exact hiring rigidity Side B claimed was stifling formalization. Side A also effectively proved that if general revenues can fund the initial safety net, the trade-off of weakening labor rights is unnecessary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which brilliantly turned Side B's proposed "double lock" and anti-churn rules into evidence that Side B's model either fails to provide flexibility or fails to prevent precarity.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing the proposed trade was unnecessary and internally strained. It turned PRO's admission that general revenues could initially fund portable benefits into a decisive comparative: India can build universal security and mandate parity benefits without surrendering dismissal protections. CON also exposed an unresolved dilemma in PRO's anti-churn safeguards—if enforced, they reintroduce the rigidity PRO blamed for informality; if avoided, churn and precarity persist. PRO's narrative about the excluded 90% remained powerful, but CON demonstrated that easier dismissal neither guarantees contractor conversion nor addresses the self-employed, while the certain loss of existing protections outweighed the speculative formalization dividend.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turn that PRO's two-term permanence cap and rising non-renewal costs either restore the rigidity PRO claims suppresses hiring or are easily circumvented, leaving workers no more secure—undermining the core flexibility-for-security bargain.
