### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): CON (B) won by forcing the debate onto the decisive comparative question — "compared to what?" — and PRO never adequately answered it. B's sharpest move was exposing PRO's unfalsifiable framework (untranslated knowledge is "invisible," translated knowledge is "dissected," leaving no possible good outcome), a charge PRO never rebutted. B also turned PRO's own Latin analogy against them (vernaculars absorbed Latin's vocabulary before displacing it, mirroring English's role today) and, most decisively, showed that PRO's own proposed alternative (multilingual regional networks) still requires a connector language, which is self-defeating for PRO's case. PRO's arguments about epistemic hierarchy, coercion, and cognitive flattening were plausible and well-articulated in the opening, but subsequent rebuttals largely restated the same claims without resolving the "no viable alternative" and "unfalsifiable harm" problems B raised. B's pressure questions were also better exploited: PRO's answers (a hypothetical decentralized multilingual system) were directly weaponized by B in the following speech, while PRO's own questions to B, though sharp, were answered convincingly (Latin's absorption dynamic, non-native scientists seizing global agendas). B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly; A's closing largely reasserted priors without resolving the comparative burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 exposing the "invisible vs. dissected" trap [B2.1], later reinforced in Rebuttal 2 by showing PRO's own alternative (regional multilingual networks) secretly requires a shared connector language [B4.5] — this pairing dismantled PRO's core framework and was never squarely answered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by offering the clearer comparison: English can add global access without deleting local languages, while institutional gatekeeping is not identical to language itself. Side A identified real burdens and domain loss, but repeatedly asserted that translation necessarily destroys knowledge without adequately establishing that mechanism or weighing it against wider reach, funding, and collaboration. Its multilingual alternative was plausible but underdeveloped. B's occasional false dichotomy and one erroneous reference label reduced the margin, but did not overturn its stronger comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B most decisively exposed the tension between A calling untranslated knowledge invisible and translated knowledge dissected. A answered with reciprocal multilingual exchange, but never showed why translation under that model preserves essence while English translation necessarily destroys it, allowing B to characterize A's standard as close to unfalsifiable.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (A) won the weighing. PRO (B) showed real costs - language tax, domain loss, conceptual flattening - but CON made the decisive comparative move: additive bilingualism, non-native majority now driving English science, languages raiding English for terms like Italian raided Latin, and agency. The unfalsifiable trap [A2.1][A3.1][A5.2] that PRO counts both invisibility [B1.3] and dissected translation [B2.7] as harm was never cleanly resolved; PRO's A1 answer [B4.1] still presupposes a connector language [A4.5][A5.1]. Compared to fragmentation vs a connector, CON gave the clearer reason that harm is less than good.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1 / A3.1 trap that PRO's framework makes erasure unfalsifiable - invisible if untranslated, dissected if translated - leaving no win condition, crystallized in closing [A5.2].

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A built a cleaner, more disciplined framework from the start—dominance-as-hierarchy versus dominance-as-shared-tool, and irreversible harm versus substitutable benefit—and sustained it across every round. A's most damaging move was turning B's own TCP/IP/metric analogy back on them (protocols coordinate without ranking users by birthplace), a question B never squarely answered beyond asserting that human language "inevitably" bundles prestige with utility—a claim that remains circular through the closing. A also neutralized B's strongest point (translation funding entrenches wealthy institutions) by drawing a clear distinction between institutional costs that scale and shrink versus irreplaceable individual years spent achieving fluency, which B never fully rebutted. B's case was coherent and made real points about equity and practical infrastructure, but repeated the same "bridge/fragmentation" framing without escaping the core distinction A kept pressing, and its closing mostly reasserted rather than resolved the clash. A's closing crystallized the decisive asymmetry more effectively than B's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's use of B's own TCP/IP and metric-system analogy against them in the Pressure Questions/Rebuttal 2 exchange (Q1 in A3.1, answered weakly in B4.1) was the turning point—B never explained why a coordination protocol must also monopolize prestige, and A's closing exploited that gap directly.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A compellingly established unequal language burdens and risks of domain loss, but did not fully carry the comparative burden that these outweigh global coordination benefits. Its decisive premise was that English could remain the practical universal default while losing the prestige and institutional dominance attached to that role. Side B persuasively challenged this as unstable under network effects and showed that A's translation alternative could shift gatekeeping onto underfunded institutions. Because that feasibility problem remained live, B retained the stronger account of why dominance enables unusually large and ongoing benefits.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest response was that A could not preserve a universally expected English channel while simply removing the educational and professional priority generated by that channel. A's protocol analogies were memorable, but they did not resolve B's point that human institutions rationally reward competence in the language where global exchange occurs.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): B won by holding the dominance vs coordination line. From opening they conceded a shared language is useful and attacked the prestige monopoly (toll booth, domain loss, irreversible loss). A defended only the value of a common standard and ultimately argued default and dominance are inseparable [A4.3], conceding the harm while claiming inevitability. B's R2 answer showed benefits survive reform while harms compound, and A never resolved the TCP/IP prestige question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 A1/A2 – reframing personal English fluency as regressive individual tax vs institutional cost, and exposing that A's bridge/megaphone arguments only justify a default, not the bundled hierarchy of hiring, promotion and universal knowledge.
