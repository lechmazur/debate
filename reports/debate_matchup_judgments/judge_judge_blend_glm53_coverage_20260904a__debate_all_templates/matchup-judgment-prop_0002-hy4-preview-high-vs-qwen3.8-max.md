### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A won by keeping the comparison correctly marginal: legalization need not make every new unit cheap or eliminate other barriers; it need only produce enough additional housing over twenty years to leave rents lower than under stricter zoning. Side B identified real frictions involving financing, redevelopment costs, and displacement, but repeatedly imposed an excessive burden of proving immediate, broadly distributed affordability. Its concession that modest additions could slow some rents substantially supported A’s proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange answered “permission is not production” by arguing that modest turnover accumulating over two decades still beats a counterfactual where those specific infill projects remain illegal. Side B’s claim that the baseline was not zero missed that A meant zero marginal homes from prohibited density, not zero construction citywide.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A won by defending the core supply-and-demand mechanism and rebutting Side B's displacement and land-value objections more effectively. Side A's argument that the true counterfactual in high-demand neighborhoods is a luxury teardown—not a preserved affordable cottage—sharply undermined Side B's teardown concern, while its division of land costs across multiple units neutralized the "upzoning raises land values" critique. Side B raised legitimate frictions around credit and construction, but overburdened the proposition by demanding proof of perfectly cheap, fast, and widely distributed units rather than a marginal affordability improvement. Side A showed that even imperfect, credit-constrained infill over two decades would leave cities more elastic and less scarcity-driven than permanent single-family illegality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal of the land-value and displacement argument in Rebuttal 2 and Closing—showing that per-unit land costs fall when divided across four homes and that the counterfactual baseline is a luxury single-family teardown, not a naturally affordable cottage—was the most decisive exchange, as it dissolved Side B's primary objection that upzoning destroys cheap housing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO won by holding the counterfactual: same demand and inequality, but by-right duplex/fourplex/ADU legal. B showed modest take-up over 20 years compounds to hundreds of thousands of net homes, and per-unit land cost falls because one lot's rent is split four ways. CON's permission≠production and land-price-up arguments were dampened but not decisive, and CON's "loss of cheap cottage" was reversed by B's closing teardown point - without density the lot becomes one luxury house, not a preserved affordable cottage. Filtering then explains citywide affordability. CON demanded perfection (cheap, fast, perfectly distributed) rather than beating the illegal baseline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B5.1-B5.3 closing reframing that the true baseline is a teardown luxury single-family mansion, so a by-right fourplex is three net homes and cheaper per unit - which collapsed CON's natural-affordability and per-home land-cost claims from A4.3 and A4.5, reinforced by B4.1 rent-split arithmetic.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing the relevant marginal claim: earlier legalization need not solve every housing problem, only permit enough additional supply to make cities more affordable than the scarcity baseline. Side B’s credit-crash argument was temporary rather than a twenty-year answer, while its capitalization argument never showed that higher parcel values fully cancel added units, vacancies, competition, and filtering. Its concession that density bans create scarcity rents further weakened its categorical denial.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange answered capitalization by distinguishing total parcel value from per-home land cost and emphasizing broad upzoning across competing parcels. Side B asserted that the development option would be priced in, but did not establish the perfect or near-perfect offset needed to defeat even a modest affordability improvement.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO successfully defended the core supply-side mechanism against CON's sophisticated critiques. While CON landed real pressure with the 2008 crash and land-value capitalization arguments, PRO showed the financial crisis was a temporary shock within a twenty-year window and demonstrated that total land capitalization would paradoxically prevent all development—a claim contradicted by observed building in more permissive metros. PRO's Sun Belt examples survived CON's attempt to attribute their affordability solely to greenfield elasticity, since those metros' permissive entitlement regimes are precisely the kind of legal density the motion envisions. By the closing, PRO had established that earlier density rights would have created competition, filtering, and vacancies, directly lowering rent burdens relative to the status quo. CON could not show why scarcity would outperform supply over two decades.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The land-cost capitalization exchange (A2.4/B2.3/B3.2/A4.2/B4.3-4), where PRO demonstrated that upzoning spreads land costs across multiple units and market competition prevents total rent capture by landowners, while CON's theory that land values would fully absorb all gains proved too strong and contradicted observable construction patterns.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO maintained that zoning is the durable ceiling and credit shock temporary, so earlier legalization would have meant more sites, more builders, and more filtering over 20 years. CON relied on 2008 erasing pipeline and full capitalization of land value, but PRO answered both: Sun Belt examples show high-demand but buildable metros absorbed migration with calmer rents, and competition among many parcels prevents one owner capturing 4x rent. CON conceded banning density creates scarcity rents, strengthening PRO's core comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's R2 land-cost answer (B4.3-B4.4) that a 4-unit lot trades at discount to four lots due to risk/carry and competition undermines CON's perfect-capitalization claim, combined with Q2 exposing that banning density can't lower rents.
