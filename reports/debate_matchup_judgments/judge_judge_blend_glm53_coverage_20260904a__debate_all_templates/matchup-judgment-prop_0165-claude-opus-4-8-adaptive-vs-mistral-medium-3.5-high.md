### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins a clear but competitive debate. Side B had a solid public-access and anti-gatekeeping case, especially on Tesla control and public infrastructure. But Side A made the sharper comparative argument: voluntary convergence and neutral standards processes are already producing interoperability, while a government mandate risks freezing an inferior standard, raising compliance barriers, and turning capture into law. Side B answered that standards can evolve, but did not fully resolve A's displacement point or explain why mandates are necessary when standards bodies and procurement conditions can address much of the concern.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's most decisive exchange was the pressure and second rebuttal around a hypothetical earlier CCS mandate: if government had locked in the incumbent standard, a superior plug could not easily displace it. Side B's standards-can-update answer was relevant but did not fully neutralize the freeze-and-capture turn.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): Side B decisively won by separating the desirable outcome (interoperability) from the flawed instrument (government mandates), effectively demonstrating that a mandate would have locked in an inferior standard in the US market while market forces organically solved the fragmentation issue.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal weaponized Side A's defense of CCS updates, pointing out that neutral standards bodies—not government statutes—drive technological evolution, and a mandate would have locked in the "loser" standard.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that the market is already producing open interoperability voluntarily, whereas a government mandate would have frozen an inferior connector standard and prevented the superior NACS from displacing the incumbent. PRO never escaped the "lock in the loser" trap: they conceded that standards evolve through technical bodies, not legislatures, but could not explain how a mandated connector could legally be displaced by a better one. CON also neutralized PRO's public-funding argument by showing that procurement conditions can ensure fair access without outlawing competing standards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal (Turn 4), where they turned PRO's examples against them by showing NACS was already moving to a neutral standards body voluntarily, that CCS evolved through engineering consensus rather than statute, and framing the U.S. NACS convergence against Europe's CCS mandate as proof that self-correction happens in freer markets while mandates risk entrenching losers.
