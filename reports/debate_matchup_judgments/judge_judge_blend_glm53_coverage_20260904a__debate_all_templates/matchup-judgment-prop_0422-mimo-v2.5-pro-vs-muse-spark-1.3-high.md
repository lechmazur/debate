### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the issue as a non-negotiable safety requirement rather than a monetized consumer preference. Side A effectively used Side B's own logic—that families should just buy standard fares—to prove that airlines are currently charging the exact "premium fare penalty" the resolution opposes. Side A also cleanly defused Side B's edge cases during the pressure rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1, which pointed out that Side B's proposed solution of having families 'select standard — not basic — fares' is literally the premium fare penalty the mandate is meant to ban.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by making the decisive clash clearer: once CON conceded a toddler should not sit alone “by design,” monetizing adjacency stopped looking like ordinary inventory and looked like a safety paywall. PRO kept the remedy narrow (same-booking standard seats, gate swaps) and consistently weighed safety against seat-fee revenue; CON’s best route—cross-subsidy, gaming, and mandate overbreadth—was real but undercut by a clipped opening, over-reliance on “already rare/voluntary,” and weaker answers once pressed on what is lost by seating already-paid tickets together.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s exploitation of CON’s direct answer that a 3-year-old should not be seated alone for lack of a seat fee, converting that concession into the closing “safety is for sale” frame.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing that seating a young child apart from an adult is a safety failure, not a mere preference, and trapping Side A into conceding that a toddler should not sit alone by design. That concession collapsed Side A's effort to equate family adjacency with aisle-seat convenience. Side B also exposed that Side A's proposed solution—booking standard rather than basic fares—was itself the premium-fare penalty the motion condemns. Side A's economic counterarguments relied on unsubstantiated claims about base-fare hikes and never overcame the fact that adjacent standard seats on the same reservation cost the carrier nothing extra to assign. Side A's clipped opening further weakened its ability to set up a full alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's direct answer to Side B's Pressure Question Q1, where it conceded that a 3-year-old should not be seated alone by design, which Side B exploited to prove that separation is a safety issue and that charging to prevent it is coercion, not inventory management.
