### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but A's "evidentiary trap" dilemma—that requiring prior proof of interconnection before serious scrutiny lets the very opacity of proprietary systems shield sophisticated coordination—was the sharpest and most consequential clash of the debate. A's clarification that "equal scrutiny" means equal investigative seriousness, not automatic liability, defused B's strongest worry (over-inclusion/burden on innocent tools) while still forcing B into a position where "graduated scrutiny" converged functionally with A's rule, leaving B's resistance looking increasingly like a labeling dispute rather than a substantive alternative. B's closing reasserted the distinction ("power to investigate ≠ license to treat as coordinators") but never showed what practical protection the narrower framing actually buys once B concedes compulsory investigation of architecture and data flows is available without prior proof of pooling. A's Pressure Questions were also more incisive, pinning B into admitting its threshold reduces to case-by-case discretion, while A's clipped opening was a real but modest execution cost that did not undermine the overall trajectory of the case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's "evidentiary trap" dilemma in Rebuttal 2/Closing (A4.3, A5.3)—arguing that CON's graduated-scrutiny standard either collapses into PRO's rule or leaves hidden coordination unreachable—was the decisive exchange, since B's response (relabeling investigative power vs. categorical license) conceded practical convergence without fully rebutting the substantive risk A identified.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B won the decisive clash over what the motion's equivalence requires. A argued strongly that function beats form, equal scrutiny is not automatic guilt, and opacity creates an access trap if interconnection must be shown first. B answered by keeping mechanism prior to posture: shared use can justify real compulsory inquiry into design, contracts, and data flows, but a supplier relationship is not yet direct price coordination. A admitted common subscription alone triggers the direct-coordination posture and never showed B's evidence-led escalation leaves coordinating hubs unreachable, so the categorical leap remained undertied.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2: same steps only when the same information exchange/influence is present; A's common-use trigger therefore overreaches and confuses investigative power with justification for treating landlords as direct coordinators.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO built the clearer path to victory by showing that shared algorithmic rent-setting is functionally equivalent to a common coordination channel and that CON's graduated alternative either collapses into equivalent scrutiny or leaves sophisticated coordination shielded by opacity. CON never resolved how investigators access landlord-level records without the posture PRO advocates, and PRO's comparative weighing of temporary innocent burden versus persistent tenant harm was left unanswered. PRO's clipped opening is a real cost, but not enough to overcome the decisive evidentiary trap established in later rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's evidentiary-trap dilemma in Rebuttal 2 and Closing: CON cannot access the landlord contracts, communications, and reliance evidence needed to prove coordination without first adopting the serious scrutiny PRO proposes, making graduated scrutiny either toothless or a rebranded version of equal treatment.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B's core distinction—reciprocal assurance/commitment versus unilateral reliance on a common vendor—proved more resilient under pressure. A's strongest move was forcing B to concede that pooled confidential data justifies escalated scrutiny, but A's own answers to the pressure questions (explicitly denying any "categorical public-data exemption" while simultaneously promising "shorter inquiry" for innocent-looking systems) created an internal tension B exploited effectively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing. B's closing dilemma—that "equivalent scrutiny" either collapses into ordinary predicate-based antitrust law (making the motion redundant) or treats shared-tool use like observed coordination (making it unjustified)—was never squarely resolved by A, who retreated to vague language about "the same coordination test" without clarifying which horn of the dilemma the motion actually endorses. B's opening was flagged as clipped, a real execution cost, but the substantive content was almost entirely delivered before the cut, and B recovered fully in subsequent speeches, ultimately landing the more decisive analytical framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3-B4.5, where B quotes A's own listed evidentiary requirements (vendor promises, monitoring, reliance on rivals) back against A to show that once those facts exist, the investigation is already evidence-based rather than software-triggered—collapsing PRO's position into either redundancy or overreach. A's Closing never cleanly escapes this trap.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won the decisive clash over what “as if … coordinating prices directly” does. PRO kept a coherent scrutiny-not-guilt line and a sticky intermediary analogy, but repeatedly conceded that shared software plus adoption alone is not an agreement and that liability still needs facilitating facts. CON converted those concessions into a clean fork: if equivalent scrutiny means ordinary evidence-driven inquiry, the motion adds nothing; if it means treating tool users like rivals caught exchanging prices, it discards the predicate of reciprocal assurance. That comparative frame survived to the end and made the motion easier to reject than accept.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3–B4.5: PRO’s own list of needed evidence (vendor promises, monitoring, confidential transmission, reliance on rivals) is exactly when heightened scrutiny becomes justified, not a reason to treat shared-tool use itself as equivalent to direct coordination.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON showed that treating shared software use "as if" it were direct coordination either overreaches by subjecting innocent public-data tools to cartel-level suspicion or adds nothing if investigators must still find independent evidence of reciprocal commitment. PRO’s admission that there is no categorical exemption for public-data tools and that software-plus-adoption alone does not prove agreement left its standard vulnerable to CON’s redundancy-or-overreach dilemma. CON therefore built the cleaner path to victory by sharpening the evidentiary distinction between unilateral advice and reciprocal coordination.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal and closing crystallized that PRO’s own list of facilitating facts (vendor promises, monitoring, communications) was exactly the evidence-based standard CON advocated, rendering the motion redundant once those facts exist and overbroad when they do not.
