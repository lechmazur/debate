### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and handled the Q&A format cleanly, but CON (A) more effectively exploited the central tension in PRO's position: PRO insisted a cap is not a ban while conceding ("some deed-dependent capital will indeed leave") that ownership-linked patient capital would exit once the ceiling binds—exactly the capital CON identified as financing irrigation, drainage, and lease-back transitions. CON also introduced and sustained an unrebutted mechanism: removing institutional bidders does not make land affordable for young farmers, it removes lease-to-own financing and pushes sellers toward all-cash sales, entrenching wealthy incumbents rather than broadening ownership. PRO's strongest late move—reframing the debate around irreversibility of concentration versus replaceable capital—was a good weighing frame, but it arrived mainly in the closing and was never squared against CON's incumbent-consolidation counter-mechanism, leaving that route live for CON. PRO's line-drawing defense (analogizing to bank capital thresholds) was reasonable but never became concrete, while CON's "toothless or de facto ban" framing exposed a real definitional weakness. On balance CON's rebuttals did more direct damage to PRO's live claims than PRO's did to CON's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 seizing on PRO's concession that "deed-dependent capital will indeed leave" and tying it directly back to the irrigation/lease-back argument, combined with the unanswered claim that shallower buyer pools push sellers toward all-cash sales that favor wealthy incumbents over young farmers.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): PRO won the central comparison on reversibility and cumulative control. CON showed deed-dependent patient capital would leave and that fewer buyers can help wealthy incumbents, but never answered why tenant rights, taxes, or antitrust would prevent a dominant institutional share from forming across many lawful purchases. PRO's Pressure Q2 exposed that gap, and PRO's answer that a cap still permits lending/partnership below a ceiling preserved a live path that ownership is not a prerequisite for finance, making the tradeoff asymmetric: replaceable financing form vs irreversible concentration.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Exchange on deed-dependent capital: B4.1 conceding patient capital leaves because it needs appreciation, and A4.1/A4.5 reframing that departure as the point – buying existing land transfers rent rather than creates irrigation – which crystallized the ownership vs finance distinction.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a clear, decisive weighing mechanism in the later rounds: the asymmetry between irreversible land concentration and replaceable capital structures. Side A effectively neutralized Side B's primary economic objection by distinguishing between purchasing existing assets (which merely transfers rent and appreciation) and providing productive capital (which can be done through lending, insurance, and partnerships below the cap). Side B made strong, plausible points about the need for patient capital and the risk of wealthy incumbents absorbing the land, but failed to overcome Side A's reversibility framework and the argument that a cap targets cumulative structural control rather than banning agricultural finance entirely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing that purchasing existing farmland merely transfers rent without creating new productive capacity, effectively dismantling Side B's claim that institutional ownership is necessary to fund long-term land improvements.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): CON's central line—that ownership identity is a poor, evadable proxy for conduct, and that direct regulation of soil/lease/competition outcomes covers every acre regardless of who holds the deed—was never fully neutralized. CON's Q2 pressure question (exempt farming corporations replicating the same harms) was the debate's sharpest strike, and PRO's answer (A2) conceded that environmental/labor rules are still needed for exempt owners, which undercuts PRO's own 'incentive floor' claim and validates CON's coverage argument. PRO's strongest material—ownership as leverage, deeds dictating contracts—was rhetorically vivid but never closed the gap on why a cap uniquely secures stewardship outcomes when incumbents, farming corporations, and restructured entities can replicate the same harms while remaining eligible. CON's closing crystallized this coverage asymmetry cleanly, while PRO's closing largely restated the leverage framing without resolving the exemption/evasion problem raised at Q1/Q2 and in A4.3-A4.4. Both sides answered the Pressure Questions with proper A1:/A2: format, but CON's answers to PRO's questions (A4.1-A4.2) more directly conceded and refined its position, whereas PRO's answers (B4.1-B4.2) admitted the threshold is a 'legislative detail' and conceded that non-exempt harmful owners still need separate rules—effectively handing CON the coverage argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q2 pressure question about exempt farming corporations replicating the same harms, and PRO's answer conceding that environmental/labor rules are still necessary for those exempt owners—this exchange exposed that the cap does not secure the resilience outcomes it promises even in its best case.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won the coverage and non-arbitrariness clash. PRO never answered that a cap mandates no sale to young/local farmers, no diversity, soil care, or tenancy security, so even perfect enforcement can fail resilience while still benefiting wealthy incumbents. PRO's A1 answer conceded threshold/geography are "legislative details" and its incentive-floor claim that farming corporations only profit from harvest collapsed under B's rebuttal that both types profit from appreciation. B's conduct-based alternative attaches to observable harm and directly addresses PRO's own concession that environmental/labor rules remain necessary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3-B4.5 - merger-review analogy fails, no threshold or market, incentive floor fictional, and cap changes bidder label not contract while direct rules bind every acre.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B wins by effectively dismantling Side A's core mechanism and exposing the structural flaws in the proposed policy. While Side A argued persuasively that ownership dictates incentives and leverage, Side B successfully proved that an identity-based cap is underinclusive (exempt farming corporations and wealthy incumbents can engage in the same extractive behaviors) and fails to guarantee the positive outcomes PRO desires. Side B's rebuttals in the second half, particularly dismantling Side A's claim that farming corporations "only profit from harvest" and exposing the flaws in Side A's merger-review analogy, decisively neutralized Side A's live routes to victory. Side B's advocacy for direct conduct regulation provided a cleaner, more comprehensive alternative that directly targets the harms rather than guessing at the morality of the deed-holder.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it systematically dismantled Side A's answers to the pressure questions, specifically destroying the claim that exempt farming corporations only profit from harvest and pointing out that merger review assesses market effects, not identity.
