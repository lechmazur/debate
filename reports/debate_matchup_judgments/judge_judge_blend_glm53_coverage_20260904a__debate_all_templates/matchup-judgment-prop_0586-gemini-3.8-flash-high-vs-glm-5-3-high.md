- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won the central comparative clash by explaining that sector-specific wage shocks allow consumers to move toward uncovered substitutes, concentrating revenue and employment losses in the targeted industry. Side A somewhat overstated the universality of minimum-wage cost effects, so the win remains competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to B4.1 was decisive: if customers have no cheaper substitute under a broad floor, demand is less elastic and firms can pass through more of the cost.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2 turning CON's automation and incumbent-bias points back onto the statutory floor, then B4's elasticity reply that a $17 national shock still lands fully on the same thin-margin sectors without phase-in or revisit.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won because it established and sustained a decisive comparative mechanism: sector-specific wage mandates distort relative prices, prompting consumers to flee to exempt substitutes and triggering concentrated demand loss, layoffs, and automation in the targeted industry. PRO conceded that sector-level demand is more elastic than aggregate demand but never squarely rebutted why that elasticity would not cause greater employment losses under sectoral targeting. CON’s use of the California fast-food council as a real-world illustration of carve-outs and adjustment costs, combined with its clear microeconomic story, gave it the cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s development of the product-substitution/demand-leakage argument (A2 and A4), showing that targeting a single industry causes consumers to shift to untargeted alternatives, shrinking sector revenue and forcing concentrated layoffs and automation that a uniform wage floor avoids.
