### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently and engaged the real clash, but CON held a clear edge on the decisive issue. PRO's strongest moves—holistic coverage, the "zero hours" hour-slashing argument, and the enforcement-crisis point—were all real and landed, but CON had a consistent structural response: sectoral bargaining requires the same political capacity (legislation, coverage rules, enforcement funding, representative selection) that PRO dismisses as a "legislative fantasy" when applied to direct statutes. B3.1's pressure question crystallized this inconsistency, and PRO's A1 answer—distinguishing "one structural framework" from "dozens of separate statutes"—was the weakest moment of PRO's case, because CON correctly identified in B4.4 that even "one framework" still must resolve every hard subsidiary question (sector definitions, gig/contractor coverage, representative legitimacy, deadlock rules) that makes fragmented-industry protection difficult. CON's misclassification response (B4.1) was also effective: fixing contractor exclusion requires the same legal intervention either way, so it is not a structural advantage for sectoral bargaining. PRO never dislodged CON's central framing—reliability under imperfect conditions—which CON carried cleanly from opening through closing. PRO's case was stronger on worker experience and holistic coverage but weaker on the foundational question of how a system reaches the least organized worker when the political conditions are hardest. CON's two clipped speeches are a real format cost but appeared to truncate concluding sentences rather than core arguments, minimally affecting the comparative assessment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B3.1 pressure question was the most decisive exchange: "If passing direct wage, scheduling, and safety laws is a 'legislative fantasy,' what political process creates mandatory sectoral bargaining, chooses representatives for nonunion high-turnover workers, and funds enforcement without the same fantasy?" PRO's answer distinguished a single enabling framework from multiple disjointed statutes, but CON's B4.4 follow-through correctly showed that "one framework" still requires all the same subsidiary political decisions—who sits at the table, whether contractors count, how deadlocks resolve—that make fragmented-industry protection hard. This exchange materially damaged PRO's most important attack line and left it partially conceded for the rest of the debate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by more convincingly showing wage hikes are undermined by employer adaptations like hour cuts and misclassification in fragmented industries, while sectoral bargaining supplies institutional mechanisms and direct pressure answers; Side B's simplicity emphasis did not neutralize those loopholes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answers on one structural framework versus multiple statutes plus the zero-hours example, which sharpened the core clash on enforcement reliability.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by more effectively proving that in fragmented service industries specifically, repeated minimum-wage hikes are structurally undermined by scheduling manipulation, subcontracting, and non-wage exploitation. While Side B landed strong arguments about simplicity and universal coverage, its case relied on supplementing wage hikes with additional statutory rights—a combination that Side A successfully framed as politically unrealistic and less coherent than sectoral bargaining's comprehensive, sector-tailored approach. Side A's "zero hours" rebuttal proved the most damaging exchange, crystallizing why a simple wage floor fails the very workers it targets when employers can evade it through fragmentation tactics.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal demonstrating that a higher minimum wage multiplied by zero hours (due to shift-cutting or subcontracting) equals zero dollars, directly undermining Side B's simplicity argument in the context of fragmented industries.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won primarily because its core comparative claim — that non-wage abuses (unpaid travel, chaotic scheduling, misclassification, subcontracting) are the dominant harms in fragmented service work, and minimum-wage hikes leave them entirely intact — was never adequately answered by Side B. Side B's best response in Rebuttal 2 was that expensive labor "forces businesses to consolidate hours," which is the debate's weakest answer: it does not touch misclassification, subcontracting, or unpaid time, which are structural evasion routes rather than simple inefficiencies that higher wages price out. Side A's framing — "simple" is not "sufficient" — held throughout. On CON's strongest points, the balance is closer. The enforcement-complexity and boundary-war arguments were real, but Side A adequately responded: current simple wage rules are already evaded through contractor status and franchising, and sector rules can be defined by work performed rather than storefront label. The cartelization concern was addressed by noting it is a design problem solvable through anti-domination rules, and that large firms already dominate through fragmentation. Side B never closed the loop on what happens to workers harmed by non-wage abuses under its preferred tool. The two clippings of Side A's speeches are a genuine execution cost, but the substantive content visible was still stronger comparatively. Side B's closing was adequately focused but relied on repetition rather than sharpened weighing, and its reference to "[C4.3, C4.4]" with incorrect prefixes is a minor formatting issue. Overall, Side A built and defended a cleaner path to victory on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal exchange was Side A's Pressure Question 1 (what do minimum-wage hikes do about non-wage abuses?) answered by Side B in Rebuttal 2 with "expensive labor consolidates hours." That answer is the weakest moment in the debate: it concedes the mechanism exists while offering only an indirect and speculative fix, leaving misclassification and subcontracting — which Side A identified as the core structural evasions — completely unaddressed. Side A correctly identified this gap in the closing as the key comparative difference, and it remained live and unanswered to the end.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built the stronger case by framing minimum standards to include non-wage issues like scheduling and subcontracting, showing how sectoral rules target industry-level evasion where wage hikes fall short. B's simplicity and cartel arguments were effectively addressed with design solutions for enforcement and lead-firm accountability. A answered pressure questions more directly and progressed the comparison across rounds, despite clipping issues.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2 structure) directly neutralized B's questions on bargaining table capture and reclassification arbitrage by proposing small-employer representation and work-based coverage rules, then weighing that wage hikes leave structural abuses intact.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by demonstrating that "minimum standards" in fragmented service industries encompass more than hourly wages—addressing scheduling, subcontracting, misclassification, and travel time—while persuasively arguing that sectoral bargaining regulates these structural abuses directly. Side B's defense of minimum-wage hikes rested heavily on simplicity and enforceability, but failed to convincingly rebut A's claim that a single wage floor is systematically evaded through fragmentation and does not set standards for non-wage harms. A's answers to pressure questions on corporate capture and boundary wars were more concrete and less speculative than B's counter-arguments, and A's closing effectively crystallized that CON's "simple" standard is insufficient because it misses the floor collapsing underneath workers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4), where it directly answered Side B's pressure questions on table composition and sectoral arbitrage with specific design safeguards, then pivoted to the decisive comparative claim that CON's mathematically auditable wage floor is hollow if employers evade it through unstable hours, off-the-clock work, and misclassification.
