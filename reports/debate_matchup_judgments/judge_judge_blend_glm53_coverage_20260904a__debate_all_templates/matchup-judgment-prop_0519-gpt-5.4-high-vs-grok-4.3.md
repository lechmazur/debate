### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B built the stronger path to victory by anchoring the entire debate in the motion's actual standard—"minimum standards, plural"—and then repeatedly exploiting Side A's concessions to demonstrate that the wage-hike model fails that standard. The decisive turning point came in B's first rebuttal: A had acknowledged that scheduling, travel time, and other abuses would be addressed by "separate statutes," and had defended allowing market "adjustment on benefits or hours" [A1.4]. B flipped both of these into the strongest vote reason—if your model needs a piecemeal stack of extra laws and still accepts unilateral employer clawbacks on hours and shifts, it is not the better mechanism for setting minimum standards. A never adequately neutralized this turn. A's best live argument—that fragmentation makes durable sectoral representation structurally impossible—was answered plausibly by B: sector rules aggregate above the firm level and follow the job rather than the unstable site, making them more durable for mobile workers than firm-by-firm enforcement. A's counter that voluntary flexibilities inside sectoral agreements recreate the same exploitation margins had some force, but B's answer—that inside a sector agreement flexibility is bounded and negotiated rather than unilateral—was clean enough to survive. Side A's shorter-than-cap speeches in later rounds left key burdens partially unanswered. Side B's Rebuttal 2 was clipped, which is an execution cost, but the surviving content maintained pressure. Overall, B's framing was consistently sharper, its turns were more damaging, and its answers to A's pressure questions were more direct than A's answers to B's questions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's turn in Rebuttal 1 on A's concession that "market adjustment on benefits or hours" is acceptable [B2.7]. B flipped this into the core reason the wage-only model fails the motion: a framework for minimum standards cannot be judged successful when employers can claw back gains through fewer hours, worse shifts, or insecure contracts. Side A never escaped this reframe—its later defense (that flexibility preserves employment levels) tacitly accepted the premise that the wage floor alone leaves those margins open.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by effectively leveraging the motion's phrasing of "minimum standards" (plural) to expose the limitations of Side B's one-dimensional wage hikes. Side A successfully framed B's reliance on supplementary laws and "market adjustments" as a concession that wage hikes alone fail to prevent exploitation on other margins.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized B's defense of market adjustments, framing B's "flexibility" as unilateral clawbacks by employers, compared to A's negotiated and bounded flexibility.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by framing the motion around "minimum standards" plural and showing that minimum-wage hikes alone set only one standard while leaving firms free to erode others through hours, scheduling, and subcontracting. CON's defense of non-wage "flexibility" conceded the central comparative issue, and their institutional feasibility objections were insufficient to overcome the core disadvantage of a one-dimensional tool. PRO's sharper pressure questions and more focused closing crystallized the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal exposing CON's "adjustment on benefits or hours" concession as proof that minimum-wage policy invites clawbacks rather than setting genuine minimum standards, a framing that dominated the rest of the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins on three live routes that Side B never adequately closed. First, the institutions argument: fragmented service industries lack the union density, employer associations, and clear sector boundaries that sectoral bargaining requires; Side B pointing to trade associations only covers the most organized, formal employers—precisely not the hardest cases. Second, and most decisive, the employer-veto argument: Side A correctly noted that PRO's own concessions—"reimbursement realities," small operators blocking "terms they cannot sustain"—mean thin margins become a front-end reason to bargain the minimum downward before workers ever receive it. Side B's A2 answer (operators participate in setting the floor rather than weakening it after the fact) actually confirmed the employer-leverage dynamic without neutralizing the normative objection. Third, Side A's sharpest logical turn—if PRO's remedy requires state-mandated extension orders and joint-liability rules to achieve coverage, the real engine is law, not bargaining—was never answered. Side B's closing (well under the word limit) crystallized the portable-gains argument but didn't revisit these structural weaknesses. Side A's repeated clipping was a minor execution cost that did not materially conceal any of its core reasoning. Side B made genuine points on calibration and spillover, but these did not overcome the veto and institutions problems, and PRO's own language about accommodating reimbursement limits was turned against it convincingly in Side A's closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 pivot after PRO's A2 answer: "If the real engine is state-imposed extension and joint liability, that proves law—not bargaining—is doing the work," combined with the closing line that PRO's model lets "thin margins become a reason to bargain the minimum downward before workers ever receive it." This repurposed PRO's own concessions to demonstrate that sectoral bargaining structurally subordinates minimum standards to employer approval, which is the opposite of what a minimum is supposed to do.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by effectively turning Side A's core mechanisms into liabilities. Side B convincingly argues that Side A's 'calibration' gives employers a veto over basic standards, and that Side A's reliance on joint liability relies on the very state-law enforcement it critiques.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first and second rebuttals brilliantly weaponized Side A's claim that sectoral bargaining lets small operators "block terms they cannot sustain," reframing this as an unacceptable employer veto over minimum standards.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by showing that in fragmented service industries, sectoral bargaining requires precisely the institutional capacity—clear sector boundaries, representative employer bodies, and enforceable extension—that fragmentation undermines. They effectively turned PRO's "calibration" advantage into a liability by demonstrating it invites incumbent capture and gives employers a de facto veto over floors through "reimbursement realities." CON also landed the decisive comparative turn that PRO's fixes (joint liability, extension orders) rely on state legal power rather than bargaining itself, making the statutory wage floor the simpler, more universal, and less gameable standard.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's framing of the "veto problem" and the argument that PRO's reliance on state-imposed joint liability and extension concedes the real protective force is state law, not bargaining. This sharply reframed the debate around which tool actually survives fragmentation rather than which sounds more sophisticated in theory.
