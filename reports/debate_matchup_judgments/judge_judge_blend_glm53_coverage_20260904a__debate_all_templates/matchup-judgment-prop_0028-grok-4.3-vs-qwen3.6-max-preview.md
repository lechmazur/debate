### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A established a plausible credential-inflation mechanism, but relied heavily on the asserted premise that many jobs stayed basically unchanged and that degree attainment outran real skill demand. Side B more effectively framed the economy as structurally transformed, used the persistent wage premium and absolute mobility benefits to challenge the “empty signal” story, and repeatedly weighed opportunity as access to resilient higher-skill tracks rather than preservation of scarcity value. Side A’s exclusion answer was relevant but did not fully neutralize why employers would keep rewarding graduates if the credential were mostly arbitrary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s most decisive rebuttal was that if degree requirements were mainly hollow gates for unchanged work, returns should have weakened much more as graduate supply expanded; the enduring premium and changed task environment made credential inflation look secondary rather than dominant.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A won by successfully dismantling Side B's core economic mechanism. Side A effectively argued that if jobs were truly unchanged and graduate supply was saturated, firms would not pay a persistent wage premium for an arbitrary signal. Side B failed to logically resolve why employers would pay more for unchanged work, whereas Side A provided a coherent alternative: the underlying tasks themselves evolved due to digitalization, requiring higher baseline skills.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively answered Side B's pressure questions by detailing exactly how administrative roles have changed and exposing the fatal economic flaw in Side B's wage premium argument.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory by establishing a coherent supply-side mechanism for credential inflation and persuasively reframing the wage premium as evidence of exclusion rather than productivity. While Side B offered a plausible counter-narrative of structural economic transformation, it never squarely refuted A's core claim that degree requirements escalated faster than the creation of roles genuinely demanding advanced skills, leaving A's ratchet-effect analysis intact through the final exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal, which turned the college wage premium—B's strongest piece of evidence—into support for the proposition by showing it measures the penalty for lacking the new minimum credential rather than an absolute expansion of valuable employment.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): A won by making the more motion-specific comparison: mass expansion at the margin turned degrees into costly defensive screens for many static roles, while B mostly proved that some degrees and sectors create real value. B plausibly argued task complexity and access, but did not clearly show those gains outweighed the ratchet effects, debt, and vocational displacement A emphasized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's most decisive rebuttal was separating specialized high-skill degrees from marginal credential expansion, then pressing waivers, certifications, and static jobs as evidence that many requirements function as screening rather than genuine skill needs.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the debate around the marginal student and convincingly arguing that degree requirements spread to static jobs as a screening tool. Side B's rebuttal on internal promotions exposed a weakness in Side A's claim about task complexity, and Side B's distinction between "offensive" and "defensive" opportunity was a decisive weighing mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's A1 effectively neutralized Side A's examples of new tech occupations by conceding their existence but marginalizing their scale, redirecting the focus back to the inflation of requirements in static administrative roles.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A wins by establishing that mass expansion at the margin flooded static labor markets with generic credentials, raising hiring thresholds without a proportional expansion of high-skill job creation. A's "toll versus ladder" framing cleanly resolved the central clash: the degree became a defensive screening filter and debt burden rather than an offensive mobility tool. B failed to square aggregate economic growth with the marginal graduate's experience, offered weak responses to why employers waive degree requirements, and never rebutted A's claim that vocational pathways were cannibalized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal, which distinguished genuine new occupations from credential inflation in static roles and reframed the pre-expansion alternative not as closed doors but as viable vocational ladders that mass expansion starved.
