### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO built and sustained the cleaner logical case throughout the exchange. The decisive clash was the default-inertia contradiction: CON argued that defaults stick (keeping counts visible for the majority) while simultaneously claiming that harmed users can just find the opt-out toggle. PRO identified this contradiction early in Rebuttal 1 and never let go of it. CON's "differential incentive" response in Rebuttal 2—harmed users are motivated to seek toggles while satisfied users aren't—was PRO's best pressure point and PRO turned it back on CON in the closing: if harmed users face extra work to escape a painful default, the current design taxes precisely those who are already suffering. CON's winter storm example was the most original contribution on its side but PRO correctly categorized it as an edge case that cannot justify a universal daily-use default, and CON relied on it too heavily throughout. PRO's sponsor-verification rebuttal (private analytics dashboards are more granular and harder to game than public counts) effectively deflated CON's Q2. The asymmetry of burden framing in PRO's closing—"A just default protects the vulnerable, not the comfortable"—crystallized the existing clash cleanly without introducing new material. CON's closing recycled earlier arguments without squarely addressing that burden asymmetry. PRO's progression across rounds was tighter and the core contradiction it planted in Rebuttal 1 became the organizing principle of the entire rest of the debate, which is exactly what a strong rebuttal should accomplish.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 identification of the inertia contradiction (B2.2–B2.3): CON cannot rely on default inertia to keep counts universally visible while also claiming that the very same inertia-resistant anxious users will successfully find and use the opt-out toggle. This point anchored the entire rest of the debate and CON never escaped it, ultimately handing PRO the winning framing in the closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the debate around who should bear the burden of friction (the vulnerable vs. the comfortable) and by dismantling Side A's key examples.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly flipped Side A's "differential incentive" defense, pointing out that if anxious users are the ones motivated to dig into settings, the current default essentially taxes the vulnerable while giving a free pass to the comfortable.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO wins by exposing a structural asymmetry in CON's use of default inertia and more effectively weighing whose burden matters. PRO showed that CON cannot simultaneously claim inertia will trap satisfied users in a hidden-by-default world while insisting anxious users will successfully find existing opt-out toggles. This symmetry argument, combined with the demonstration that sponsor verification relies on private analytics rather than public counts and that all functionality remains available on opt-in, leaves CON's case relying on special pleading. PRO's closing crystallized the clash cleanly: the default should protect the vulnerable rather than tax them with extra settings work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal (A4.4-A4.6) exposing that CON's default-inertia argument produces opposite outcomes for different groups, which CON's "differential incentive" response failed to fully resolve.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the stronger cumulative path. Three moves proved decisive. First, the mismatch-detection point—users can see 50k likes paired with skeptical comments and register the discrepancy; the algorithm's internal signals (watch time, saves) are invisible to users, so public counts supply an external, human-readable check that does independent work even if algorithms drive reach. PRO never cleanly answered this. Second, CON's observation that most users see algorithmic feeds, not chronological ones, gutted PRO's "12 likes penalty" argument: on an algo feed the 12 are invisible anyway, so hiding counts hands the single surviving legibility signal entirely to the algorithm PRO admitted is the real engine of harm. Third, and most decisive, CON's closing introduced the network-good/individual-good asymmetry: the credibility signal is collective and degrades for everyone when most users don't toggle back, while privacy from social comparison is individually attainable without destroying anyone else's access. That distinction resolved the defaults-coercive-vs.-trivial contradiction CON had set up in Rebuttal 2 and which PRO never fully answered. PRO's asymmetry-of-motivation response (motivated visibility-seekers will find the toggle; vulnerable users have no reason to seek harmful features) was the best single move of the debate, but CON's network-good framing answers it directly: even if motivated users find the toggle, widespread non-toggling degrades the collective signal. PRO's harm case was real and the Instagram-test reference was acceptably grounded, but the "no cost to platform utility" claim was exposed as overstated, and the closing failed to match the analytical sharpness CON brought. The clipping of PRO's first rebuttal is a minor execution cost but not the swing factor; CON simply won the live comparisons more consistently across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 on the defaults contradiction: PRO's case requires defaults to be powerful enough to trap vulnerable users in the harmful status quo, yet accessible enough that visibility-seekers won't be stranded after the flip. CON named this structural tension clearly, and PRO's asymmetry-of-motivation answer, while creative, left the core tension unresolved—because if the credibility signal is a network good that only functions when most users see it, motivated opt-ins by a minority cannot restore it. That exchange framed the debate's decisive weighing question and CON answered it more fully in the closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won decisively by exposing a structural contradiction in Side B's case regarding the power of defaults, and then brilliantly crystallizing the debate around the difference between a network good and an individual good.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing sequence perfectly dismantled Side B's logic by showing that PRO needs defaults to be coercive to justify the flip, but trivial to defend the toggle, culminating in the decisive 'network good vs. individual good' weighing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): CON wins by exposing a structural asymmetry PRO never resolved: visible metrics act as a network good whose credibility value depends on broad visibility, whereas the privacy benefit is purely individual. CON's sharp pressure questions and closing crystallized that flipping the default destroys a collective information layer for a harm that could be fixed through better opt-out design. PRO's clipped first rebuttal and failure to explain how a minority opt-in preserves crowdsourced credibility left CON's cost-benefit frame live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing framed public metrics as a network good requiring broad visibility, showing PRO's plan destroys collective credibility for an individually obtainable benefit while exposing PRO's inconsistency on whether defaults are coercive or trivial.
