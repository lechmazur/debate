### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): PRO built and sustained the stronger path to victory, but the margin is real rather than large. PRO's most decisive move was the self-defeating-donor turn: if donors withdraw support when their children stop receiving preferential treatment, that proves the transaction is a purchase, not philanthropy. CON's retreat from "mass donor exodus" to "marginal chilling effect" in Rebuttal 2 was defensible but conceded the transactional core of PRO's argument. PRO's cleanest weighing line—"projected costs are speculative; moral costs are certain"—was never squarely answered. CON's strongest counter was the reshuffling argument (seats migrate to athletes, VIPs, or other development cases rather than flowing to the disadvantaged), which PRO largely deflected with "address all inequities" rather than a direct empirical rebuttal. CON's "additive not prohibitive" analogy-collapse in Rebuttal 2 was their sharpest analytical distinction and genuinely hurt PRO's overblown segregation/footbinding comparison; PRO's defense (targeting only the logical structure, not the moral equivalence) was awkward and partial. CON's pressure question about why only "sharp limits" not abolition was effective and exposed an internal tension PRO never cleanly resolved. PRO's closing was clipped, which is an execution cost, but the visible portion maintained the core framing. Ultimately PRO wins because the core moral claim—preferences systematically advantage the already advantaged—was never refuted, only argued to be worth tolerating; CON's funding-collapse arguments remained speculative; and PRO's corruption-proves-itself turn forced a significant rhetorical retreat. The reshuffling gap and the awkward analogy defense keep the margin narrow rather than comfortable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 turn on the donor-withdrawal logic: if donors walk away when their children lose preferential treatment, that proves the donation was a purchase, not mission-driven philanthropy—precisely the corruption PRO described. CON's retreat in Rebuttal 2 from "donor exodus" to "marginal chilling effects over time" was a meaningful concession that the transactional core of the arrangement is real, even if CON reframed it as ordinary human reciprocity rather than bribery.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B more convincingly demonstrated that limiting preferences would not redirect advantages to the disadvantaged but instead risk reducing funding for access programs and merely reshuffle privileges, while Side A’s integrity argument was strong but did not sufficiently counter the material costs and lack of direct benefits. B better addressed the pressure on tradeoffs and past reforms analogies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 2 clarified the marginal impact on giving due to reciprocity and distinguished the policy from past exclusionary practices that had clear gains, weakening A's moral equivalences and forcing the debate onto practical outcomes.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully shifting the clash from abstract meritocracy to concrete institutional trade-offs. CON's distinction between restricted endowments and unrestricted annual giving, combined with the argument that marginal donor chill compounds into real scholarship cuts, provided a more mechanically grounded impact scenario than PRO's assertions about donor behavior. CON also effectively deflated PRO's moral framing by distinguishing additive preferences from prohibitive identity barriers and arguing that the policy merely reshuffles advantage while starving the aid programs that actually help low-income students. PRO landed sharp rhetorical blows on institutional corruption but never fully answered the net-benefit question for the disadvantaged, leaving CON's consequentialist weighing cleaner and more decisive. The clipped PRO closing further hampered late-stage crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 response to the segregation/footbinding analogy, which cleanly separated additive legacy preferences from prohibitive identity-based exclusions and refocused the debate on whether the motion delivers material gains to the underprivileged, effectively neutralizing PRO's moral equivalence argument and securing the impact weighing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): PRO won on the central empirical clash. CON's core case depended on the assertion that removing donor preferences would significantly reduce scholarship funding, but this causal claim was stated confidently without meaningful grounding—"development offices have learned this" is not sufficient support. PRO effectively countered that philanthropy predates formalized admissions preferences, that universities with massive endowments and robust conventional fundraising pipelines would not collapse, and that the displacement of deserving candidates is immediate and guaranteed while the scholarship benefit is speculative. The decisive exchange was PRO's A2 answer to CON's pressure question: the directionality distinction between diversity preferences is analytically clean—geographic and first-generation preferences are deliberately designed to broaden underrepresented access, while legacy preferences mechanically concentrate advantage among the already-advantaged. CON's A2 response (athletic preferences also don't broaden intellectual diversity) is somewhat deflective and does not neutralize this distinction. CON's best moment was the asymmetric risk framing in closing—if PRO is wrong, real students lose scholarships; if CON is wrong, wealthy applicants face marginally more competition—but this came late, was not fully developed earlier, and PRO had already established the underlying empirical premise is speculative. PRO's "hostage situation" framing in the closing memorably crystallized the core structural critique without introducing new evidence. CON never showed why universities specifically need to sell admission rather than simply solicit donations without transactional promises, which left their main defensive argument vulnerable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 answer in Rebuttal 2 to CON's pressure question about why some birth circumstances are legitimate diversity factors and others aren't. PRO's directionality answer—geographic and first-generation preferences are designed to broaden underrepresented access while legacy preferences concentrate advantage among the already-privileged—is analytically sharp and directly answers the question without restating the conclusion. CON's response (athletic preferences also don't broaden intellectual diversity) acknowledged the distinction rather than defeating it, leaving PRO's framework largely intact for the rest of the debate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side B wins by consistently anchoring the debate to concrete material tradeoffs—real scholarship funding generated by donor incentives versus speculative alternatives—while exposing Side A's lack of evidence on fundraising continuity. Side A built a rhetorically strong moral case on displacement and directionality but never neutralized the net-benefit claim or explained why legacy's birth-circumstance tiebreaker differs from geographic ones beyond restating the conclusion. B's pressure-question answers and closing crystallized the unproven gamble at the heart of A's position.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 exchange, which reframed the market-transaction critique as irrelevant to tangible scholarship outcomes and defended differential institutional purposes among birth-circumstance preferences without widening the debate.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by more effectively weighing the certainty of meritocratic displacement against the speculative nature of CON’s funding tradeoff. PRO’s “directionality” distinction cleanly resolved the birth-circumstance comparison, and its rebuttals consistently exposed the replaceability of donor revenue given modern university endowments and development pipelines. CON’s risk-asymmetry framing was strong but ultimately rested on an unproven causal link that PRO successfully cast doubt on. PRO’s rhetoric sharpened the stakes without sacrificing argumentative discipline, making the path to victory clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 exchange, where it directly answered CON’s pressure questions with the “directionality” framework and flipped CON’s funding tradeoff by contrasting guaranteed displacement with speculative scholarship windfalls, effectively neutralizing CON’s core utilitarian claim.
