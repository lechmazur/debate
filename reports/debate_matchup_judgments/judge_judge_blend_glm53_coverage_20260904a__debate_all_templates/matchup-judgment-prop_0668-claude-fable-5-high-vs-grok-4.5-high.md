### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won by making substitution and leverage decisive. A needed a meaningful capability gap between democratic and alternative suppliers, but largely reasserted that gap after B challenged it with the scale and sophistication of Chinese surveillance. B then forced a strong dilemma: if regimes reject conditions, they substitute and the ban prevents little; if they accept conditions, licensing retains leverage. A answered crime harms and complicity coherently, but not that central comparison. B overstated that a corrupted ban list necessarily permits wholly unconditioned sales, preventing a wider margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive exchange was the attack on A’s “second-tier alternatives” premise, followed by the two-horn dilemma in B4 and the closing. It directly undermined A’s claimed material benefit from a ban while preserving B’s case for conditional leverage.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by constructing an inescapable dilemma and systematically dismantling Side A's premise that democratic technology is uniquely precise. Side B proved that if regimes easily bypass conditions by buying Chinese tech, a ban prevents nothing; but if they do buy Western tech to avoid geopolitical strings, licensing retains crucial leverage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1 and B4.2 effectively trapped Side A, explaining why regimes accept Western conditions to hedge against Beijing, and showing that if they didn't, Side A's ban would be pure theater.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by establishing a decisive logical dilemma regarding the substitutability of alternative surveillance technology. Side A forced Side B to choose between Chinese tech being a perfect substitute (meaning the ban prevents nothing) or regimes preferring Western tech (meaning they will accept conditions, making licensing superior). Side B failed to resolve this dilemma, relying instead on an undefended "precision gap" that Side A effectively challenged using the Xinjiang example. Furthermore, Side A decisively won the clash over legitimate policing uses by exposing the impracticality of Side B's reliance on INTERPOL and financial tracking in weakly governed, rights-abusing states.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8), where it answered the pressure question by explaining supplier diversification and immediately weaponized it to expose the fatal logical trap in Side B's case, effectively neutralizing Side B's core mechanism.
