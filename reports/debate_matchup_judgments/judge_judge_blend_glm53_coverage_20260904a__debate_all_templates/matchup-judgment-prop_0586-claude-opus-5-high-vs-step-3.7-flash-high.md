### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing that Side B achieved fewer job losses mainly through smaller, delayed, exempted, or narrower raises rather than a unique institutional mechanism. A showed that legislatures can also phase, tier, index, and regionalize minimum wages, while sectoral boundaries create coverage and classification problems. B never established a like-for-like advantage at the same wage level, and its case leaned heavily on suspiciously precise, unsupported claims about California, state comparisons, and home-care boards. A was clipped twice, but still resolved the central comparison more convincingly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s decisive turn was that a board making a raise effective only when a sector can absorb it does not eliminate the pay-employment tradeoff; it administers the tradeoff by withholding or shrinking the raise. The follow-up challenge to hold the wage increase constant exposed the absence of a unique board advantage.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing that PRO's mechanism for avoiding employment losses was not a unique institutional advantage but simply withholding, phasing, or exempting wage increases where they would bite—tools available to statutory minimums without employer-captured carve-outs. CON's "hold the raise constant" challenge and turn on PRO's Germany evidence revealed that sectoral boards either replicate minimum wage tradeoffs or fail to cover most low-wage workers. PRO's framing that the motion does not require universal coverage narrowed the debate in a way that weakened their ability to prove the proposition about low-wage workers broadly, while CON's closing crystallized that boards relocate politics to a room where employers draw exemption lines around themselves.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's claim that a board's floor "only takes effect when the sector can absorb it," demonstrating that this was not a superior pay-employment tradeoff but merely the same tradeoff administered selectively, alongside the "scope is the loophole" point showing sectoral boundaries create reclassification incentives a universal floor avoids.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive comparison. PRO's employment-loss advantage rested on phase-ins, small-employer thresholds and delayed implementation, which A showed a statute can also write and which means less pay, not a better pay-vs-jobs tradeoff. Holding the raise fixed ($20 vs $20), PRO never identified a unique board mechanism. CON also won coverage/multiplication: boards cover one fast-food industry plus Medicaid-funded home care where taxpayer not calibration absorbs cost, while universal floors reach all low-wage workers, and Germany's statutory minimum was needed because sectoral bargaining left a fifth uncovered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2/A4 turn that PRO's own description - 'a $20 floor only takes effect when the sector can absorb it without layoffs' - concedes boards avoid job losses by not delivering the raise, plus the hold-the-number-fixed test and bakery-carve-out as proof scope is the loophole.
