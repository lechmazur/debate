### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the more coherent and better-defended path to victory. Its opening established a two-directional mechanism (surges fund off-peak discounts, expanding total admissions and turning sellout lotteries into choices), and its rebuttals exploited a real structural weakness in PRO's case: PRO conceded that time-based pricing (matinees, senior/student discounts) is legitimate, yet never supplied a non-circular line separating that from banned "aggressive" pricing—its own attempted definition ("fractures shared events") simply restated the harm it needed to prove. CON also correctly noted PRO's misunderstanding of dynamic pricing on half-empty shows (algorithms cut prices there rather than gouge), and repeatedly invoked the sound meta-point that a ban carries the burden of proof, which PRO's unsupported "permanent attrition to streaming" claim never discharged. PRO's opening was vivid and concrete (family pricing example, two-tiered row), but its rebuttals mostly reasserted the attrition story without evidence and never cleanly answered the definitional-line and burden-of-proof pressure. CON's closing sharply exposed a PRO misstatement of CON's position, crystallizing the clash in CON's favor. Both sides suffered similar clipping penalties, roughly canceling out.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2 [A2.3]'s correction that dynamic-pricing algorithms lower prices for half-empty shows (rather than surcharge them), combined with A4's exposure that PRO's "aggressive = fractures shared events" definition in [B4.1] was circular, most decisively undercut PRO's central causal story and its attempt to draw an enforceable line for the ban.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing the unsupported causal hinge in Side B’s case. B repeatedly asserted that displaced peak-time customers permanently migrate to streaming, but never grounded that claimed pattern or showed net cultural participation falls. A offered the clearer comparison: scarcity already excludes people, off-peak variation can broaden access, and venue viability is a prerequisite for any mass cinema habit. A also showed that B’s distinction between acceptable discounts and prohibited aggressive pricing remained vague and largely defined by the disputed harm itself. B’s opening was culturally compelling, but its later speeches mostly repeated rather than substantiated the attrition story.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions directly, identified B’s harm-based definition of “aggressive” as circular, conceded the lack of audited data, and then correctly emphasized that B had not proven the permanent streaming attrition needed to justify the proposed restriction.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B wins because A conceded time-based variation is acceptable in [A2.1], allowing B to reframe the debate as anchor-labeling not harm. B's pressure exposed the core gap: A's line for "aggressive" was circular - "fractures shared events" in [A4.1] - while B offered a workable captive-vs-choice test and a mass-habit metric of total admissions under open roofs. A's attrition-to-streaming claim was asserted across four speeches without comparative weighing against sellout lotteries under flat pricing, leaving B's cross-subsidy / empty-seat-filling mechanism live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1 + B4.1 - B turns PRO's endorsement of discounts into proof that dynamic pricing is already accepted, then in R2 shows A's proposed dividing line restates its conclusion and is unadministrable, collapsing the motion's key term "aggressive".
