### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won primarily by exploiting a significant self-inflicted wound in CON's case. In A2.3, CON explicitly conceded that placement rates are "overwhelmingly driven by contraception access, abortion law, and economic conditions" — a concession PRO pressed in B3.1's pressure question and crystallized in the closing. This undermined CON's central pillar: that the chilling effect on placements justifies permanently denying adopted adults access to records about themselves. CON's attempt to recover by saying "policy operates at the margins" never adequately reconciled with its own admission that confidentiality is not a primary driver of placements. PRO also sustained two structurally strong frames throughout: (1) that this information is fundamentally *about* the adopted adult, not about the birth parent, which CON never squarely answered; and (2) the birth certificate comparison — every non-adopted adult receives theirs unconditionally, while CON offers no principled reason why the adopted adult's own birth record is uniquely dangerous. CON's best arguments — the promise-reliance framing, the triadic system, the preventive versus reactive distinction on harassment laws — had real force but were progressively narrowed. The "alternatives satisfy the goals" argument was adequately rebutted: consent registries give birth parents veto power over an adult's access to their own origin, which PRO correctly called paternalism. CON's clipping-free format gave it an execution advantage PRO squandered through repeated word-limit violations, but the substance PRO preserved was sufficient to win the key clashes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure question B3.1 — weaponizing CON's own concession that macro-factors "overwhelmingly" drive placement rates to show the chilling effect cannot simultaneously be the load-bearing justification for permanently sealing an adult's own identity records — was the most decisive exchange. CON never recovered a coherent response to this internal contradiction, and PRO returned to it in both Rebuttal 2 and the closing to decisive effect.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side B won by making the central tradeoff clearer: permanent denial of an adult's own origin records requires more than a speculative marginal chilling effect. Side A had a strong reliance and child-placement framework, but its pipeline harm remained mostly theoretical, while Side B gave a more concrete comparative answer with open-record jurisdictions and pressed that alternatives answer medicine or reunion, not identity. Side A's privacy and promise arguments kept the debate competitive, but Side B better explained why those interests should not let the state permanently withhold records from the adult subject.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's most decisive rebuttal was using Side A's own macro-factor concession to argue that confidentiality could not be the load-bearing wall of adoption, while the denial of identity access was categorical and ongoing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): B wins by better establishing identity as a core adult right belonging to the adoptee and directly neutralizing CON's chilling-effect claim via real-world open-records examples plus CON's own concession that macro factors dominate placements, while CON's alternatives leave veto power and incomplete info unaddressed; B's responses to pressure questions and closing crystallization provide clearer weighing on rights versus speculative child harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 directly answering both pressure questions, reframing records as facts about the adult, and exposing CON's inconsistency on empirical drivers before closing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): PRO built a cleaner, more consistently defended path to victory. Three CON speeches were clipped for exceeding word limits — a real execution cost, though in each case the core arguments had already been stated before the cut. More substantively, PRO's three pillars (identity/autonomy, medical necessity, equal citizenship) were never fully neutralized. CON's best argument — that birth parents relied in good faith on a legal promise of confidentiality — carried real philosophical weight, but PRO's counter that records were sealed to protect children from mid-century stigma, not to grant birth parents perpetual veto power over another adult's identity, was never squarely rebutted. CON's deterrence argument was fatally weakened when CON itself admitted in A1 that it could produce no controlled evidence isolating the effect of record access on placement rates; the admission converted CON's central harm into an unproven mechanism exactly when it mattered most. On medical necessity, PRO's Q2 — how does a physician trace inheritance patterns or interpret variants of uncertain significance without knowing who the biological relatives are — landed clearly, and CON's response (routine management of incomplete histories) sidestepped the specific clinical tracing problem. CON's guardrails trap (if disclosure were harmless, why build infrastructure?) was clever, but PRO's A1 distinction between communication preferences and factual access was coherent enough to survive. PRO's framing — "expectations are not vetoes" and adoptees as "perpetual wards bound by promises they never made" — was rhetorically sharper and better crystallized the comparative stakes. CON's closing was emotionally resonant around the birth mother's vulnerability, but the underlying argument still rested on a deterrence premise CON had already admitted it couldn't prove.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's Q2 asking how a physician traces inheritance patterns or interprets variants of uncertain significance without identifying biological relatives, combined with CON's explicit concession in A1 that it had no controlled study isolating unsealing's effect on placement rates. Together these admissions undermined CON's two strongest independent claims — medical adequacy of alternatives and deterrence harm — in the same speech, leaving PRO's core case largely intact for the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B won by making the central comparison clearer: concrete adult interests in identity, medical information, and equal treatment outweighed Side A's largely speculative deterrence and contested confidentiality claims. Side A was strongest on reliance and privacy, but never fully established that sealed records created a binding permanent anonymity right. Its future-adoption harm was pressed effectively and remained under-evidenced. Side B also answered the guardrails point well by distinguishing factual access from unwanted contact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal to deterrence was most decisive: it forced Side A to defend withholding adult rights based on unproven marginal behavioral predictions, while Side A ultimately conceded the lack of controlled evidence and relied mainly on mechanism and burden-shifting.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by more effectively defending adult autonomy and medical access while directly answering pressure on evidence and tracing needs; Side A (CON) was penalized for clipped incomplete speeches, reliance on unproven deterrence mechanisms, and weaker weighing of privacy expectations against living adults' rights.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers to CON's questions: distinguished non-binding administrative expectations from adult rights, rejected veto by old promises, and noted absence of isolated evidence for deterrence versus burden on rights.
