### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): CON won on the proposition's central question—what "adequacy" requires—by maintaining a sharper, more analytically grounded argument throughout. The three-vector framework (data exfiltration, algorithmic manipulation, compelled cooperation) gave CON a clean scorecard, and the concession that data storage improved actually strengthened CON's position: PRO could never explain away the remaining two vectors. The decisive analytical move was CON's repeated insistence that Beijing's National Intelligence Law creates mandatory, not commercially discretionary, compliance obligations for ByteDance engineers, meaning PRO's entire "cost-raising tripwire" logic applies to commercial espionage models but is largely irrelevant against state-compelled behavior. PRO's answer—routing through a US-regulated entity makes cooperation more visible and risky—was not persuasive because visibility does not deter legally obligated behavior; it might actually push it underground and make it harder to detect. CON also successfully distinguished the telecom/aviation analogy: those sectors manage static, contractually bounded assets while TikTok's threat lives in a continuously updated algorithm controlled by people under a superseding foreign legal obligation. PRO never closed this gap. PRO's clipped turns were a minor execution cost, but the core argument was alive even without them; the real loss was on substance. PRO's "managed exposure" framing was rhetorically clean and genuinely captures how great-power competition works in practice, but CON correctly held that "making a threat more expensive" is not the same as "adequately addressing" it when the primary threat vectors remain open and the deterrence mechanism cannot reach legally compelled actors. PRO never landed a clean answer to CON's Q2 (how does legal exposure deter legally mandatory behavior?), and that gap was live at the close of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B3.2 pressure question—"If Chinese law already compels cooperation from Beijing-based engineers, how does 'heightened legal exposure' deter behavior that is already legally mandatory?"—was the most decisive exchange. PRO's A2 answer (forcing coordination through a US-regulated channel raises detection risk and deniability cost) responded to the commercial deterrence logic but did not answer the mandatory compliance logic: if engineers are legally obligated to cooperate, making cooperation more visible does not stop it; it merely changes how it is concealed. CON's subsequent rebuttal [B4.2–B4.3] pressed this distinction cleanly—"a state intelligence apparatus does not calculate 'reputational cost' the way a corporation weighs PR risk"—and PRO never recovered the ground.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (PRO)): Side A won by making adequacy hinge on whether the JV controls the dominant threat vectors, not merely whether it reduces one risk. Side B had a plausible managed-risk frame, but relied too heavily on repeating that Oracle review and US jurisdiction raise costs without clearly explaining how they catch subtle algorithmic manipulation or overcome state-compelled cooperation by Beijing-based engineers. Side A more directly answered the impossibility/perfection charge by conceding data-storage progress while arguing that leaving algorithmic influence and legal compulsion intact makes the structure inadequate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was against the "visible tripwire" claim: it argued that dynamic recommendation changes can serve both engagement and covert influence, making intent hard to audit, and that Chinese legal compulsion is not deterred like ordinary commercial misconduct.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by decisively breaking Side B's core deterrence mechanism. PRO argued that Oracle's oversight creates a tripwire that raises the cost and visibility of Chinese influence, equating this friction with adequacy. CON effectively countered that state-compelled cooperation under China's National Intelligence Law does not operate on commercial cost-benefit calculus, making reputational risk and paperwork delays irrelevant to the primary threat vector. CON also successfully dismantled PRO's telecom/aviation analogy by distinguishing static, contract-bound assets from TikTok's dynamic, continuously updated algorithm. While PRO framed CON's position as demanding an impossible zero-risk standard, CON clarified they only required control over the dominant threat vector, which the JV explicitly leaves in Beijing. CON's comparative weighing on adequacy versus mere cost-raising was sharper, more responsive to the proposition's burden, and survived pressure intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 exchange where it answers PRO's pressure questions by dismantling the telecom analogy (static vs. dynamic systems) and explaining why heightened legal exposure fails to deter legally mandatory state intelligence cooperation. This directly neutralized PRO's managed friction path to victory and clarified why partial mitigation does not meet the adequacy threshold.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (CON)): Both sides were competent, but CON edges PRO on the decisive clash. PRO's three-vector adequacy framework was well-structured and the "proves too much" challenge against Chinese law arguments was effective early. However, PRO never fully answered CON's sharpest claim: that subtle, iterative weighting changes to the recommendation model cannot be detected through snapshot audits because intent cannot be attributed to gradient updates. PRO's audit-as-paper-trail response in A4.1–A4.2 was the weakest exchange in the debate—it asserted continuous monitoring without explaining how it catches covert intent rather than merely logging activity. CON's counterargument—that influence operations at population scale require no data breach, only strategic control over the algorithm that PRO itself concedes remains in Chinese hands—was never squarely neutralized. CON also fixed its main structural weakness in B4.1 by finally defining what "adequate" would look like (actual transfer of recommendation-engine development to U.S.-controlled entities), defusing PRO's repeated "you can't define adequate" attack. CON's B4.2 distinction—data access enables surveillance while algorithmic manipulation enables population-scale influence operations—established a credible threat hierarchy that PRO's Q2 pressure question tried but failed to undermine. PRO's closing was clipped before completion, a real execution cost at a stage where crystallization mattered. The proposition's second clause ("without meaningfully ending Chinese influence") ultimately works against PRO rather than for it: because PRO admits influence over algorithm strategy persists, and CON argued persuasively that algorithmic influence is the security threat, the two halves of the proposition do contradict rather than coexist as PRO claimed. The margin is clear but competitive because PRO's adequacy framing and multi-vector analysis remained live throughout and CON took until the second rebuttal to fully address PRO's definitional challenge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.1–B4.2 answer to PRO's pressure questions: CON clarified it never claimed no arrangement could ever be adequate (defusing the "proves too much" attack), then defined what adequate would actually require (transfer of recommendation-engine development to U.S. control), and established that CFIUS vectors are not equal in severity—algorithmic manipulation enabling population-scale influence operations outweighs data-access surveillance. This answered PRO's two strongest structural attacks simultaneously while reinforcing CON's core claim.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side A won by making the central contradiction in the proposition harder for Side B to resolve: meaningful Chinese influence over the algorithm and product strategy is itself a national-security concern, not a harmless residual. Side B had a credible adequacy frame based on layered mitigation, data localization, audits, and oversight, but it leaned too much on “imperfect is still adequate” without proving why the remaining algorithmic control was tolerable. Side A also directly answered the “blanket prohibition” pressure by naming a narrower alternative: transferring recommendation-engine control and oversight to US-controlled entities. Side B’s closing repeated the claim that Side A had no threshold, which had been answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was the answer that the data barrier protects only one pathway while the dominant influence pathway remains: ByteDance-linked control over the live recommendation system. That undercut Side B’s claim that audits and paper trails were enough for adequacy.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): CON wins by successfully weighing algorithmic control as the dominant national-security threat and delivering a technically precise rebuttal that neutralizes PRO's audit defense. CON cleanly escapes the "unconditional prohibition" trap by specifying a workable adequacy threshold (U.S.-controlled algorithm development), while PRO's reliance on contractual friction and paper trails fails to answer how covert model tuning would be detected. PRO's textual argument that the motion explicitly concedes persistent influence was solid, but CON's threat prioritization and sharper comparative weighing made the path to victory clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's explanation that snapshot code audits and version logs cannot detect intent embedded in live gradient updates or phased A/B tests, effectively dismantling PRO's claim that Oracle's oversight adequately mitigates algorithmic manipulation.
