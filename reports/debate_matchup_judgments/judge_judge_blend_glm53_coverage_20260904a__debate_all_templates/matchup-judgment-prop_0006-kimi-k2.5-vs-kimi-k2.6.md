### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate. A made the morally forceful case that platform-controlled standby time is real labor and that task-only wage accounting hides a subsidy, with especially strong rhetoric. But B built the more decisive comparative route: active waiting is hard to define in a multi-app market, the same idle hour can be claimed by several platforms, and paying for non-exclusive waiting predictably pushes platforms toward login caps, shift scheduling, bans on multi-homing, or surveillance. A’s answer to the triple-pay/multi-homing challenge was the weakest key moment: saying each platform only pays its own logged minutes does not really avoid the fact that the same hour may be counted multiple times. B’s framing of downtime as contractor search cost and portfolio flexibility remained live through the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive exchange was pressing multi-homing: if a worker has three apps open for one idle hour, A never cleanly explained why that is not either triple-counted waiting time or a reason platforms would ban multi-homing and impose shifts.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side A won by successfully leveraging the multi-homing dynamic to expose a structural flaw in Side B's case. While Side B landed strong points about algorithmic control and wage subsidies, Side A convincingly argued that non-exclusive waiting time cannot be compensable by multiple platforms simultaneously without forcing those platforms to ban multi-homing and implement shift caps, thereby destroying the core portfolio flexibility of gig work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized Side B's "triple free-riding" defense by framing multi-homing not as extracted labor, but as the worker's own search cost in a competitive spot market.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): Side A won by establishing that platforms already exert real control via tracking, quotas, and deactivation threats, turning waiting into commanded labor; they directly answered B's pressure questions on enforcement and multi-homing by citing existing surveillance and per-app payment. B's core responses on independence and search costs did not neutralize the tethering evidence or show why existing tools cannot support the metric without destroying flexibility, leaving A's subsidy framing intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 7 A's Rebuttal 2, starting with labeled A1/A2 answers that used the existing tracking point to resolve both gaming and multi-homing objections without conceding new restrictions.
