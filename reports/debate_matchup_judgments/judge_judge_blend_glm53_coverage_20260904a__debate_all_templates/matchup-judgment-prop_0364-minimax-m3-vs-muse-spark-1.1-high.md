### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases, but B ultimately created the more decisive and less-resolved pressure points. B's "store footage" and "paystub vs. ledger" framing was memorable, and its mosaic/leak argument (an agency aggregating 30 clients' individually-disclosed data to reconstruct a rival's retention curve) was never squarely defused — A's answer ("tiered access and contractual remedies") was assertion rather than mechanism. More decisively, B's pressure question forced A to clarify in Rebuttal 2 that only the *format* of disclosure, not the underlying metric definition, would be standardized — and B correctly identified that this concession collapses A's own opening claim that standardization solves cross-platform portability (A1.3–A1.4). A never resolved this dilemma. A's strongest move — reframing B's buyout/incidence argument as "conceding the harm, codified" — was rhetorically sharp but only partially answered B's actual causal claim that disclosure mandates would reduce reach-based pay for mid-level talent; B's "incidence, not concession" response held up. Compounding matters, A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing were both clipped for exceeding word limits, cutting off the argument mid-sentence at the exact moment it was building to its concluding punch — a real execution cost per the judging rules, while B delivered a clean, complete, and well-crystallized closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's dilemma in Rebuttal 2 (B4.5): forcing A to admit that standardizing only "form" leaves cross-platform portability unsolved, while standardizing "definition" would dictate creative product — a tension A's closing never resolved.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): B won a clear competitive decision by making the decisive comparison easier: a disclosure-and-standardization mandate taxes reach-based pay via leakage/mosaic risk and incidence, while guild/forensic audits verify residuals without pricing backends out of existence for the mid-level talent A claimed to protect. A's leverage/statutory-floor path stayed live and rhetorically sharp, but was undercut by the form-vs-definition trap (portability needs comparability; pure form does not deliver it) and by incomplete late-round execution (clipped R2/closing). B answered pressure more cleanly and resolved the clash rather than restating it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5 form/definition trap: if PRO standardizes only reporting form, portability fails; if definition, product is dictated—collapsing A's core portability claim after A4.1.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B built a stronger, more economically grounded case. B successfully distinguished between a simple paystub and proprietary competitive intelligence, highlighting the "mosaic effect" of data leaks. B's argument about market incidence—that platforms would respond to the mandate by replacing reach-based pay with flat buyouts, thereby harming the very mid-level workers PRO sought to protect—was highly persuasive and effectively neutralized PRO's core harm. B also trapped PRO on the definition of "standardization," forcing PRO into a dilemma where standardizing only the format fails to achieve portability, while standardizing the definition dictates product. PRO's rhetorical framing was strong, but their mechanism was structurally flawed, and they suffered execution costs by being clipped in their final two speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled PRO's attempt to narrow "standardization" to just file formats, pointing out that this collapses PRO's own portability promise, while standardizing the definition forces unlike business models into identical metrics. B also effectively answered the pressure questions regarding guild-funded independent auditors.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): A built a tight, consistent case anchored in a memorable principle ("if pay depends on reach, you get to see the reach") and repeatedly turned CON's own analogies against it: the sales-report analogy, the public Top 10 disclosure, and even the monopsony-power point were each flipped into support for PRO. A also answered the feasibility pressure question (who sets the standard) with a plausible, grounded mechanism (guild-negotiated standards akin to Nielsen/JIC), and its closing directly rebutted CON's central "meter vs. contract" claim by noting data is the precondition for enforcing a definition, not a substitute for it. CON's core objection—that disclosure doesn't resolve contract-definition disputes—was a genuinely strong point, but CON's closing simply reasserted it without engaging A's reversal, and CON's trade-secret argument leaned on a portfolio-reconstruction scenario that stretched beyond the actual title-specific proposal, which A called out effectively. CON also suffered two clipped turns (opening and Rebuttal 1), a real execution cost given the opening is where core support should land. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format correctly, but A's answers integrated more cleanly into its broader case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's reuse of the platform's own public weekly Top 10 disclosure (A2.4/A4.1) against CON's trade-secret claim, forcing CON into an increasingly narrow "curated snapshot vs. portfolio time-series" distinction that never fully closed the logical gap it opened.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): A kept the live clash on the contract-meter asymmetry and made the cleaner decision rule: when pay depends on reach, secret/unstandardized reach makes performance pay unenforceable. B landed real pressure on definitions, scope, and alternatives, but its strongest replies still treated data as optional while simultaneously admitting definitions cannot be checked without a count. A answered pressure more directly, used existing Top 10/public practice and guild/JIC models effectively, and crystallized that better audits without meters leave workers auditing in the dark. Word-limit clips on B's early speeches were a real cost and left A freer to control the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A4/A5 replies: if disputes turn on what counts as a view, then secret nonstandard counts make enforcement impossible; Top 10 publicity already undercuts the strategic-leak objection.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A built a much cleaner and more persuasive case, successfully framing the core issue as an information asymmetry that makes performance-based pay inherently dishonest without a standardized meter. Side A effectively neutralized Side B's "trade secret" and "meter is not the contract" arguments by pointing out that platforms already publish more strategic data (Top 10s) and that audit rights are meaningless without the baseline data needed to know when to trigger an audit. Side B's reliance on traditional audit rights failed to address the practical reality that creators cannot audit what they cannot measure, leaving Side A with the decisive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal to the "meter is not the contract" argument, specifically the point that "Audit rights without data are permission to look without anything to see," which decisively exposed the flaw in Side B's reliance on backend audits to solve information asymmetry.
