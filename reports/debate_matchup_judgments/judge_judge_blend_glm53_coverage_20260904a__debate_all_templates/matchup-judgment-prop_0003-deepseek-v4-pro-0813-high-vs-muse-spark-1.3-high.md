### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built substantive, well-grounded cases: PRO framed historic protections as exclusionary zoning that trades housing access for aesthetic permanence, while CON countered with a concrete mechanism (small district footprint, central-land economics, and the "concentrated loss, diffuse gain" affordability paradox). The decisive exchange came in Pressure Questions: PRO's Q1 forced CON to admit that its "build elsewhere" alternative is currently illegal and requires the same zoning fight PRO wants to have inside historic districts, undercutting CON's claim to a costless substitute. PRO's Q2 also pressed CON into revealing its position amounts to an absolute veto on losing any significant building regardless of need, which PRO exploited cleanly in its closing to sharpen the tie-breaker framing. CON's answers were competent and its closing reframe (the tie always falls on current occupants, not future ones) was a clever rhetorical parry, but it never squarely neutralized the "equal political difficulty" concession, leaving PRO's narrowed framing of the case as the more resolved and comparative one by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question forcing CON to concede that outside-district housing capacity is currently illegal (making "build elsewhere" an equally hard political fight, not a free alternative), which PRO then leveraged decisively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by framing the motion as a limited tie-breaker after gentler options fail. B persuasively established concentrated heritage and displacement harms, but relied too heavily on the false choice of building elsewhere and on the questionable claim that density redevelopment yields fewer total homes. B ultimately conceded that its alternative capacity is also legally blocked and maintained an effectively categorical veto for significant buildings, leaving A's central argument intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure questions forced B to admit that outside capacity requires its own zoning fight and that significant buildings remain protected regardless of housing need. A then converted those answers into a clear comparison between a conditional override and an automatic preservation veto.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the core clash around the "tie-breaker" between housing need and architectural significance, exposing Side B's position as an absolute preservation veto that fails to weigh the costs of the status quo. Side A also effectively dismantled Side B's "build elsewhere" alternative by forcing Side B to admit that such outside capacity is currently illegal, meaning it requires the exact same political zoning fights without the advantage of existing central, transit-rich infrastructure. Side B's economic arguments about displacement were strong, but they ultimately could not overcome Side A's sharper structural rebuttals and cleaner weighing of the proposition's explicit trade-offs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing argument pointing out that Side B's "build elsewhere" alternative is merely the same zoning fight transferred elsewhere, and that Side B's refusal to ever allow a significant building to fall for housing constitutes an absolute veto rather than a defensible policy.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A built a consistent, well-executed case across every round: historic districts often sit precisely where transit and jobs converge, freezing them causes quiet but real scarcity-driven displacement, and demolition can be mitigated with tenant safeguards while blockade cannot be mitigated at all. This throughline (scarcity displacement vs. addressable demolition displacement) was pressed hard in Rebuttal 1, defended cleanly against pressure questions, and crystallized memorably in the close ("choose the hundred"). Side B's opening and later rounds (PQ, Rebuttal 2, Closing) offered a genuinely strong counter-architecture — reversibility of zoning/office-conversion alternatives versus the permanence of demolished fabric, plus a sequencing burden ("prove alternatives exhausted first") — and B's closing exchange on reversibility was the sharpest moment for that side. However, B's entire first rebuttal turn was corrupted/unreadable, wiping out a full round of direct response to A's strongest arguments (the scarcity-pricing rebuttal and the geography point that historic districts are precisely where density is needed). Although B partially recovered this ground in Rebuttal 2 and the PQ answers, the missing round is a real execution cost per the judging criteria, and it left A's Rebuttal 1 points less contested than they would otherwise have been. Combined with A's cleaner, more consistently landed rhetorical framing, this gives A a clear but competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange is A's Rebuttal 1 point that district freezes cause "scarcity displacement" that is "larger, quieter, and permanent" than demolition displacement, paired with B's Closing counter that reversibility of zoning alternatives versus irreversibility of demolished fabric is the real asymmetry. B's version of this clash is strong, but the fact that B's original Rebuttal 1 turn meant to directly engage A's scarcity argument was lost to a formatting failure gave A's framing more staying power throughout the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): A won by showing that preservation-induced scarcity creates broad, uncompensated displacement and that alternatives elsewhere cannot necessarily substitute for housing near jobs and transit. B’s sequencing argument was strong but ultimately conceded relaxation after alternatives are exhausted, which is compatible with the conditional proposition. B also lost an entire rebuttal to clipping and gibberish, leaving A’s central mechanisms unanswered during a crucial round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest response was that “build elsewhere first” does not defeat the motion: A accepted prioritizing parking and non-contributing sites, then justified relaxing protections where residual shortages remain. B’s own qualified concession reinforced rather than refuted that position.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A wins clearly due to a catastrophic execution failure by Side B in Rebuttal 1, which produced only glitched, unreadable text. Beyond this massive penalty, Side A built a highly persuasive and consistent case around "scarcity displacement," effectively neutralizing Side B's "build elsewhere first" argument by highlighting the geographic necessity of transit-rich cores. Side B's remaining speeches were competent and raised valid points about sequencing and reversibility, but could not overcome the lost round and Side A's sharper comparative weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where they effectively turned Side B's "older cheap rentals" argument against them by explaining how capping supply creates scarcity displacement that prices out the very renters Side B claims to protect.
