### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): PRO won a clear but competitive debate for two reasons. First, CON never seriously contested the core premise—that luxury and performance vehicles produce unusually concentrated external costs—and instead bet almost entirely on the workability objection. That left PRO's causal story about engineered exhaust systems, instant-torque kinetic hazards, and recreational noise essentially uncontested on the merits. Second, PRO's best mid-debate move—the bus/sanitation-truck allowance argument (A4.4, A5.2)—genuinely undermined CON's "just set universal limits" counter-proposal. If a city-wide decibel ceiling must accommodate heavy municipal vehicles, a performance car roaring well below that ceiling is still inflicting purely recreational harm while technically legal. CON's response ("noise is noise, ticket the 90-decibel roar") completely missed the threshold-calibration problem: the issue is that the roar may be sub-threshold under the universal limit, not that cameras cannot measure it. CON's strongest moment was Q2—"if a camera already measures the lethal surge, why classify the vehicle at all?"—and PRO's answer (preventing over-criminalization of ordinary vehicles) was plausible but thin. Still, CON never fully leveraged that pressure, partly because CON's own rebuttal speeches were somewhat repetitive, cycling through "badge vs. behavior" and "VIN database" without meaningfully advancing the comparison. PRO's specs-based classification response (horsepower thresholds, active exhaust indicators) was imperfect but adequate for a policy debate where the motion only requires proportionate, not perfect, enforcement. CON's definitional challenge is real, but it is an implementation difficulty rather than a principled objection to the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.4 bus-allowance argument was the most decisive single exchange. By pointing out that a universal noise threshold must be calibrated high enough to permit buses and delivery trucks, PRO showed that CON's "just enforce universal limits" proposal structurally protects performance cars that exploit that headroom for purely recreational purposes. CON's closing rebuttal (B4.5, B5.2) simply asserted "noise is noise" and "ticket the 90-decibel roar," but never engaged with the calibration problem—why the universal ceiling would, by necessity, sit too high to catch the specific recreational harm PRO identified.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by making the central comparison cleaner: enforce objective urban harms like decibels and dangerous acceleration universally, rather than creating class based thresholds for luxury or performance vehicles. Side B plausibly established that performance cars create concentrated noise and acceleration risks, and its bus and active exhaust points were strong. But A repeatedly showed that the proposed tools measure the harmful conduct directly, making vehicle classification either redundant or arbitrary, especially for modified ordinary cars producing the same harm. B never fully solved the implementation and equity problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest rebuttal was that acoustic and velocity cameras undermine PRO's own framework: if the harm can be measured directly, the system does not need to identify make, model, price, or factory class to issue the citation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): CON successfully demonstrated that targeting vehicle classes is legally arbitrary and practically unworkable because modified economy cars produce identical harms, requiring an impossible real-time surveillance apparatus to classify vehicles. CON also showed that objective, behavior-based enforcement using acoustic and velocity cameras captures the exact harms PRO described without discriminatory two-tiered standards. While PRO landed a strong unrefuted point about universal noise limits accommodating transit vehicles, CON's decisive pressure questions and rebuttals exposed that PRO's framework redundantly classified vehicles for acceleration harms that could be universally measured, and that the modified-car loophole undermined the coherence of the luxury distinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question Q2 and Rebuttal 2 argument that if automated cameras can objectively measure a lethal two-second surge, identifying the vehicle's make or price tag is unnecessary, exposing PRO's acceleration enforcement argument as redundant and the classification scheme as legally arbitrary.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): This was a genuinely competitive debate, but PRO maintains a narrow but real advantage by keeping its core empirical claim alive through the exchange: that no single universal decibel threshold can simultaneously catch a stock supercar's normal downshift without also over-penalizing ordinary commuters, delivery trucks, or buses. CON's strongest counter — acoustic cameras can detect behavioral patterns like sharp spikes rather than just raw decibels — is a plausible technical rebuttal, but PRO never needed to concede it fully because CON never shows it eliminates the threshold gap. CON's "driver must adapt" answer to PRO's Q1 is the weakest moment in the debate; legally-sold factory vehicles cannot be expected to modify their engineering to meet a limit that their design necessarily exceeds during normal operation. PRO's A1 answer to B's pressure question is the debate's clearest pivot: a modified Honda at 90dB still gets ticketed under the universal baseline, while a stock Porsche at 85dB would be legal under a universal system calibrated to permit normal traffic — tiered enforcement catches both, a universal threshold catches neither cleanly. CON's "ticket the physical harm, not the hood ornament" framing is rhetorically effective and the double-standard argument is the sharpest sustained pressure in the debate, but PRO's response — that the tiers reflect genuinely different baseline capabilities, not arbitrary class-based favoritism — is sufficient to survive it. The truck-regulation analogy also provides unrebutted real-world precedent for differential enforcement by vehicle class. CON scores higher on rhetoric and originality with its memorable phrasings, but PRO's argumentative structure holds together better across the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained double-standard attack in Rebuttal 2 and Closing — forcing PRO to explicitly admit that a stock Porsche at 85dB gets a ticket while a stock Honda at 85dB does not — was the most damaging pressure applied. PRO's answer (the Honda producing 85dB already violates the universal baseline whereas the Porsche at 85dB would be legal under any universal limit calibrated for normal traffic) was adequate but left some residual doubt, making this the decisive clash of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): A won by making the decisive clash about physical harm versus vehicle classification. B had a plausible capacity and concentration story, and its baseline-plus-stricter-tier answer helped against the modified-car objection. But A more effectively exposed that B's system can ticket the same 85-decibel event for a performance car while allowing it for an ordinary vehicle, without adequately explaining why identical public-health harm should be treated differently. B's universal-threshold dilemma was important but under-supported and partly answered by A's universal enforcement of sharp spikes and actual acceleration.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was turning B's Rebuttal 2 answer into the double-standard argument: a modified ordinary car above the baseline can be ticketed, but the same lower-level harmful acoustic spike that triggers the performance tier remains legal for ordinary vehicles.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): CON successfully exposed that PRO's tiered enforcement creates a double standard: identical physical harm (decibel spikes, acceleration) is treated differently based on vehicle class, undermining the externality rationale. CON's argument that urban harm is physical and should trigger universal behavior-based thresholds remained intact, while PRO's claim of unusually concentrated costs was weakened by modern EV performance and the prevalence of modified ordinary cars. PRO failed to adequately rebut the principle that "human ears do not care about DMV paperwork," and their commercial-truck analogy was distinguished. The universal-threshold framework proved cleaner and more principled.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (Turn 8) exposing the fatal contradiction that PRO's tiered system legalizes the exact same 85-decibel harm from ordinary vehicles while penalizing luxury cars, effectively collapsing PRO's externality justification.
