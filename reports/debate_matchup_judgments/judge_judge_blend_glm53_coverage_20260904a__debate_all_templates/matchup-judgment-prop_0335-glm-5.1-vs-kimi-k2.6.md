### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger cumulative case. Three things decided the debate. First, PRO's "architecture shapes priorities" argument—that the statutory category signals to legislatures what deserves dedicated resources—was never cleanly answered by CON. CON insisted political will, not a label, drives funding, but that claim collided with PRO's elder abuse example, which is real precedent for how a distinct category unlocked training, dedicated units, and accountability metrics that general fraud never provided. Second, PRO effectively weaponized CON's own concessions: CON acknowledged existing fraud units lack trauma-trained investigators and AI-specific detection knowledge, then explained this as merely a resource problem solvable within current structures. PRO correctly identified the contradiction—if existing structures have chronically failed to deliver these resources for ordinary romance scams, calling it political will rather than structural design is unpersuasive. Third, CON's strongest running argument—that hybrid schemes create a jurisdictional labyrinth—was answered adequately by PRO's "primary mechanism intake screening" analogy to how mixed drug-and-weapons cases already function. The answer wasn't perfect, but it was sufficient to neutralize the pressure without conceding the core. CON's best moments were the "telemarketing targets a channel, elder abuse protects a legal class" distinction and the "taxonomy theater" framing, but neither fully closed off PRO's mechanism-of-harm argument or the structural-neglect inference. Both sides had speeches clipped, roughly canceling as execution costs. PRO's closing crystallized the live clash more cleanly than CON's, which largely restated earlier ground without sharpening the comparison further.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 crystallization: "CON keeps insisting existing fraud frameworks are sufficient while conceding they lack trauma-trained investigators, AI-specific detection knowledge, and victim-centered intake." This turned CON's own admissions into a structural indictment of their position, forcing CON to argue that political will—not framework design—explains chronic under-resourcing. That explanation was never made persuasive given CON's inability to explain why fresh political will would arrive without the signal sent by a distinct statutory category.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A wins a competitive debate because it better matched the motion’s consumer-protection framing. B was strong that fraud statutes already cover deception-for-money and that labels do not automatically create budgets, but it leaned too heavily on criminal/legal anatomy and underanswered A’s point that prevention, reporting, intake, and victim support must be tailored to AI-enabled sustained intimacy grooming. A’s specialization analogy and “clear front door” framing made distinct treatment seem useful rather than merely symbolic, despite some overclaiming about budgets and neglect.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s rebuttal that “same fraud” misses consumer-protection specialization was decisive: telemarketing, elder abuse, and specialized intake showed that overlapping legal elements do not preclude distinct regulatory treatment, weakening B’s fragmentation frame.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): CON wins by anchoring the debate in the actual function of consumer protection law (remedying economic injury) and effectively turning PRO's structural arguments into practical liabilities. CON consistently distinguished PRO's key examples (elder abuse targets a legally defined age class, telemarketing targets a channel, neither targets an emotional pitch) and exposed PRO's "primary mechanism" test as a jurisdictional hurdle that delays investigations. While PRO compellingly argued that distinct categories force specialized resources and trauma-informed intake, CON successfully challenged the causal link between relabeling and funding, demonstrating that political will—not taxonomy—drives appropriations, and that a new label risks fragmenting enforcement and cannibalizing existing budgets. CON's tighter legal grounding, sharper analogy dismantling, and clearer pragmatic weighing provided the more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 dismantling of PRO's telemarketing and elder abuse analogies, showing they target specific channels or legally defined vulnerable classes rather than emotional deception textures. This distinction neutralized PRO's core precedent for fragmentation, anchored CON's claim that consumer law properly focuses on economic harm, and went unanswered in PRO's later speeches.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): The decisive clash is the internal contradiction CON exposed in PRO's case: PRO's stated goal is fixing defective platform design (the "haunted house"), but the proposed category is built around the scammer's technology ("AI-enabled"), not the platform's failure. This means human fraud rings exploiting the exact same defective algorithmic pipeline escape the category entirely, giving identically harmed victims second-class protections—the opposite of PRO's goal. CON pressed this contradiction sharply in Rebuttal 2 and the closing. PRO's answer in A1 of B4 ("AI-enabled describes the marketplace defect, not the criminal's laptop") was too hand-wavy to resolve the problem: if the defect is the platform's architecture, the category should attach to platform design across all intimate fraud, not just AI-assisted variants. CON also landed the cleaner EPA rebuttal—existing frameworks adapt to new threats without creating per-threat categories—directly undercutting PRO's analogy. PRO's strongest live argument is empirical: existing law has conspicuously failed to compel deepfake detection or algorithmic audits, and classification creates a statutory mandate. CON's answer (underfunded enforcement, weak political will) is accurate but doesn't fully explain why more funding without classification would succeed where funding-with-general-authority has failed. The "budgets follow statutory missions" point from PRO therefore retains some force. However, CON's rebuttal—missions multiply, budgets don't—is plausible, and the category-mismatch argument is never neutralized. PRO's rhetoric was consistently stronger and the closing was complete (CON's was clipped, a real execution cost), but CON's technical argument discipline and the unrebutted mismatch claim carry the debate. A clear but competitive win for CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.2–A4.5): CON exposed that PRO's own framework is misdesigned for its stated purpose. If the real hazard is platform design enabling industrial-scale intimate fraud, the legal category should attach to platform negligence across all intimate fraud—not to the scammer's choice of tool. A dating app funneling victims toward human fraud rings using the same defective pipeline is an identical marketplace failure, yet falls outside PRO's category. PRO asserted "AI-enabled describes the marketplace defect" but never explained why human-operated scams on the same pipeline deserve weaker protections, and this contradiction was never squarely resolved.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. A made a vivid and plausible case that AI changes romance scams through scale, synthetic intimacy, and platform-mediated harm, and argued well that a consumer-protection mandate could be more proactive than ordinary fraud enforcement. But B more effectively narrowed the decisive clash: if the real problem is negligent platform design, an AI-enabled romance-scam category is an awkward and underinclusive way to address it. B also consistently argued that existing consumer-protection law can impose platform duties without fragmenting enforcement or sorting victims by the scammer's tool. A's mandate argument was rhetorically strong but remained more asserted than proven, and it never fully solved B's line-drawing and category-mismatch pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive rebuttal was the Rebuttal 2 turn arguing that A's own haunted-house framing points to regulating defective platform design generally, not creating a category centered on AI-enabled romance scams. That made A's proposed classification look misdesigned for its stated purpose.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): CON wins by exposing a structural contradiction in PRO's case: the mismatch between the motion's proposed category ("AI-enabled romance scams") and PRO's actual regulatory target (platform design and safety). CON convincingly argues that tying legal treatment to the scammer's tool creates arbitrary line-drawing problems, incentivizes criminal adaptation, and partitions victims, while existing consumer-protection frameworks (like FTC unfair practices authority) already possess the forward-looking mandate to regulate platform safety if properly resourced. PRO delivers vivid, memorable rhetoric and a strong normative case about industrialized emotional predation, but repeatedly sidesteps the taxonomy problem and offers a hand-wavy account of how a new statutory label solves zero-sum funding constraints. CON's sharper policy mechanics and direct engagement with the motion's explicit wording secure the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 dismantling of PRO's category/target mismatch ("If what matters is fixing the pipe, why build the legal category around what flows through it?"), which trapped PRO in an unresolvable tension between the motion's AI-specific wording and their platform-focused remedy, neutralizing PRO's core route to victory.
