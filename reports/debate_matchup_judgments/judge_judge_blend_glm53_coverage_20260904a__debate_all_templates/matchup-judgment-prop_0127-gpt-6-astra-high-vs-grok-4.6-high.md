### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON did more of the decisive work. CON introduced three durable mechanisms early (the "waiting effect" that shifts shopper behavior and shrinks full-price sales, the donation cost-shift onto charities, and the evasion problem where managers cull stock early to dodge the duty) and sustained them through every round. PRO neutralized the donation-logistics objection well by stressing the discount fallback, but never solved the evasion problem: PRO's repeated answer ("trigger is condition," "occasional inspections") never explained how enforcement catches a manager who simply reclassifies stock before it hits the "edible/unsaleable" threshold, which CON kept pressing (B1.5, B2.7, Q2, B4.6) and PRO's A2 answer left largely unrebutted in substance. CON also successfully distinguished its disposal-charge alternative from the mandate in the pressure-answer round (B4.3), defusing PRO's parity question, whereas PRO's burden-of-proof claim ("CON hasn't shown the net effect") was turned back effectively by CON's framing that the side proposing a mandatory duty carries that burden. PRO's case remained sound in principle (incentive misalignment, bounded duty, transfer vs. loss distinction it forced CON to concede), but CON's sharper causal mechanisms and unresolved enforcement critique gave it the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's persistent "early culling" evasion argument (B1.5, B2.7, Q2, B4.6), which PRO never squarely defeated, most damaged PRO's practical enforceability claim and became the debate's most decisive unresolved thread.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by exposing that Side B's "social cost" framing conflates transfers with net losses and by showing that B's preferred disposal-charge alternative faces the same lean-inventory risks while allowing edible food to bypass consumers entirely. A's bounded-duty framing—requiring a meaningful opportunity, not guaranteed rescue—absorbs B's legitimate cost concerns without collapsing into B's speculative predictions of empty shelves and ruined variety.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal distinguishing lost full-price sales as transfers rather than social losses, paired with the pressure showing that B's disposal-charge alternative creates similar inventory pressures without ensuring surplus reaches people, undermining B's chief counter-proposal and net-loss narrative.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): PRO kept the decisive distinction live: purchase cost sunk vs rescue decision, lost full-price revenue as transfer not social loss, which CON eventually conceded for the markdown itself. PRO's "or" flexibility and existing retail-channel infrastructure answered charity-capacity and cost-shift objections, and showed disposal pricing alone lets edible food bypass eaters. CON's waiting-rationality and early-cull evasion are plausible but remained predictive, not net-loss proven, and its own alternative shares price/variety side-effects. Clearer proportionality case goes to A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1/R2 transfer-vs-destruction move on B's lost-sale claim plus Q1 about net loss: forced B to concede markdown is a transfer and retreat to handling/variety costs, narrowing the clash to a manageable proportionality question PRO answered with bounded duty.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and used the Pressure Questions round to real effect. B's Rebuttal 1 landed a genuinely damaging blow by showing PRO's mandate does not prohibit edible-food destruction outright, only requires a prior sales attempt, and B sustained that "process vs. outcome" critique effectively through the timing and recipient-capacity problems. However, A's pressure question forced B into a decisive concession: B explicitly admitted "disposal can remain a business cost" under its fee-based alternative, which directly validates the very framing the motion is written to reject. A exploited this concession sharply in Rebuttal 2 and the closing ("Pricing a skip still leaves destruction lawful at industrial volume"), tying the clash back to the motion's precise language about not treating waste as normal overhead. B's closing tried to reframe the debate around actual waste reduction rather than formal compliance, which is a legitimate weighing move, but it never fully escaped the fact that its own proposed system openly tolerates disposal as a priced-in cost, while A's rule at minimum forecloses that as the default path. On balance, A's case tracked the resolution's specific normative test more closely and better weaponized the concession extracted via pressure questioning, giving it a modest but real edge despite B's sharper individual rebuttal moments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 point (B2.1) that PRO's rule "does not prohibit edible waste; it prohibits waste without a preceding sales attempt" was the single most damaging strike, but A's Pressure Question extracting B's admission that "disposal can remain a business cost" (B4.1) proved decisive in the final weighing because it directly conceded ground on the motion's core language, which A repeatedly leveraged through Rebuttal 2 and the closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A successfully defended the core mechanism of closing the "easiest exit" and forcing a genuine recovery attempt before disposal, matching the proposition's specific target of ending waste as a normal business cost. Side B's counterproposal of pricing disposal harm was coherent but failed to overcome A's key distinction: under a fee, dumping remains lawful overhead, whereas the mandate makes it Plan B. B's ritual objection was blunted by A's "donate OR discount sharply" structure and the concession that imperfect recovery is acceptable. A maintained cleaner comparative framing throughout and crystallized the clash more decisively in closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the disposal-fee alternative by showing it merely prices rather than closes the waste exit, crystalized in "a dearer skip does not feed anyone next to the dumpster," while maintaining that the dual mandate (donation or sharp discount) answers B's logistics concerns without requiring zero waste.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): CON won the core effectiveness clash. PRO conceded a sharp discount followed by discard satisfies the rule, so the mandate does not ban edible waste, only ritualizes it. B showed that a genuine offer needs selling time, forcing earlier clearance that cannibalizes full-price sales, while a late theater discount changes paperwork not waste. B's alternative – price disposal and fund collection – rewards both prevention and successful recovery, whereas PRO's sequence rewards only attempted recovery. PRO never resolved the Q1 timing fork and relied on labeling discard as “Plan B” rather than proving more food saved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 on the one-minute sharp discount: a genuinely sharp offer with no time to buy satisfies the rule but saves nothing, and a usable window must pull forward clearance – exposing that PRO mandates cost without guaranteeing consumption.
