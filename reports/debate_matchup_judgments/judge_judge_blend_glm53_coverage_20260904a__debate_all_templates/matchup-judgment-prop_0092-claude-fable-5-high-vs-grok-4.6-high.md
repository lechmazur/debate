### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): CON successfully pinned PRO to the text of the motion, showing it names specific consumer classes for higher parking, registration, or curb fees rather than enacting a genuine fleet-wide user-pays weight schedule. PRO's reframing into a universal loophole-closer was clever but could not overcome CON's demonstration that parking and curb fees would meter a silhouette rather than measured use, and that the safety surcharge is either too small to change fleet behavior or large enough to punish current owners without addressing immediate hazards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's dismantling of PRO's "user-pays" framing by distinguishing between metering actual use (gallons, time, axle load, ticketed violations) and metering a vehicle label, crystallized in Rebuttal 2 and Closing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): PRO won the user-pays hinge: CON conceded weight belongs on registration across the fleet [B1.3][B4.1], which under any continuous scale makes oversized SUVs/pickups pay more — exactly what the motion allows via "parking, registration, or curb fees." PRO also answered the tool test: cities can't set hood heights, fees fund daylighting and travel with the blind zone beyond corners, and shift next purchases like insurance/congestion. CON's silhouette/marking-error and "physics unchanged this month" lines never escaped that concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 trap: accepting a universal weight schedule still satisfies the proposition because oversized pay more when costs are higher, turning CON's principle into PRO's ballot.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively dismantling Side B's core objections. On safety, A's point that blind zones travel with the vehicle into driveways and mid-block crossings—where street redesign cannot reach—neutralized B's "physics stay the same" argument. On curb space, A's economic argument about finite block length defeated B's claim that a marked stall is a fixed unit. A also successfully navigated the textual clash by framing the motion as closing a loophole for consumer heavyweights who currently escape the weight-based pricing already applied to commercial fleets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 on safety, specifically pointing out that daylighting corners only fixes intersections, while the blind zones of tall hoods kill in driveways, parking lots, and mid-block, making vehicle pricing the only tool that addresses the hazard everywhere it goes.

### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by exposing that the motion’s class-based targeting was arbitrary and inferior to neutral dimension-based pricing, dismantling the safety rationale (fees relocate rather than reduce harm), and revealing that city registration fees correlate with owner address rather than actual dangerous usage. PRO was unable to show why the specific motion was needed beyond generic externality pricing that CON’s alternative handled more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 distinguishing neutral per-foot pricing from the motion’s class-based targeting [A4.1] and exposing the registration-address mismatch [A4.3], demonstrating that everything defensible in PRO’s case survived without the motion while everything the motion uniquely added was arbitrary.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): PRO kept a live externality route: finite curb, hidden sightlines, and higher crash energy impose costs the motion prices. CON's strongest hits – fee doesn't lower hoods, registration follows address – shrink to only one implementation, while CON conceded in Q/A that a long-bed should pay more per foot and that neutral dimension pricing works. That concession is the proposition in substance: charge more when size imposes more cost. PRO's response that revenue ends the socialization, plus turnover and trip frequency effects, survived the safety-theater attack better than CON's class-tax label survived PRO's equivalence point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B2.5 and B4.1: parking surcharges relocate rather than remove hood-line risk, and per-foot neutral pricing achieves PRO's defensible goal without a class definition, forcing PRO to defend a sliding scale and address-based registration mismatch.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully distinguishing the motion's class-based tax on "oversized SUVs and pickups" from a neutral, dimension-based pricing alternative. B effectively dismantles PRO's claim that registration and parking fees accurately track the vehicle's safety hazard, pointing out that parking prices static storage and city registration follows the owner's address rather than the vehicle's movement. This exposes the motion's specific mechanisms as poorly targeted and arbitrary, leaving PRO unable to defend the class label without conceding the superiority of neutral pricing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it explains that city registration follows the owner's address rather than the vehicle's movement. This perfectly neutralizes PRO's claim that registration fees "follow the vehicle through every crosswalk," highlighting that the suburban commuter who actually drives the SUV into the city pays nothing, while the city resident pays full freight.
