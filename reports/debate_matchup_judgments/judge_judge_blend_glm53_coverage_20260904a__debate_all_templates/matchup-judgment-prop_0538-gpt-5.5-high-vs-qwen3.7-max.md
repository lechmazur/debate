### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a more coherent and structurally complete case across the debate. The decisive advantage came in two places. First, PRO's pressure question Q1—why hasn't decades of raw-material exporting already produced the manufacturing capacity CON promises?—was genuinely damaging, and CON's answer in A4.1 ("governments diverted capital into premature manufacturing") actually conceded the structural trap rather than refuting it. CON offered no mechanism for why continuing the same approach would produce a different outcome. Second, PRO's "existing demand" reframe was effective and largely unanswered: Africans already import food products, textiles, medicines, and refined fuel, so producing these regionally is not creating protected new demand behind tariff walls—it is competing for demand that already exists, using proximity advantages. CON's strongest counterargument—the regressive-tariff tax on poor consumers—had real force in isolation but was blunted by this reframe. PRO also correctly clarified throughout that "prioritize" does not mean "abandon global exports," defusing CON's zero-sum infrastructure claim without CON ever adequately restoring it. CON's answer to B's Q2 was similarly incomplete: describing the treadmill of earning dollars from exports and spending them on imported processed goods was PRO's argument, not an answer to it. PRO's closing crystallized the exit-strategy framing cleanly; CON's closing mostly recycled the foundation-before-roof metaphor without sharpening the comparison. Both sides were disciplined about sourcing, but PRO's examples (cocoa/chocolate, crude/refined fuel) were more vivid and repeatedly anchored the core comparative claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure question Q1—asking why decades of raw-material dependence had not already delivered manufacturing—was the most decisive single exchange. CON's answer in A4.1 blamed governments for misallocating revenue into premature manufacturing, which ironically validated PRO's structural-trap argument rather than defending continued raw-material dependence as a reliable bridge to industrialization. PRO then pressed this in B4.3, noting CON had still offered no mechanism to make the bridge end, and CON never recovered a satisfactory response.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory by exposing CON's missing transition mechanism from raw exports and delivering cleaner direct answers to pressure questions on competition and capital; B repeated tariff and sequence claims without neutralizing PRO's value-capture and exit-strategy points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 directly answered B's Q1/Q2 on tariffs and capital sources, then crystallized the absence of any CON bridge out of raw-material dependence.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by exposing that CON's model of raw-material export dependence has no credible exit mechanism: decades of extraction have not produced the broad manufacturing base CON promises, and CON offered no answer for why postponement would end. PRO successfully reframed intra-African manufacturing as a bridge—capturing existing demand for food, fuel, textiles, and cement through regional scale and proximity advantages, rather than as a protectionist enclosure. CON's warnings about regressive tariffs and diversion of scarce capital were blunted by PRO's distinction between time-limited regional competition and permanent national monopolies, and by the argument that cross-border infrastructure serves global exporters too. CON repeated the "sequence" and "roof before foundation" frame without adequately answering PRO's historical challenge or the treadmill dynamic, leaving PRO's route to victory live and cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to CON's pressure questions in Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), especially the rebuttal that CON treats raw-material dependence as a bridge to industrialization but supplies no mechanism that makes the bridge end, while PRO identifies the actual treadmill dynamics of exporting crude and importing fuel, exporting cocoa and importing chocolate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON wins on a clear but competitive margin despite three clipped speeches. The decisive moment came when CON caught PRO's strategic concession — "not an overnight ban, commodity revenue continues" — and turned it into a sequencing argument: if existing hard-currency exports remain the financing bridge for machinery, fuel, debt service, and public budgets, they cannot logically be "deprioritized," and PRO's motion collapses into CON's own position of diversify-and-upgrade. PRO never cleanly answered this. CON's pressure questions were sharper than PRO's: Q2 (what concrete test distinguishes genuine value creation from protection?) forced PRO into a late-stage "regional export success" answer that CON correctly identified as too slow and too weak. PRO's transition-funding answer (redirect extractive-firm incentives plus regional development banks) was thin — CON rightly noted these sources don't escape scarcity and still need capital. PRO's closing recycled the "ceiling vs. ladder" metaphor without sharpening the live clashes, while CON's closing applied the opportunity-cost frame cleanly to the motion as written. PRO had genuine strengths: the structural-poverty-trap framing was rhetorically potent, the cocoa example landed initially, and the infrastructure-follows-demand argument had historical plausibility. But PRO's answers to the hardest questions — where does transition capital come from, and what is the viability test — were never made rigorous enough to beat CON's sustained pressure on them. The clipping of three CON speeches is a real execution cost that reduces the margin, but the visible arguments were precise and consistently applied.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 catching PRO's "no overnight ban" concession: PRO's own qualification that commodity revenues continue during transition effectively converged with CON's position, and CON exploited it cleanly — "that concession moves toward CON: diversify and upgrade, yes; deprioritize the revenue base before replacement capacity exists, no." PRO never recovered from this turn in the closing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins on stronger adaptation, direct handling of pressure questions with required A1/A2 structure, and clearer weighing of structural transformation vs. perpetual raw-export dependence. Side B's multiple word-limit clips, missing A1/A2 format, and incomplete rebuttals create real execution costs that weaken its sequencing argument despite solid core points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A1/A2 answers to pressure questions, crystallizing the treadmill vs. ladder comparison and exposing CON's false binary.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON won by exposing a fatal sequencing flaw in PRO's plan. The hard currency, scale, and infrastructure financing needed to industrialize comes primarily from the very raw-material exports to rich markets that PRO wants Africa to deprioritize. PRO never overcame this funding gap, offering only vague alternatives like "redirecting extractive subsidies" and regional development banks, which CON dismantled as fiscally and commercially inadequate to generate the necessary foreign exchange and repayable capital. CON's sharp focus on opportunity cost—showing that uncompetitive regional manufacturing protected by tariffs can destroy rather than retain value, and that PRO's "not an overnight ban" concession effectively conceded the sequencing argument—proved the decisive clash. PRO's anti-colonial narrative was rhetorically powerful and emotionally resonant, but CON demonstrated that the motion's call to prioritize intra-African manufacturing *over* rich-market exports is economically self-undermining rather than liberating, because the ladder PRO invokes is built from the hard currency they want governments to stop centering.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over transition funding and the hard-currency trap. CON's paired pressure questions forced PRO to rely on redirecting tax incentives and regional banks to bridge the gap; CON then directly exposed that these mechanisms cannot replace the scalable, hard-currency revenues from global commodity exports that pay for imported machinery, fuel, and debt service. This collapsed PRO's transition mechanism and crystallized the debate around sequencing.
