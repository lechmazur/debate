First, the behavioral claim—motivated asylum seekers prefer wages over welfare once work is legally permitted—was never squarely neutralized. CON's most effective counter was the informal-labor-market argument (workers already work off the books, so formal authorization's welfare-reduction impact is smaller than claimed), but PRO correctly noted that informal arrangements generate no tax revenue and that legal authorization incentivizes employer formalization; CON never closed that loop. Second, and most importantly, PRO preserved a structural point CON never directly engaged: the 60-day authorization is a preliminary employment permission while full merits adjudication continues, not a truncation of vetting. Fraud risk "spikes" only if authorization forecloses the full biographical review, but it does not—it merely permits work while the case proceeds. CON's vetting dilemma (constant quality + faster = resources or compression) was genuinely sharp, and PRO's response ("existing front-end capacity already does rapid initial checks") was hand-wavy and asserted rather than demonstrated. However, that point loses force once you accept that authorization doesn't end vetting—it runs parallel to it. CON also never offered a causal mechanism for why fraud increases specifically at the 60-day mark that PRO couldn't answer with "downstream penalties are unchanged." PRO's fraud handling was more coherent throughout. CON's strongest sustained argument (the vetting dilemma) was partially but not decisively answered; PRO's behavioral and fraud arguments were partially but not decisively answered by CON. On net, PRO held more unrefuted ground and its core causal story (shorter blocking period → less welfare dependency, same fraud deterrence downstream) remained more intact after the full exchange.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by building a cleaner, more consistent case and identifying the decisive contradiction in CON's position. The core structural flaw in CON's argument—that work authorization simultaneously attracts fraudulent claims (implying meaningful economic value) and fails to reduce reliance on public assistance (implying negligible economic value)—was exposed sharply in B's pressure questions and hammered in Rebuttal 2. CON's attempt to resolve this in A4.1 by distinguishing "attractiveness at filing stage" from "insufficiency at labor-market stage" was the first coherent response, but it arrived late and relied on speculative steps. B's most durable live route was simple: per-household indirect costs are fixed regardless of work status, so any employment adds tax contributions without adding new expense categories; CON's own concession in A3.2 ("these costs attach regardless of work status") walked into this trap. CON's counter—that volume surges add more total households—was plausible but speculative and required CON to win several independent empirical claims simultaneously. B also kept the "existing 180-day mechanism" framing largely unanswered throughout: CON never squarely rebutted that adjudication proceeds in parallel and that screening isn't actually compressed. Both sides had minor execution costs (clipped speeches), roughly canceling. B's rebuttal work was sharper, particularly the "you cannot have it both ways" crystallization, and the progression across rounds built a cleaner path to the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7) crystallizing the fatal contradiction: "CON has argued that work is simultaneously attractive enough to trigger fraudulent claims and weak enough to offset nothing. These cannot both be true." Combined with the observation that CON had already conceded indirect costs are fixed regardless of work status, this was the most decisive single exchange, forcing CON's A4.1 resolution—which came too late and remained only partially developed.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (CON)): Side B wins by cleanly resolving the core clash over the "magnet" paradox and focusing the debate on application volume. Side A attempts to trap B by arguing that if work is lucrative enough to attract fraudulent claims, it must be lucrative enough to offset public costs. B brilliantly breaks this paradox in Rebuttal 2 by explaining that entry-level US wages are a massive private incentive for migrants (driving up application volume), but those low-wage jobs do not generate enough tax revenue to offset the fixed public costs of that increased caseload. Side A's closing ignores this critical volume dynamic and merely repeats the broken paradox. Despite a confusingly phrased Pressure Question from B, B's substantive superiority secures a clear win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which dismantles Side A's "paradox" trap by clearly distinguishing between the private wage incentive that draws migrant volume and the insufficient public tax yield that fails to offset systemic costs.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): PRO won by offering a coherent institutional alternative—parallel screening with revocable permits—that undermined CON's central claim that 60-day authorization necessarily bypasses vetting and invites fraud. PRO's fiscal framework (fixed indirect costs plus added tax contributions) and the sharp paradox attack exposing CON's tension between "work is a magnet" and "work is too weak to reduce dependence" proved decisive. CON's volume and embedding arguments had intuitive appeal but never adequately answered why reducing the timeline from 180 to 60 days, rather than eliminating work authorization itself, would trigger the surges they predicted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (B4), which used the revocability of work permits and the fact that preliminary screening already precedes the current 180-day clock to neutralize CON's fraud and embedding fears, while turning CON's conceded "fixed indirect costs" point into a winning fiscal comparison.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (CON)): CON wins by successfully shifting the weighing to systemic volume and net fiscal impact. While PRO lands a sharp rhetorical blow on CON’s apparent contradiction (work as a fraud magnet vs. work as fiscally insufficient), CON adequately resolves it by distinguishing the immediate attraction of legal status/quick cash from the long-term inadequacy of low-wage earnings for full independence. CON’s argument that a 60-day pre-screening window triggers a surge in applicants, multiplying fixed indirect costs and politically embedding workers before credibility checks, directly answers the proposition’s claim about reducing aggregate reliance. PRO’s per-person fiscal improvement is logically sound but ultimately outweighed by CON’s dynamic analysis of how the policy change alters incentives, swells the total pool, and prolongs stays. PRO’s dismissal of the volume surge as merely an "enforcement flaw" leaves the core incentive shift unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s R2 and Closing attack on CON’s internal tension (“work can’t be both a massive magnet and fiscally useless”) was the sharpest exchange, forcing CON to explicitly resolve the paradox through volume weighing and distinguish legal-status attraction from wage-driven self-sufficiency. CON’s survival and pivot to net aggregate costs ultimately decided the round.
