### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): B won by holding the right frame throughout. The motion takes crowding-out as given and asks whether restrictions are appropriate—not whether restrictions alone solve the entire housing crisis. A's strongest argument, that restrictions merely reroute scarcity without adding supply, was sharp and well-articulated through the "shell game" and "ten households, three homes" images. But A never fully engaged with B's core claim that large institutional investors are categorically different from other competitors—that their scale, cheap capital, and simultaneous portfolio acquisitions create a structural advantage no individual bidder or small landlord can match. Removing that actor may not eliminate all competition, but it ends the most systematically lopsided competition in the starter-home segment. A's "build more" counter is sound long-run policy but sidesteps the motion's tight-market, immediate-crowding-out scenario, which B correctly and repeatedly reanchored as the operative question: who gets the scarce homes that already exist today? B also handled the renter objection adequately—a buyer exiting the rental pool reduces rental pressure, and multifamily is the more appropriate channel for rental investment—though B never fully silenced A's capital-flight concern. The minor execution cost from B's clipped Rebuttal 1 barely matters since the key arguments preceded the clip. A's rhetorical craftsmanship (shell game, "renames the lock") was the debate's most memorable writing, but B's alignment with the motion's actual question kept its case more decisive overall.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was A's "reroutes scarcity" argument in A4.1—that removing one institutional bidder still leaves nine households competing over three homes, price unchanged—against B's response in B4.1-4.6 that the local buyer exiting the rental market eases rental pressure and that institutional scale/cheap capital creates a qualitatively different competitive advantage. A's attack was the sharpest single pressure in the debate; B's answer that the house still shelters a family while the institution's privileged position ends was adequate but never fully explained why prices or access odds would materially improve for a first-time buyer when other well-capitalized private bidders remain. That gap left A with a live line of attack, but B's structural-advantage framing still held enough ground to sustain its case.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A wins by building a clearer path on immediate allocation of scarce stock to locals in tight markets as a bridge to supply fixes. A's rebuttals and direct A1/A2 answers neutralized B's rental-loss and capital-flight claims by showing tenure differences and narrow tools, while B's repeated "build more" pivot left the crowding-out harm unaddressed. No major format or grounding violations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.2 directly answering B's pressure questions on renters and capital shifts, reframing exit as ending crowd-out.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A wins by successfully narrowing the clash to short-term allocation and the non-interchangeability of renting versus owning. While Side B consistently argued that restrictions fail to address underlying supply scarcity, Side A effectively conceded the long-term need for building while demonstrating why immediate, targeted allocation rules matter for wealth-building and neighborhood stability. Side A's rebuttals directly answered Side B's pressure on renters and capital flight by explaining how local purchases ease rental demand and why policy success does not require forced capital migration. Side B's case, though economically coherent, relied heavily on a long-term supply frame that never fully engaged with Side A's core weighing on tenure differences and immediate crowd-out, making Side A's path to victory cleaner and more responsive to the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 sharply neutralized Side B's renter-harm and capital-flight pressure by clarifying that a local purchase removes a household from the rental queue (easing, not worsening, rental pressure) and that the policy's goal is simply stopping institutional crowd-out, not dictating where every investment dollar goes. This directly resolved the central trade-off and kept the debate focused on allocation and tenure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins on the decisive clash: the "missing link" between banning institutional buyers and making ownership actually reachable for local households. PRO's core mechanism — "stock preservation" — answers whether more homes remain formally owner-occupied, but never closes the gap to whether the crowded-out buyer can actually afford and secure one of those homes. CON's arithmetic held throughout: in a market where ten families chase eight houses, removing two institutional bids leaves ten families still chasing eight houses. PRO's response that this "stops the ownership pool from shrinking" is a real point but a narrower one — it defends a structural category, not demonstrable buyer access. CON effectively named this in the closing as "symbolism against scarcity" and repeatedly pressed that preserving an ownership label is not the same as making ownership reachable. PRO's strongest countermove — the feedback loop framing and the equity-vs-rental distinction — carried genuine force and kept the debate competitive, but CON's rebuttal correctly noted that families live in rentals too and that homes return to market when portfolios turn over, blunting "permanent conversion." On enforcement, PRO's beneficial-ownership answer was reasonable but CON credibly replied that every definitional line invites evasion. PRO's pressure questions were pointed, but CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to Q2 — "deleting bidders does not give a household its only chance; nine families still chase eight houses" — directly neutralized PRO's most emotionally resonant framing. Neither side introduced fake citations or brittle precision; both argued from plausible general knowledge. CON simply made the burden of the proposition clearer and kept PRO from proving it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's Q2 ("why sacrifice a household's only chance to own to preserve a seller's surplus of bidders?"): CON turned the framing back by pointing out that removing institutional bids still leaves nine families chasing eight houses, so the local buyer has no guaranteed "only chance." This directly neutralized PRO's most emotionally persuasive argument and exposed that PRO's mechanism depends on assuming the institutional buyer is the marginal bidder the local family would otherwise beat — an assumption PRO never proved.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): B wins by sustaining the core claim that restrictions merely reshuffle scarce bidders without creating supply, lowering barriers, or proving a path to ownership for locals, directly answering A's pressure questions on mechanism and feasibility. A relies on "stock preservation" and structural conversion but never bridges how that makes homes attainable in tight markets. B's closings crystallize the missing link more sharply across the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2, which confronts the pressure on concrete mechanisms and enforcement by noting unchanged scarcity and easy evasion, exposing the unproven leap in A's ownership-pool preservation.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by consistently exposing the missing causal link in Side A's case: removing institutional bidders does not automatically lower prices, ease financing, or guarantee that cash-constrained local buyers will win the bid. While Side A effectively framed the debate around "stock preservation" and stopping financial extraction, Side B's arithmetic clash (scarcity still sets terms; nine families still chase eight houses) and practical market realities (renovation needs, seller liquidity, evasion risks) proved more decisive. Side B successfully argued that the policy is a symbolic reshuffle that fails to solve crowd-out, whereas Side A never fully bridged the gap between banning funds and actually empowering local families to purchase homes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 sharply dismantled Side A's "stock preservation" mechanism by pointing out that deleting one bidder class doesn't lower mortgage costs, create inventory, or guarantee a local buyer wins against other wealthy purchasers, effectively neutralizing PRO's core path to victory.
