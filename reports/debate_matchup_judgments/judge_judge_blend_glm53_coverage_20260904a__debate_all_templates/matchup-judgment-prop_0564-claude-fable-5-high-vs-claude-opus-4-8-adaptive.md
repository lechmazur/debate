CON then successfully framed that scarce political capital for taxation should buy network expansion, not merely remove the meter for existing riders, and flipped PRO's own "certainty" frame against them in a decisive closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's severability argument that all-door boarding and frequency gains can be captured via proof-of-payment systems without sacrificing fare revenue, which directly neutralized PRO's claim that fare-free policy uniquely improves transit speed and service.

While PRO built a strong moral and operational case around enforcement costs, boarding speed, and tax stability, CON effectively severed the speed benefit (proof-of-payment), mitigated the enforcement concern (civil vs. criminal administration), and landed a devastating opportunity-cost argument: even if taxation funds the backfill, the political capital required to pass it is scarce, and spending it on free rides for existing users yields far less than spending it on frequency and coverage. CON also brilliantly turned PRO’s closing “certainty vs. speculation” frame, noting that the only guaranteed outcome is spending massive capital for modest gains while leaving service gaps unfilled. CON’s rebuttals were sharper, directly answered pressure, and crystallized a cleaner voting issue.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by making the finite-dollar comparison decisive: if a city can raise broad taxes, riders are better served by buying frequency and reliability while targeting free fares to those who need them. A persuasively showed that fares are regressive and burdensome, but never proved that administrative savings, dignity, and faster boarding outweigh the opportunity cost of replacing a major revenue stream for everyone. B also answered A's tax and means-testing points well enough to keep the service-quality tradeoff central.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was in Rebuttal 2, where it answered A's fare-collection argument by saying modern payment systems can capture much of the boarding benefit while fares still provide far more revenue than collection costs consume, turning A's savings claim into “dollars traded for dimes.”
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO)): CON won by sustaining the central opportunity-cost comparison: even if fare revenue is fully replaced by taxes, finite fiscal capacity means zeroing fares for everyone crowds out spending on frequency, reliability, and maintenance that riders actually feel. CON effectively blunted PRO's administrative-savings counter by showing modern fare-collection costs are a tiny fraction of lost revenue, and sharpened the equity debate by demonstrating that targeted free fares for the poor plus service investment dominates universal free fares funded by regressive broad-based taxation. PRO landed solid hits on CON's tax-capacity inconsistency and correctly noted CON attacked absent congestion claims, but never fully resolved the per-dollar trade-off or the 'dignity is a bus that comes' crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response that modern fare collection (contactless tap, all-door boarding, proof-of-payment, auto-enrollment) reduces collection costs to low single digits of revenue, while fares supply a third to half of operating budgets—framed as 'trading dollars to save dimes'—which decisively undermined PRO's claim that eliminating fares is a self-funding service improvement.

While Side B (PRO) effectively argued the moral and equity case against regressive fares, CON consistently forced the debate back to the per-dollar trade-off: finite tax capacity means backfilling fares buys stagnation, whereas investing in service plus targeted fares delivers tangible improvements for the poor. CON's "dollars for dimes" rebuttal successfully exposed the mathematical weakness in PRO's claim that administrative savings would offset lost fare revenue, and CON's practical solutions (all-door boarding, contactless tech, auto-enrollment) neutralized PRO's arguments about boarding speed and means-testing stigma. CON also better addressed the political economy of sustaining permanent taxes without visible service gains. PRO's closing relied heavily on the "dignity/guarantee" frame but failed to resolve the scale mismatch on costs or answer why a free but stagnant system beats a targeted, high-frequency alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 directly answered PRO's pressure on administrative savings by demonstrating that modern proof-of-payment and all-door boarding capture speed gains without surrendering fare revenue, then crystallized the efficiency clash with the "trading dollars for dimes" comparison. This mathematically dismantled PRO's claim that going fare-free "funds itself" and secured CON's path to victory on the per-dollar weighing.
