### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO built and sustained a coherent distinction throughout: existing law regulates product claims, but not the synthetic replication of the endorser's identity. That gap is genuine and CON never fully closed it. CON's best work was the syllable-regeneration example attacking PRO's "clear legal line" and the enforcement challenge (bad actors just record manually), which PRO never fully answered on the catching-cheaters dimension. However, PRO correctly pivoted in Rebuttal 2 to expand the harm beyond malicious liars: the industrialization of parasocial trust at scale is a real problem even for honest creators, and CON's framing of this as a PRO concession was rhetorically sharp but argumentatively weak — PRO added a harm category, not abandoned the first. CON's closing crystallization ("this rule does not stop lies, it only punishes convenience") is memorable but rests on the unstated premise that informing consumers about who is speaking has no independent value. PRO's closing framing — "faking the endorser" is categorically different from "lying about a product," and existing law covers the second but not the first — was cleaner and left a live route to victory CON never neutralized. The photo/deepfake analogy in PRO's Rebuttal 1 was the clearest single rebuttal, directly dismantling CON's "no coherent line" attack. CON's enforcement gap and the hobbyist-burden arguments are real costs PRO only partially addressed, keeping this a clear but competitive result rather than a dominant one.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's photo-filter vs. deepfake analogy in Rebuttal 1 (A2.1–A2.2): it directly collapsed CON's false-equivalence attack by giving a workable, intuitive bright line — enhancement of a human recording versus synthesizing a vocal performance from text — forcing CON to rely on the edge-case syllable-regeneration example rather than the central claim that no line exists at all.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A won by making the decisive distinction between ordinary fake product claims and the separate deception of synthetic host presence. B’s best points on line-drawing and bad actors recording manually were relevant, but A answered them with a workable enhancement-versus-generation distinction and showed that the mandate is about transparency, not banning AI. B’s burden arguments were more speculative than A’s low-cost disclosure framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal most decisively damaged B’s case by rejecting the audio-enhancement conflation and framing AI voice ads as synthetic performance rather than mere editing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side B won by consistently showing the rule burdens honest creators and tools without stopping actual fake endorsements (which bad actors bypass via manual recording) while existing fraud standards already cover deception; A's right-to-know framing was philosophically appealing but never resolved the enforceability and overbreadth problems.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 2 exposed that the rule targets scaling efficiency on genuine endorsements rather than fraud, directly answering the pressure questions.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A's strongest structural move — the internal contradiction embedded in Side B's case — was introduced in Rebuttal 1 and never cleanly escaped. If audiences do not care about the production method, mandatory disclosure costs them nothing; if they would care, they are entitled to know. Side B's attempt to resolve this by distinguishing "caring about production" from "aesthetic disruption from a robot reminder" actually conceded the point: if the disclosure shatters the show's aesthetic, that is precisely the information listeners are being denied. Side B's definitional-ambiguity argument was its best material challenge, but Side A handled it adequately by invoking the reasonable-person standard, good-faith safe harbors, and the common-sense gap between fixing one syllable and generating a full performance. Side B kept returning to the "manual labor" framing to characterize Side A's position, but this was a caricature: the real issue is whether the human whose voice the audience trusts actually spoke, not whether they physically stood at a microphone. Side B's other key pillars — existing law adequacy and disclosure fatigue — were plausible but underweighted. Existing false-advertising law targets false product claims, not the undisclosed substitution of a human voice with its AI replica; that gap was never closed. Disclosure fatigue is a real phenomenon but does not outweigh an audience's entitlement to basic information about what they are hearing. Side A also answered both pressure questions directly with A1/A2 labels, handled them honestly rather than evasively, and carried the contradiction argument cleanly through the closing. Side B was technically competent and raised genuine concerns, but its core structural response to the proposition's strongest ground never landed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 [A2.5–A2.6]: The logical trap — "if audiences do not mind, the ad performs exactly the same; if they would mind, that is exactly proof this is material information they are entitled to" — was the decisive exchange. Side B's answer in [B2.3] tried to distinguish production indifference from aesthetic disruption, but that response actually confirmed that audiences do care, leaving the entitlement-to-know argument standing throughout the remainder of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): A won by making the materiality of the missing human performance clearer: the audience reasonably believes the host actually said the endorsement, and a short disclosure addresses that specific deception without banning the tool. B raised a credible workability and over-disclosure concern, but leaned too heavily on edge cases like noise cleanup and syllable repair that A plausibly excluded from the rule. B's existing-law argument also did not fully answer the distinct deception about host involvement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response that if disclosure does not matter it will not hurt performance, but if it does hurt performance then the fact is material, was the cleanest rebuttal and framed B's disclosure-fatigue argument as weaker than the transparency interest.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A wins by establishing and defending a workable bright line, directly answering pressure questions to neutralize the quagmire claim, and repeatedly exposing the internal contradiction in Side B's position on audience indifference vs. performance harm. Side B's edge-case focus and reframing never recover after rebuttal 2.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 directly answers B3's pressure questions, then uses the responses to dismantle the entire "unworkable definitions" route that underpinned B's case.
