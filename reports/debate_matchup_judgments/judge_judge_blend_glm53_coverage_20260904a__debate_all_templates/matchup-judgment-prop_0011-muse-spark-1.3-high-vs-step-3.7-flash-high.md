### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by making mixed test-score evidence less decisive than safety, fit, equal access to choice, and the irreversible loss of a child’s school years. Side B raised serious fixed-cost, selection, and accountability concerns, but repeatedly asserted sweeping exclusion and harm claims without enough grounding, did not adequately answer why a guaranteed public-school seat constitutes meaningful access when that school is failing, and suffered two clipped rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest response contrasted visible private-school failure, for which the public system remains a fallback, with chronic public-school failure where families have no immediate exit. That directly weakened Side B’s claim that legal universality and democratic oversight necessarily provide better practical accountability.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a highly effective moral framework—that children should not be treated as revenue to subsidize a failing system. Side B struggled with execution, getting clipped in both rebuttals, and failed to adequately engage A's point that failing public schools act as an invisible, multi-year failure while the public system remains a fallback if a private school closes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 argument that a private school closing is a visible, actionable failure while a violent assigned school with no exit is an invisible, multi-year failure.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won by embracing the motion's built-in condition—expansion even when academic evidence is mixed—and converting it into a decisive advantage. Instead of disputing test scores, PRO showed parents seek safety, fit, discipline, and graduation, making mixed standardized-score evidence largely irrelevant. CON never squarely answered why an individual child should remain trapped in a violent or failing school to preserve district scale, and PRO's crystallization of "Fund the student, not the system" was far more memorable and weighing than CON's repeated appeals to universal-but-often-inadequate public mandates. CON's strongest argument—that vouchers structurally exclude the highest-need students—was partially neutralized by PRO's honest answer that severe-disability aid can remain weighted and protected while still allowing exits for families the district has failed. PRO's moral urgency, cleaner reframing, and sharper progression across rounds created the clearer live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's reframing of mixed standardized test scores as the wrong metric for judging vouchers—analogized to judging hospitals only by weight loss—which directly defanged CON's attempt to make mixed academic evidence dispositive under a motion that explicitly asked for a decision "even when" gains are mixed, paired with PRO's answer that weighted funding for severe disabilities can be protected while still permitting exits.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing Side A's higher-per-pupil claim as a denominator trick: fewer total dollars cannot cover largely unchanged fixed costs merely because spending per remaining student rises. B also showed that testing and reporting do not answer unequal admissions, tuition, transportation, expulsion, and disability-service obligations. A mostly repeated its funding assertion, incorrectly claimed B had not answered non-academic benefits, and relied on suspiciously exact statistics and sweeping accountability claims without support. A's targeted-program framing was legitimate, but it did not resolve B's central resource and access harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer that scattered enrollment losses cannot proportionally reduce teachers, buildings, buses, or boilers directly defeated A's central funding arithmetic and explained why flexible services would absorb the cuts.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively dismantling Side A's two main pillars. B's explanation of the 'denominator trick' effectively neutralized A's claim that public schools become richer when students leave, and B's distinction between 'accountability' and 'gatekeeping' exposed the flaws in A's equity argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's distinction in Rebuttal 2 that 'The gap is the right to be served at all' perfectly neutralized A's claims about voucher school accountability.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing the critical flaw in Side A's fiscal argument and maintaining pressure on the equity harms of selection. A's central claim that vouchers increase per-pupil funding for remaining public school students was effectively reframed by B as a "denominator trick"—total revenue falls while fixed costs remain, forcing cuts to the flexible services that protect vulnerable students. A never resolved this practical bind, merely repeating that funding formulas separate fixed and variable costs without explaining how a rural school losing ten scattered students avoids cuts. B also successfully cast doubt on A's equity narrative by showing that private voucher schools can reject applicants, charge above the voucher, and lack special-ed obligations, meaning the families most able to navigate barriers exit while the majority are left in poorer schools. A's retreat to income-capped programs felt narrow given the motion's general wording, and B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly: mixed gains for a selected few cannot justify certain resource harm to the unselected majority. B's rebuttals were sharper, more memorable, and did more material damage to A's live routes to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's reframing of A's per-pupil funding claim as a "denominator trick" (B2.1, B4.1), showing that fixed costs don't shrink when scattered students leave, so the shortfall lands on counselors, arts, and aides—not on the abstract "higher per-pupil" calculus.
