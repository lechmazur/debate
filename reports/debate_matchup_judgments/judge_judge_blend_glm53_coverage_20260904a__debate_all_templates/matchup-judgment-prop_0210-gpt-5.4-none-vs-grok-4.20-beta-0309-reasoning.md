### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON)): PRO built a more durable path to victory by converting CON's strongest concession into a structural weakness. When CON repeatedly argued that regulators already see all negative data, PRO correctly identified that this proves the data is material—and then pressed the unanswered question: why should guideline panels, formulary committees, HTA bodies, and independent researchers be confined to filtered regulatory summaries rather than the underlying record? CON's "filtration is the feature, not the bug" reframe was clever but underdeveloped; it never explained why expert synthesis at the regulatory level is a satisfying substitute for scrutiny by the broader healthcare ecosystem that makes post-approval decisions. PRO's sharpest original move—"disclosure punishes secrecy, not failure"—effectively deflated the innovation-chilling argument without conceding it. The corollary ("if a drug's commercial case weakens when hidden negatives become visible, transparency is revealing existing harm, not causing it") was never squarely answered. CON's innovation argument remained speculative and somewhat circular: firms avoid transparency to protect pipelines, but that model depends on hiding evidence, which is precisely what the motion targets. PRO's format costs from repeated clipping are real execution penalties, but the substantive case that came through was consistently stronger, and CON never neutralized the downstream-actors argument across any round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's [A2.1]–[A2.2] in Rebuttal 1: turning CON's "regulators already demand full datasets" concession into a proof of materiality, then reframing the entire debate as whether that material evidence stays trapped inside the agency or flows to the clinicians, guideline writers, insurers, and researchers who make post-approval decisions. CON's response ("filtration is the feature") was clever but never demonstrated why those downstream actors should accept regulatory synthesis as a ceiling rather than a floor for scrutiny.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON)): Side A established that market access is a public privilege requiring public evidence, and that regulatory review alone cannot substitute for independent scrutiny by downstream decision-makers. While Side B landed serious concerns about innovation chilling and therapeutic deserts, it repeatedly overstated the policy as a 'blunt veto' that blocks approval, whereas Side A clarified that regulators still judge benefit-risk while sponsors must simply disclose the full record. Side B's execution was cleaner—Side A incurred real costs from multiple clipped speeches that cut off final weighing sentences—but Side A's central framework and rebuttal to the innovation scare remained live after the exchange. The decisive clash resolved in favor of transparency as a proportionate condition for selling to the public.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction between punishing scientific failure and punishing the commercialization of selectively revealed evidence, which blunted Side B's innovation-chill argument by reframing hidden negatives as a market distortion rather than a necessary protection for risky research.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully isolating the gap between regulatory approval and downstream medical decision-making. This dismantled Side B’s blunt veto framing and sharpened the clash around downstream scrutiny versus sponsor-controlled filtering.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): CON wins a clear but competitive debate primarily because its pressure questions exposed a genuine definitional vulnerability—and PRO's answer at A4.1 ("all clinical trial results conducted in the development program," including exploratory studies, failed dose-finding, and feasibility-stopped trials) was the pivotal moment. Rather than narrowing the motion to where it does the most good, PRO embraced the maximalist reading, which CON then spent the back half of the debate exploiting under a "necessity vs. excess" frame that PRO never cleanly defeated. CON's core distinction—that full regulator submission plus mandatory disclosure of clinically material trials can solve the selective-publication problem without requiring countries to make a sale illegal until every unrelated dead end is published—remained a live alternative throughout. PRO's best argument was that voluntary and targeted tools have already proven inadequate and that blocked revenue is the only lever that truly changes incentives. This is genuinely strong, but PRO never showed why that lever must be calibrated to demand everything rather than being targeted to studies relevant to the approved indication. CON's deterrence point (filing chills in smaller markets) was somewhat speculative, but PRO's rebuttal—that a genuinely beneficial drug gains from disclosure while marginal ones deserve the barrier—was only partially persuasive, because CON correctly noted that an otherwise-approvable therapy can be deterred by a rule demanding disclosure of genuinely irrelevant development intelligence. Both openings were clipped, and CON's rebuttal and closing were clipped, which is a real execution cost; however, enough of CON's reasoning survived intact that the proportionality frame and overbreadth argument came through. PRO's grounding was clean and its core logic sound, but the debate's progression narrowed the live clash to whether the specific rule as written is necessary, and CON held the better position on that question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure Q1 (asking PRO to define what exactly counts as a "negative result") was the most decisive exchange. PRO's direct answer at A4.1—all negative results across the entire development program, including exploratory studies and feasibility stops—confirmed CON's overbreadth critique. CON then spent Rebuttal 2 and the closing specifically quoting that answer back as the "round-losing concession," demonstrating that the policy as PRO defended it would deny patients an otherwise-approvable therapy until unrelated development dead ends were also published, a condition CON correctly identified as disproportionate to the stated patient-safety goal.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): Side B won by successfully narrowing the debate to whether every negative result in a development program must be publicly disclosed before market access. Side A's explicit concession that exploratory, feasibility, and dose-finding failures must all be disclosed [A4.1] allowed CON to frame the motion as an excessive, punitive barrier rather than a targeted safety rule. CON showed that if regulators already review full confidential data and clinically material trials are reported, blocking access over unrelated negative development data uses patients as leverage for transparency beyond safety necessity. PRO never adequately justified why unrelated exploratory failures must be public to approve the specific product, and CON's "necessity versus excess" frame provided the cleaner comparative lens by the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure questions and subsequent rebuttals pinning PRO to defending mandatory public disclosure of all exploratory, feasibility, and subgroup trials in the entire development program, then framing that breadth as punishment for nonpublication rather than a necessary patient-protection condition for market access.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Reasoning) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully narrowing the clash to proportionality and necessity. While Side A effectively highlighted the dangers of selective publication and the need for strong incentives, Side B forced a critical concession in Rebuttal 2 where A committed to disclosing all development program results, including unrelated exploratory and feasibility-stopped trials. B exploited this overbreadth masterfully, arguing that blocking market access for an otherwise safe drug over unrelated negative data is punitive rather than protective, and risks deterring launches. A's reliance on "market access is a privilege" failed to meet the burden of justifying such a sweeping condition against B's targeted alternative. B's closing crystallized the necessity vs. excess clash decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which directly leveraged Side A's explicit commitment to disclose all development program results (A4.1) to demonstrate the policy's fatal overbreadth, shifting the debate from "transparency vs secrecy" to "necessary safety rule vs excessive gatekeeping."
