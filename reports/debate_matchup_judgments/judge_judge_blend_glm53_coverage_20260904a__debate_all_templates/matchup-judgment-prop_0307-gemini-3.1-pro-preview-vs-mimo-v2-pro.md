### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won the three decisive clashes. First, on the human-learning analogy: PRO consistently pressed that industrial-scale, zero-marginal-cost output is categorically different from human inspiration, and CON's own Rebuttal 2 partially conceded this ("AI's industrial scale and zero marginal cost are features of automated reproduction"), a concession PRO exploited cleanly in the closing. Second, on how defaults actually work: PRO's explanation that a stronger default shifts the compliance burden to capitalized tech firms while requiring zero action from the individual creator was never squarely answered; CON's "byzantine permission architecture" counter was plausible but applied better to an active licensing regime than to a simple opt-out default. Third, on the enclosure/paywall clash: PRO's inversion—that current unregulated scraping is what drives artists behind paywalls, while default protection lets them share safely—was more intuitive and better answered than CON's mirror claim. CON's "toll booth" counter had genuine force, but PRO's answer (a corporation paying an artist for her portfolio is a functioning market, not a toll booth) largely neutralized it. CON's strongest original argument—that granting a veto over AI training creates an unprecedented property right over the act of learning itself—was interesting and partially landed, but CON never built it into a decisive comparative story about which regime produces the better ecosystem. CON's closing also suffered from two empty reference-tag placeholders ([A2.2] and [A4.3]) that replaced substantive crystallization, a real execution cost in the round where narrowing the clash matters most. PRO's closing, by contrast, sharpened the central question—who is entitled to the economic value of human creativity—and used CON's own concession as the final word.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q2 was the most decisive exchange. CON asked what prevents creators from monetizing the veto into exclusive licenses that fragment the commons. PRO answered that a multi-billion-dollar corporation paying an artist to use her portfolio is not a toll booth—it is a functioning, equitable market—and then turned the framing: it is the current unregulated extraction regime that destroys the public commons by forcing artists to hide their work entirely. This response neutralized CON's strongest original counter-argument rather than evading it, and it set up PRO's closing use of CON's own scale-concession.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON)): Side A won by making the central comparison clearer: commercial AI scraping is not ordinary human learning because scale, automation, and market substitution change the burden and harms. Side B had a coherent commons and overbroad-property-right objection, but it repeatedly overstated the motion as criminalizing learning generally and never fully answered why creators rather than commercial AI platforms should bear the default burden. A also handled the enclosure clash better by arguing that licensing is a market, while unconsented scraping is what drives creators behind paywalls.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was the answer to the human-learning analogy and default-burden point: it separated individual inspiration from mass commercial harvesting and showed why a default right shifts costs to the firms profiting from the data rather than adding work for creators.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B (PRO) maintained a tighter causal story around economic survival and burden-shifting, successfully turning the enclosure clash by arguing that a default right enables safe public sharing while the status quo forces creators behind paywalls. PRO dismantled CON's core "human learning" analogy by consistently emphasizing industrial scale, zero marginal cost, and direct market substitution. CON critically undermined its own case in Rebuttal 2 by conceding that AI's scale makes it "automated reproduction" rather than learning, a concession PRO exploited in the closing. CON also suffered execution costs with unrendered reference tags in the final speech. PRO's weighing on who should bear compliance costs was sharper and more responsive to the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 cleanly resolved the pressure questions by noting that distinguishing scrapers from humans is standard network management, not invasive surveillance, and sharply framed the central tradeoff: whether independent artists or capitalized tech firms should bear the cost of the AI transition. This forced CON into a damaging concession about AI being automated reproduction, which decisively broke CON's learning analogy.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (PRO)): This was a genuinely competitive debate with both sides executing their core cases cleanly. PRO's decisive advantages were: (1) the consent-as-default framing, which carries strong intuitive weight and was never fully neutralized—CON never answered the core question of why the burden should be on creators to opt out of a use they never contemplated; (2) PRO's turn on the monopoly argument in Rebuttal 1 [B2.5], arguing the status quo is itself a platform monopoly, effectively shifted the terrain so CON had to defend the current arrangement rather than simply warn about PRO's proposal; (3) the distinction between human learning and industrial-scale commercial product development is morally compelling and CON's scale-doesn't-change-nature response, while philosophically coherent, never fully addressed why competition from a direct commercial AI substitute is equivalent to a human artist learning from peers. CON's strongest live argument—transaction costs destroying open-source access—remained partially unanswered, as PRO's tiered licensing response [B4.1] still faces the bureaucratic overhead problem CON raised [A4.3]. CON's Common Crawl point was a sharp specific counter. On balance, PRO held the stronger position because the monopoly turn cut CON's central pillar, the consent framing was persistent and compelling, and CON's most powerful argument (transaction costs) was blunted enough by tiered licensing to deny it decisive weight.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's monopoly turn in Rebuttal 1 [B2.5–B2.6]: by arguing that the status quo already guarantees monopoly power to platforms—who unilaterally harvest creative value—PRO reframed CON's central concern so that CON was now defending the arrangement that most benefits incumbents. This forced CON to spend two subsequent rounds explaining why their position actually favors openness, shifting the burden effectively and never fully recovering the original framing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): B won by giving the cleaner limiting principle: public work can be analyzed for uncopyrightable patterns, and a new opt-in right would create severe transaction costs that favor large platforms with private data. A’s consent and creator-harm framing was compelling, but its answer to the monopoly/open-source problem relied on speculative flexible licensing and did not fully explain why scale turns analysis into a right to exclude.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was against A’s tiered-licensing answer: B pressed that even free or flexible opt-ins require locating, verifying, and managing millions of permissions, making the proposed default practically favor the largest firms.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully flipping the monopoly argument and exposing the fatal transaction-cost flaw in PRO’s opt-in licensing model. While PRO offered a compelling moral frame around exploitation and consent, CON grounded the debate in the practical reality of data analysis: even “free” tiered licenses require massive legal and logistical overhead that would crush open-source developers, leaving only walled-garden tech giants able to train models. CON’s search-engine analogy and clear distinction between analyzing public patterns versus owning expression gave a cleaner, more resilient comparative path. PRO’s rebuttal on tiered licensing failed to answer the administrative burden, leaving CON’s market-structure weighing decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 dismantling PRO’s “tiered licensing” solution by highlighting the insurmountable transaction costs of tracking and verifying millions of individual permissions, even for free/open-source use. This turned PRO’s pro-competition framing into a liability and cemented CON’s monopoly-flip argument.
