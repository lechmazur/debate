### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON built a consistent alternative — risk-triggered validation plus universal recordkeeping — and repeatedly pressed PRO to explain what the categorical "high-risk" label adds once PRO conceded that simple, transparent tools need only "light" verification. That concession in Rebuttal 2 (A1) was the pivotal moment: it let CON argue in both remaining speeches that PRO's own answers collapsed into CON's proportional, risk-based framework, meaning the extra categorical mandate was doing no independent work while still imposing a differential compliance cost that could push employers toward less accountable subjective judgment. PRO's strongest material — the asymmetry argument (undetected harm at scale vs. bounded testing cost) and the "who bears uncertainty" framing — was rhetorically effective and never fully erased, but PRO never cleanly rebutted CON's substitution-incentive mechanism (that equalized recordkeeping still leaves a marginal cost differential that changes employer behavior at the margin) nor closed the gap opened by its own light-touch concession. Both closings were clipped, but CON's closing landed a sharper, more specific final blow by directly quoting PRO's concession back at them, while PRO's closing largely restated the asymmetry point without answering the "what does the label add" question. This makes CON's case marginally cleaner and more resolved by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 ("if inputs/outputs are genuinely identical, marginal risk is low and testing should be light") conceded ground that CON then exploited relentlessly in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to argue the categorical high-risk label adds nothing beyond CON's own targeted validation regime — a concession PRO never fully neutralized.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON maintained a live targeted alternative (method-neutral records + validation triggered by opacity/material reliance on unvalidated proxies) while PRO twice clipped on word limit. PRO's proportionality defense conceded that the licence-checker needs only light verification, which collapses into CON's accuracy checks and leaves the incremental value of mandatory bias testing for every algorithmic tool unjustified. CON also answered pressure with an objective trigger and explained marginal substitution, whereas PRO never resolved why bias testing adds beyond implementation checks for simple rules or how "strongest feasible" stops endless inquiry in small promotion pools.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's paired attack on the licence-checker and small-pool cases in R2: showing that implementation checks cover scale errors, that bias testing adds no benefit when the rule is independently justified, and that PRO's "light verification" concession removes the need for a categorical high-risk label.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully dismantling the necessity of the categorical high-risk label. Side A forced Side B to concede that simple tools only require light verification, which Side A effectively used to show that a targeted, method-neutral approach achieves the same safeguards without the overbreadth and perverse incentives of the categorical mandate. Side A's economic argument regarding the marginal substitution effect toward subjective human judgment was sharp and largely unresolved by Side B, who relied on general assertions about employer efficiency rather than engaging with marginal differential costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dissected Side B's proportionality defense, pointing out that if simple tools only need light verification, the high-risk label adds no demonstrated protection, and pressing the lack of an operational limit for small-pool testing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): The clash centered on whether "algorithm + large employer" is a coherent trigger, and on who bears the burden of surfacing discrimination absent mandated testing/audit trails. CON's strongest weapon—that PRO's tiered scrutiny concedes the categorical label does no work—landed and was never fully defused; PRO essentially bit the bullet ("a meaningful minimum protection with lighter compliance... deeper scrutiny") rather than showing why the universal floor is necessary rather than cosmetic. However, PRO's core structural argument—that rejected applicants cannot demonstrate what an opaque system did, and that records/testing shift the burden of investigation onto the party with information and incentive to hide problems—was never squarely answered. CON's alternative (targeted, complaint/disparity-triggered review) still requires someone to notice a disparity to trigger review, and PRO's Q1 pressure question exposed that gap: CON's A1 answer ("regulators use disparities, complaints, sampling... to trigger review") concedes that without records/testing, the disparity itself may never surface, especially since the whole point of opaque algorithmic scoring is that outputs look neutral. CON's rebuttal that audit trails "cannot alone establish discrimination without comparator data" is a fair point about limits, but doesn't refute that testing/audits make detection more likely than the status quo. On the pressure round, PRO's answers to A1/A2 were reasonably direct and used the required A1:/A2: format cleanly; CON's answers were also direct but leaned heavily on reasserting the danger-tracking framework without fully resolving the enforcement-trigger problem PRO raised. Overall PRO's closing crystallized the actual comparative stakes (burden-shifting, proportionate floor) more sharply, while CON's closing repeated the categorization critique effectively but left the evidentiary-asymmetry argument somewhat unaddressed. This gives PRO a clear but not dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B3.1 Q1 pressure question ("who is required to examine records before a worker can substantiate a complaint, and if nobody, what triggers enforcement?") forced CON to concede in A4.1 that enforcement relies on disparities/complaints/sampling to trigger review—exposing that CON's method-neutral alternative still depends on someone noticing what mandatory testing would proactively surface, which PRO leveraged effectively in the final rebuttal and closing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) sustained the affirmative burden on evidence asymmetry: opaque hiring tools repeat errors at scale and leave rejected applicants unable to prove discrimination without preserved evidence. CON (A) scored with the overinclusive license-filter and indeterminate fairness-standard critiques, but B answered with a workable floor — lighter checks for simple accuracy failures, deeper job-relevance and less-discriminatory-alternative review for predictive models, without requiring parity. CON's triage alternative never clearly identified who examines outcome records before a complaint can be substantiated, leaving the enforcement gap exposed by B's Q1 live. That clearer comparative path favors the mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 answers to A3 Q1: defining concrete failures as systematic rejection of valid licenses or unsubstantiated gap penalties, stating failure requires correction/retesting, government sets nondiscrimination standard, and less-discriminatory alternatives matter — directly neutralizing CON's "paperwork not substance" and failure-standard attack.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively exposing the category error in the proposition's trigger. By contrasting a simple algorithmic license check with an opaque human interview panel, Side A forced Side B to concede that the depth of scrutiny must vary based on a tool's actual opacity and autonomy. Side A effectively argued that if obligations are determined by these underlying risk factors, the blanket "high-risk" label based merely on employer size and the presence of software is an overinclusive misdirection. Side B made a strong case for proactive auditing over reactive triage, but could not overcome Side A's structural critique of the motion's regulatory line.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where it trapped Side B in a dilemma: either the "high-risk" label mandates identical, burdensome testing for all tools (which Side B denied), or it merely serves as an empty label while actual obligations are determined by opacity and autonomy (which Side A argued should be regulated directly). Side B's concession that scrutiny would be "lighter" for simple filters effectively handed Side A the structural clash.
