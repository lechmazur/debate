### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B won this debate by consistently landing three arguments that Side A never fully neutralized. First, B drew a legally accurate distinction between recognition (who speaks for a territory) and the separate legal architectures governing sovereign debt, asset control, and immunity. A's "master switch" framing was never rebutted with precision, and B's repeated insistence that targeted sanctions, Magnitsky measures, and ICC referrals do the pressure work without severing humanitarian channels remained a live alternative A never dismantled. Second, the Taiwan example was a decisive own-goal for A. A introduced Taiwan to show engagement can work without formal recognition—but that observation actually undermines A's own thesis that formal recognition is uniquely powerful and necessary. B then weaponized the same example to show that the world's most democratic, rights-compliant non-state actor is denied recognition for geopolitical reasons, proving B's core claim that a rights test would simply launder existing great-power discretion. A's Rebuttal 2 answer ("our framework would recognize Taiwan") was circular and did not address why Taiwan remains unrecognized under any framework. Third, when pressed on who certifies "minimum standards," A's answer—pointing to the UDHR—was thin and never showed how treaty language would be operationalized to constrain rather than empower dominant states. B's Myanmar and Crimea counterexamples also effectively rebutted A's claim that territorial control automatically generates recognition, exposing A's caricature of the opposing standard. A retained genuine force on the moral hazard of recognizing tyrants, but the combination of the legal-precision gap, the Taiwan boomerang, and the unanswered surgical-tools alternative gave B a clear comparative edge across the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal to PRO's Taiwan example [B2.4] was the most decisive single exchange. PRO introduced Taiwan to demonstrate that engagement thrives without formal recognition—inadvertently suggesting that the value of formal recognition A was defending was unnecessary. B immediately reversed the example to show that a fully rights-compliant entity is denied recognition purely for geopolitical reasons, which directly proved B's thesis that a rights-based test would expand rather than constrain great-power discretion. PRO never recovered from this inversion across the remaining rounds.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A wins by consistently clarifying recognition's limited legal function, correcting B's overstatements about assets/debt, and using concrete examples (Taiwan, Myanmar, Crimea) to show the proposition's practical failures. B's responses to pressure questions and rebuttals repeatedly inflate recognition into a master switch without adequate legal grounding. A's direct A1/A2 answers and functional framing create clearer comparative pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.2/A2.4 on Taiwan and legal separation vs. B's master-key claim, sharpened further in A4.1/A4.3
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively separating diplomatic recognition from the financial and legal powers Side B conflated it with, demonstrating that targeted sanctions and asset freezes are the actual mechanisms for pressure. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won the central framing contest. By consistently articulating recognition as a "dial not a switch," A neutralized B's most repeated argument—that conditional recognition means diplomatic blackout—throughout the debate. B kept attacking a blackout position A explicitly rejected, and never squared that circle. A's concrete examples (EU Copenhagen criteria, Helsinki Basket III, apartheid South Africa) were used with genuine argumentative precision rather than name-dropping: they illustrated the incentive mechanism and the proposition's plausibility. The strongest pressure in the debate was B's Q1—if informal diplomacy is sufficient, why would a regime bother reforming for formal recognition? A's A1 answered this adequately by distinguishing the full recognition package (IMF access, trade preferences, domestic legitimacy) from mere informal contact, though the clipping of A4 before its final sentence is a small execution cost. A's own Q1 (territorial control is equally politicized by the same powerful states B distrusts) was the most decisive single move in the debate; B's response in Rebuttal 2 claimed territorial control is "empirical and verifiable" but never adequately answered why powerful states' influence over who actually holds a capital is less political than minimum-standards judgments. B's historically accurate Helsinki point—recognition of borders came first under Basket I—was its best substantive rebuttal, and A never fully rebutted it, but A's counterclaim that Basket III's human-rights commitments were the actual lever for dissidents is also defensible and undermines B's use of the example as a categorical proof. B's case suffered from repetition across rounds (the same three claims recycled) and from never engaging A's "graduated" model seriously. A built a more consistent, adaptive case with a cleaner decision-rule by the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure Q1—pointing out that territorial control is itself a political decision made by the same powerful states B distrusts, and that recognition flows to coup-backed regimes backed by those states anyway—was the most damaging single move. B's response (territorial control is an "empirical fact") was visibly insufficient given how routinely external actors determine which side holds the capital, leaving B's central "objectivity" claim materially weakened for the rest of the debate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B wins by maintaining a clearer distinction between full recognition and ongoing diplomacy, better rebutting historical examples like Helsinki and China, and directly answering pressure questions on incentives and politicization; Side A’s pragmatism case is undercut by failing to neutralize the leverage mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) that ties legitimacy carrot to reform without blocking channels.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully reframing recognition as a graduated "dial" rather than an all-or-nothing "switch," directly neutralizing Side A's core fear of diplomatic blackouts and isolation. B's consistent use of EU conditionality and Helsinki Basket III as proof that standards-based leverage drives reform was more persuasive and less effectively answered than A's realist warnings about fragmentation. While A smartly turned the pre-1979 China example and grounded its case in pragmatic stability, it never adequately explained why territorial control is truly neutral or how unconditional recognition actually generates reform incentives. B handled pressure questions directly, maintained sharper comparative weighing across rounds, and closed with a cleaner crystallization of why conditional recognition preserves engagement while extracting concessions. A's later speeches leaned heavily on recap and failed to dismantle B's central mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2 dismantling of the "blackout" false dilemma. By establishing recognition as a dial and showing how graduated conditionality (EU/Helsinki) preserves functional channels while withholding the legitimacy prize, B materially damaged A's live route to victory. A's insistence that informal contact is a "dangerous illusion" never squared with B's documented examples of sustained engagement without full recognition.
