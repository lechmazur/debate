### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued well, but CON built the more durable structural case. CON's central mechanism argument — that voting weight is not the lever that controls actual emissions, since implementation remains sovereign and self-enforced — was never fully neutralized; PRO's answer (legitimacy feeding litigation/border adjustments) was met with the direct counter that those levers already exist under consensus, leaving the enforcement gap open. CON also exploited a real tension PRO created: in Rebuttal 1, PRO conceded that other existentially threatened states (Bangladesh, Sahel) would also gain voice under this logic, which CON turned into a clean dilemma (dilution vs. arbitrary favoritism) that PRO only partially repaired in the closing by asserting islands are the "limiting case." CON's pressure questions forced PRO into defending a metric ("share of territory rendered unlivable") that CON then persuasively branded as reverse-engineered, and PRO's closing concession that "every formula is chosen after the winner is known" reads as much as an admission as a rebuttal. PRO's moral framing and AOSIS/Paris examples were rhetorically strong and kept the debate close, but CON's closing crystallized the trade-off (authorship without enforcement, at the cost of the accountability consensus provides) more cleanly than PRO's final appeal to legitimacy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 dilemma [B2.1]-[B2.2], turning PRO's own concession about non-island existential risk into a collapse of the motion's scope, was the most decisive exchange; PRO's attempted fix in Rebuttal 2/Closing never fully escaped the "rigged criterion" charge CON pressed home.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): PRO maintained a live moral and institutional path — voice should track existential stake — and repaired CON's strongest attack. The swamping/rigging dilemma [B2.1] was answered by defining existential as share of state rendered uninhabitable — annihilation vs injury [A4.1] — which fits the proposition's wording and explains why Tuvalu can outweigh Dhaka without arbitrary island-favoritism. CON kept implementation realism alive but conceded structural power remains with emitters, weakening its own theater charge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The category-vs-risk swamping attack [B2.1-B2.2] and PRO's A1 reply [A4.1] distinguishing total state loss from partial catastrophic loss — dissolving the claim that Bangladesh would necessarily swamp Tuvalu and turning rigging into criterion-fit.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A built a more compelling and resilient case. They successfully defended the core mechanism of the proposition by distinguishing "existential risk" (state annihilation) from general climate vulnerability, effectively neutralizing Side B's "swamping" dilemma. Side A also won the implementation clash by demonstrating that the status quo's reliance on emitter consent has failed for thirty years, whereas risk-weighted baselines create tangible downstream leverage through litigation and border adjustments. Side B's arguments were practical and well-articulated, but they struggled to overcome Side A's sharp rebuttals and ultimately relied on defending a consensus model that Side A successfully framed as a perpetrator's veto.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A1 and A2), where they crisply defined existential risk as "annihilation versus injury" to solve the metric dilemma, and explained how outvoted text still creates binding baselines for international law and trade, dismantling Side B's claim that it would be mere "moral theater."
