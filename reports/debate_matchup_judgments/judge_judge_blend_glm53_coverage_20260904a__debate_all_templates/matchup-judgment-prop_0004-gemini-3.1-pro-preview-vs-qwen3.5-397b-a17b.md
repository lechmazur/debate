### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): PRO won on three decisive moves that CON never fully neutralized. First, PRO's staggered-shifts clarification [A2.1] correctly established that the mandate resets the individual-worker threshold, not business operating hours—CON's "1.25 employees" math is true for fixed-coverage roles but PRO countered that current absenteeism, turnover overtime, and sick leave already impose equivalent or greater hidden costs, a point CON never directly refuted, only calling it "variable." Second, PRO's two-tier trap was the sharpest exchange in the debate: CON introduced the two-tier risk, PRO turned it back by arguing voluntary adoption permanently excludes low-wage service workers, and CON's answer—"a two-tier of employed workers beats a shrunken workforce"—was a real answer but conceded the permanent exclusion rather than defeating it. Third, PRO's coordination-problem framing for legal mandate was the most original structural argument, and CON's counter ("voluntary experimentation allows fit") was never paired with a credible mechanism for extending the benefit to service laborers. CON's strongest live arguments were the physical-presence constraint for care work (the nurse cannot chart faster) and the fixed vs. variable cost asymmetry for SMEs—both are genuine and PRO's rebuttals were somewhat handwavy on the headcount math. CON's historical distinction (manufacturing boom then vs. stagnant service sector now) also went largely unanswered. However, PRO's cumulative rhetorical and logical arc was more adaptive across rounds: each rebuttal narrowed the clash, the A1:/A2: structure was followed cleanly, and the hidden-cost reframe in closing crystallized the existing clash rather than introducing new material. CON's later speeches became repetitive ("CON wins on precaution," "voluntary adoption allows fit") without deepening the comparison. On net, PRO built a stronger and less-refuted path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's two-tier flip [A2.4–A2.5]: CON introduced the two-tier economy risk to oppose the mandate, but PRO turned it back by demonstrating that voluntary adoption is precisely what creates a permanent two-tier system, with elite professionals getting Fridays off while service and care workers are structurally excluded. CON's response—"a two-tier of employed workers beats a shrunken workforce"—was a legitimate answer but conceded the exclusion problem rather than solving it, handing PRO the moral core of the debate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): Side A won because its rebuttals more effectively neutralized the fixed-cost arithmetic and sectoral constraints by answering the pressure questions directly (disputing static math via alertness/turnover and defending price adjustments), consistently reframed voluntary adoption as guaranteeing inequality, and crystallized the dynamic human costs vs. historical adaptation in the closing. Side B's later speeches repeated cost-shock claims without sufficient adaptation and left the two-tier voluntary critique largely unaddressed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 sequence directly answering B's questions on headcount and savings, shifting the clash to predictable turnover offsets and rejecting static presence assumptions.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): CON won by showing PRO assumes universal productivity elasticity that does not exist in fixed-presence service sectors. CON effectively turned PRO's equity argument into an exclusion argument, demonstrating that a legal mandate acts as a regressive cost shock risking unemployment, automation, and business closures for vulnerable workers and SMEs. PRO's historical analogy and coordination problem were coherent but insufficiently responsive to CON's sectoral heterogeneity and survival-margin arithmetic, leaving CON's concrete harms unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's pressure question on economic growth thresholds — "aggregate GDP masks sectoral reality... The limit is not money; it is time itself" — combined with the pivot that mandates guarantee exclusion rather than equity, decisively reframed the debate around concrete operational limits rather than abstract efficiency.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): CON won by landing a durable structural contradiction that PRO never fully escaped: PRO simultaneously claimed productivity gains would offset costs and that presence-based sectors would simply hire more staff. CON [B2.1] correctly identified these as competing concessions—if you must hire more bodies, you are explicitly accepting a major new cost, which cannot be waved away by appealing to "record corporate profits" concentrated in multinationals that have no direct financial relationship with neighborhood daycares, municipal schools, or non-profit hospitals. PRO's response—"governments can adjust tax structures" [A4.1]—was the weakest answer in the debate, effectively admitting the public bears the cost, which CON correctly capitalized on in closing. CON's pressure questions were sharper than PRO's, and PRO's answers were inadequate: Q1 got a plausible but speculative burnout-retention answer, Q2 got the vague tax-reallocation dodge. PRO's strongest live arguments—the coordination-failure rationale [A1.3], the burnout-shortage loop [A4.3–A4.4], and the historical transition analogy [A4.5]—were genuine and partially unanswered, which is why the margin is competitive rather than dominant. But PRO's own closing conceded rising unit labor costs and reframed them as deliberate redistribution, which CON correctly treated as confirmation that the "who actually pays" question had been settled against PRO. The childcare paradox also went unresolved: PRO argued parents working less reduces paid-childcare demand, but never answered CON's point that childcare workers' own wages would also be mandated upward, driving prices beyond the demand reduction effect. CON's "laptop class" framing was rhetorically effective and grounded in the genuine selection-bias critique of the trial evidence. PRO's case was well-structured and the equity reframe was plausible, but the financial mechanism arguments collapsed under sustained pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal [B2.1] catching the internal contradiction in PRO's dual-track response: PRO claimed knowledge-work productivity gains handle the cost while simultaneously conceding that presence-based sectors must hire more staff. This forced PRO to split its case across two incompatible mechanisms and accept that presence-based sectors face a genuine new cost—which then made PRO's "corporate profits will fund it" argument untenable, since those profits are not available to local essential-service providers. That catch set the terms for the rest of the debate and was never cleanly resolved.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): B built the stronger path by exposing the operational impossibility of instantly scaling licensed essential workers and by showing that A's funding solutions (taxes, prices, shareholder returns) directly contradict PRO's equity claims; A never neutralized the structural shortage or small-business mismatch. Later rounds crystallized this without evasion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1 directly neutralized A's contradictory productivity-plus-hiring defense, then B4 reinforced it by forcing A to concede rising unit costs funded by taxes or inflation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B won by exposing an unresolved contradiction in Side A's case: PRO simultaneously claimed productivity gains would offset reduced hours while admitting presence-based sectors would need to hire significantly more staff to maintain coverage. B successfully established that a blanket legal mandate would impose severe cost shocks on low-margin essential services and municipalities, and that PRO's funding mechanisms (corporate profits, tax restructuring) failed to bridge the gap to local businesses and frontline sectors. A's closing concession that unit labor costs may rise confirmed B's central economic critique. While A offered strong framing on coordination failure and equity, B's pressure questions and rebuttals more effectively neutralized PRO's live routes to victory by showing the mandate would harm the very working-class families it claimed to help. Side B incurred a modest execution cost by burying answers to A's pressure questions in prose without the required A1:/A2: labels, but this did not outweigh B's substantive advantage on the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1, which identified that PRO could not simultaneously claim productivity gains from compressed hours while admitting presence-based sectors must hire 20% more staff, creating a fatal tension in PRO's cost model that A never fully resolved.
