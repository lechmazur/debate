### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built a single, tightly disciplined meta-argument—that rehabilitation cannot self-justify punishment and every limiting principle PRO invokes (proportionality, due process, "structured sentences," guilt-as-threshold) is secretly borrowed from desert—and drove it through every round with escalating precision. PRO's opening and Rebuttal 1 leaned on proportionality/due process to block the "indefinite therapeutic detention" objection, but CON's Rebuttal 1 exposed that "proportionate to what?" answers itself with desert, and PRO never recovered from this. In Pressure Questions, PRO's answers (A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2) conceded that sentence structure is fixed independent of treatment progress and that guilt (not rehabilitative need) triggers punishment—both of which CON correctly flagged as desert operating under another name. PRO's closing attempt to salvage this with "a boundary is not an aim" was a bare assertion rather than a resolved rebuttal, leaving CON's "keel vs. cargo" framing standing largely unanswered. PRO's consequentialist case (recidivism, future victims, forward-looking harm prevention) remained emotionally resonant and reasonably grounded, but it never squarely resolved the structural challenge that an aim which cannot itself justify who may be punished or when punishment must stop cannot be the more important one. CON's opening being clipped is a real execution cost, but the core argument survived intact and was reinforced in later speeches, so the damage was limited.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 response to PRO's claim that "proportionality and due process are constitutional safeguards, not proprietary inventions of retribution"—showing that proportionality only makes sense relative to desert, and that mandatory minimums are condemned as disproportionate (i.e., violations of desert, not retribution's failure)—was the decisive exchange, and PRO never fully neutralized it in later rounds.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): A won by making the decisive clash clearer: what authorizes and bounds punishment is more important than what improves outcomes inside it. A repeatedly showed B borrowing desert for limits (proportionality, not punishing the innocent, structured fixed sentences) while still claiming rehab ranks first, and leveraged B's own answers as concessions. B's future-harm case was coherent but never displaced A's justification-and-limits burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2/closing use of B's answers: structured sentences independent of cure plus "guilt explains who" as quiet desert concessions that make retribution the keel.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A built a philosophically rigorous case that retribution provides the necessary justification and limits for punishment, effectively forcing Side B to smuggle retributive concepts like proportionality and guilt into their rehabilitative framework to avoid dystopian outcomes. Side A's rebuttals were exceptionally sharp, dismantling Side B's safeguards and capitalizing on Side B's concessions during the pressure questions. Side B relied on generic critiques of retribution as mere vengeance and struggled to defend pure rehabilitation against the cured offender and innocent detainee dilemmas. Side A's keel versus cargo framing perfectly crystallized the clash, securing a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it systematically dismantled Side B's answers to the pressure questions, exposing that Side B's structured sentences and rule of law safeguards inherently relied on the very retributive concepts of desert and proportionality that Side B claimed to reject.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's "ceiling vs. aim" distinction (desert sets the limit of punishment; rehabilitation governs its purpose and content) proved the more durable and adaptable framework across rounds. It let B absorb A's strongest weapon — the "museum piece" examples of non-dangerous or incorrigible offenders — by conceding the ceiling point without surrendering the substantive claim that retribution accomplishes nothing beyond what conviction already delivers. A's closing never squarely dismantled this framing; it kept reasserting that desert is "necessary," which B had already granted was compatible with rehabilitation being the operative purpose. B also answered the Pressure Questions with sharper, more direct A1/A2 responses (the "speed limit is not the purpose of driving" line neutralized A's release-at-ceiling gambit), while A's parallel answers were serviceable but more circular, restating that "the proportionate deprivation is itself accountability" without explaining why that accounts for cases with zero future risk. B's rhetoric (the "key opens a door" and "bookkeeping" lines) also gave the debate a clearer throughline than A's more repetitive closing. B's clipped opening is a real execution flaw and cost it some polish points, but the core content still landed and was reinforced in later rounds, so it did not flip the outcome.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's ceiling/aim distinction, first raised in Rebuttal 1 (B2.3) and sharpened in Rebuttal 2 with the "speed limit is not the purpose of driving" analogy (B4.3), directly defused A's central pressure question about retributive ceilings forcing release, and A never found an adequate counter to it in the remaining speeches.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): A won by keeping the motion on "more important aim" tied to what actually governs entry, quantum, and exit. B's next-victim framing and key/door rhetoric were memorable, but whenever the cases collided B still needed desert to authorize punishing the non-dangerous and to force release of the unreformed dangerous; A used those concessions cleanly to show desert is the decisive authority while rehabilitation remains a contingent preference inside it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1/A4.3: treating B's desert-as-ceiling concession as proof retribution is primary because it binds who is punished and when release must occur even against rehab/risk goals.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully drawing a sharp philosophical distinction between the warrant/limits of punishment (retribution) and its active aim (rehabilitation). Side A's core strategy was to argue that because retribution sets the boundaries (who gets punished, the ceiling, and when they are released), it must be the "primary aim." Side B effectively neutralizes this by using clear analogies—a speed limit is not the purpose of driving, and a key opening a door does not decide the destination. Side B's focus on "what fills the years inside" provides a much cleaner, more persuasive path to victory on the specific wording of the motion, whereas Side A struggles to overcome the conflation of a boundary condition with an active aim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where the "speed limit is not the purpose of driving" analogy perfectly dismantles Side A's claim that setting a retributive ceiling inherently makes retribution the primary aim of the punishment.
