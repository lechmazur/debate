- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by establishing a plausible, demonstrated path from higher prices to lower sugary-drink purchases, then connecting sustained exposure reduction to lower disease risk. Side B powerfully emphasized immediate regressivity and fairer funding alternatives, but repeatedly treated the absence of direct long-term disease-outcome proof as though it erased the ordinary causal link between reduced sugar intake and health. Its alternatives also remained less concrete as mechanisms for meaningfully shifting consumption. A incurred a clipping penalty and overstated some fiscal claims, but ultimately offered the stronger comparison between a modest present burden and concentrated downstream health and financial harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s most decisive response was that Side B imposed selective certainty: it demanded direct proof of reduced diabetes rates while giving little weight to demonstrated purchase reductions and the accepted dose-response pathway from sustained sugary-drink consumption to disease. Side B exposed weaknesses in A’s revenue-backfill story, but did not neutralize the tax’s core consumption mechanism.

B landed strong pressure on immediacy versus speculation and the "poor pay for their own rescue" frame, but never neutralized the dose-response comparison or the medical-debt outweighing, so A's route stayed live after full exchange.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won a competitive debate by showing that lower-income consumers are both especially exposed to SSB-related harms and demonstrably price-responsive, making the policy potentially progressive in net welfare despite its regressive payment incidence. Side B raised legitimate concerns about substitution, scaling, and weak outcome evidence, but relied too heavily on an unsupported assumption of unusually inelastic poor consumers and an excessive certainty standard. A's repeated clipping and questionable extrapolation from a moderate tax narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest response was that the Mexico example directly undercut B's foundational claim that lower-income demand is least responsive. B effectively challenged linear extrapolation to higher rates, but never established why meaningful purchase reductions would yield no material health benefit.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): B won by forcing the motion's "high enough to meaningfully shift" threshold against A's modest Mexico purchase data, exposing the unproven links (elasticity scaling, net sugar reduction after substitution, health outcomes over proxies) while A repeatedly over-relied on status-quo health regressivity without closing the certainty gap on benefits for a deliberately regressive design. Clipping hurt A's later completeness but was secondary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5–B4.6 (and echoed closing): PRO must prove three links simultaneously for a high tax; one modest purchase proxy fails that chain under the motion's own conceded regressivity.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won by exposing a persistent extrapolation gap: PRO's only concrete evidence was Mexico's modest tax producing modest single-digit purchase drops, but the resolution demands a rate "high enough to meaningfully shift consumption." CON showed that scaling from modest to "meaningful" requires assuming demand stays elastic and substitution effects don't swamp health gains—assumptions PRO never proved. CON's "three unproven links" framing and the sharp turn that juice has similar liquid-fructose loads undermined PRO's causal chain. PRO's status quo health-regressivity argument remained live but never overcame the burden-of-proof problem CON established: when a policy is deliberately regressive, inferred distant benefits cannot justify certain immediate costs without outcome data or a reliable scaling mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal crystallized the decisive vulnerability by showing that PRO needed three unproven links to hold simultaneously—demand responsiveness scales linearly, purchase drops translate to net sugar reduction rather than substitution, and resulting health gains outweigh guaranteed income losses—and that Mexico's modest evidence could not support that chain at the "meaningful" scale the resolution requires.
