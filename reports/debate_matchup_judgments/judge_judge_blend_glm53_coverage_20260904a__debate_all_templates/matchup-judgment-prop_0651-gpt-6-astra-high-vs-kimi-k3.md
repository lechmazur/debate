### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built a durable framing early ("judge against the real alternative") and then exploited a genuine tension in PRO's case: PRO's own proposed remedy kept English as the dominant international channel while merely fixing institutions, which CON repeatedly leveraged to argue the harms are policy-contingent while the goods (network effects, translation infrastructure) are structural. CON's pressure question forced PRO to admit it "cannot substantiate an entire knowledge system destroyed specifically by English," a costly concession that undercut PRO's generational-erosion narrative and that CON hammered in both subsequent rebuttal and closing. CON also introduced a clean, unrebutted network-effects mechanism (hub-and-spoke translation economics, prestige following readership) that PRO's closing never squarely answered—PRO reasserted a "shared channel vs. required passport" distinction without explaining why prestige wouldn't naturally track readership under CON's mechanism. PRO's case remained conceptually coherent (circulation vs. capacity to create knowledge) but lost ground once its own remedy was shown to preserve the very dominance it condemned, and its softened retreat from "language death" to merely "harms before extinction" registered as a real concession rather than a clarification.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 move (turning PRO's "English as working language + strong local institutions" into an admission that PRO's own ideal keeps English dominant) combined with the pressure-question follow-up that extracted PRO's concession it could not name a knowledge system destroyed by English—this pairing structurally reoriented the whole debate in CON's favor and was never fully neutralized by PRO.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by successfully framing the benefits of a shared language (the 'network') as inextricably linked to its dominance and prestige, dismantling Side A's attempt to separate the two. B's explanation of network effects and translation pivot structures proved that the structural benefits of dominance outweigh the policy-level harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized A's attempt to separate 'shared channel' from 'dominance' by pointing out that 'prestige is the network's gravity' and using the Esperanto example to show a fair bridge without dominance attracts no investment.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A executed a highly effective strategic trap, demonstrating that Side B's proposed "remedy" still fundamentally relied on English dominance, thereby conceding the structural benefits of the motion while reducing the harms to fixable institutional policy issues. Side A also successfully dismantled Side B's core distinction between a "shared channel" and "dominance" by explaining network effects: the channel's utility, translation quality, and dense library inherently rely on the concentration of investment and prestige. Side B's concession that they could not name a specifically destroyed knowledge system further crippled their offense, leaving Side A with a clear, decisive path to victory based on structural goods versus fixable harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they collapsed Side B's semantic distinction between a shared language and dominance by explaining that the network's utility inherently relies on the concentration of investment and prestige (the "gravity" of the network), proving that stripping the "outranking" would destroy the very bridge Side B wanted to keep.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): B built a coherent three-part harm case (epistemic narrowing, participation tax, domain erosion) anchored in a vivid example, then sustained it by turning A's own counterexamples (Japan, Germany, and later Finland) into evidence that only already-powerful, state-protected languages survive under English dominance—leaving A's central defense ("other languages can coexist with English science") unable to answer for Quechua, Yoruba, or Khmer. B's "portable benefits vs. permanent losses" weighing frame was introduced early and consistently pressed through rebuttals and closing, and A never squarely dislodged it, instead relying on "reforms are possible" claims that B correctly noted have no working instance. A's rebuttals were competent and well-organized (distinguishing publication language from domestic scientific life, noting translation also requires human judgment), but they were largely defensive, chipping at B's certainty rather than establishing an affirmative net-benefit case strong enough to outweigh the conceded "unequal burden" and "unfair head start." The pressure-question exchange was pivotal: B's demand for a small/indigenous-language counterexample was answered only with Finland, which B then folded back into its own pattern, and A's closing did not recover from that specific challenge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's exploitation of A's Japan/Germany/Finland examples—showing that even the most privileged non-English languages have already ceded scientific publication to English—was the decisive turn, since A never produced a counterexample of a smaller or non-state-protected language sustaining a living scientific register under English dominance.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully proving that the harms of English dominance to smaller languages are systemic and irreversible, while the benefits of coordination are merely substitutable utilities. A's relentless pressure on B's examples exposed that B's model only protects languages with massive state support, and A's analysis of the prestige gradient dismantled B's proposed funding reforms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4's argument that "reward flows are the dominance itself" effectively neutralized B's claim that funders could simply choose to reward local-language dissemination, proving the hierarchy is systemic.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B built a much stronger comparative case by distinguishing between portable benefits (information flows that can be achieved via translation) and permanent harms (domain erosion and language death). Side B also effectively dismantled Side A's examples (Japan, Germany, Finland) by pointing out they are wealthy, state-protected languages, leaving Side A without a credible defense for the most vulnerable non-anglophone cultures like Quechua or Yoruba. Side B's rebuttals were sharper, and their weighing in the closing crystallized the clash decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they distinguished between technical translation costs (which scale and fall) and the structural hierarchy of prestige (which resists reform), effectively neutralizing Side A's reliance on hypothetical funder mandates and exposing the limits of Side A's state-protected language examples.
