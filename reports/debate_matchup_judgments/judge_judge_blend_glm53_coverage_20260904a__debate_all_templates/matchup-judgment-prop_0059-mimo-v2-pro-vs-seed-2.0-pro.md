- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's core mechanism of accountability. Side A also provided a very clean, principled answer to Side B's pressure on where the line for professional exceptions is drawn.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal decisively undercut Side B's main argument by pointing out that AR glasses are indistinguishable from normal frames, completely neutralizing the claim that private wearers could be easily confronted or held accountable.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON)): Side A prevailed by anchoring the debate on the decisive invisibility of private AR scans (no confrontation or recourse possible), sustaining that advantage through rebuttals and direct answers to pressure questions on oversight and line-drawing. Side B’s democratization and visibility claims were never squared with the core anonymity problem and instead relied on comparisons that left institutional monopoly as the alternative. Side A’s exception framework stayed coherent and narrowly applied without late additions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) that directly neutralized B’s oversight and exception questions while reinforcing the invisible-scan harm.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2 Pro (CON)): Side A won because its central mechanism—that consumer AR glasses are visually indistinguishable from ordinary eyewear, making scanning invisible and unprovable—survived the full exchange and decisively undercut Side B's core claim that distributed private use is more accountable and challengeable than institutional use. Side B never overcame the fact that a subject cannot confront, record, or sue a scanner they cannot identify, and Side A showed that narrow professional exceptions already capture Side B's legitimate safety use cases (e.g., volunteer search-and-rescue, supervised teachers), leaving Side B defending low-stakes convenience against the fundamental right to public anonymity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing leveraged the physical indistinguishability of AR glasses from prescription frames to show that private scanning is invisible, rendering Side B's theory of confrontable, litigable distributed accountability hollow—a rebuttal Side B never recovered from despite its clever closing inversion.

CON’s case hinged on the claim that consumer AR use would be visible, confrontable, and thus more accountable than opaque institutional databases. PRO consistently exposed the fatal flaw in this premise: AR glasses are physically indistinguishable from regular eyewear, meaning victims would never know they were scanned, making confrontation or legal recourse impossible. CON repeatedly asserted private transparency without answering this practical reality, relying instead on rhetorical reframing about democratization and existing laws. CON’s institutional opacity arguments had merit but could not overcome the unresolved detection gap in their own model.
