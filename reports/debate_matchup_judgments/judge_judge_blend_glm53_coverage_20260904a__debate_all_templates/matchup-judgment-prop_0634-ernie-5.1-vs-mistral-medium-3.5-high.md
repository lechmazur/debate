### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built a more durable path to victory across the full exchange. Its core framework—that germline editing is categorically different from natural reproduction not because of quantity of risk but because the intervention is deliberate, heritable, and impossible to recall—was consistently maintained and never fully neutralized. CON's most powerful running argument was the ~60 natural mutations comparison, but PRO's qualitative asymmetry response (random+distributed+filtered by selection vs. deliberate+permanent+propagating) was coherent enough to prevent CON from claiming an outright win there. The most decisive single exchange was PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Huntington's "all embryos affected" scenario: calling it vanishingly rare and pointing to donor gametes and adoption as real alternatives. CON had staked its most emotionally resonant concrete case on this extreme scenario, and PRO deflated it without being dismissed—CON's Rebuttal 2 never meaningfully re-engaged this reduction. PRO also maintained a live consent distinction (selecting existing variation vs. authoring new heritable variation) that CON never directly answered, resorting instead to the overbroad claim that consistency demands we ban reproduction itself. CON's genetic tourism turn was clever and created genuine tension, but PRO's response—that a domestic ban removes legal demand and delegitimizes the practice internationally even if a few black-market outliers persist—was adequate. CON's speeches were notably shorter in several rounds (opening at 254, Rebuttal 2 at 223 vs. PRO's fuller speeches), leaving some burdens incompletely met. Both sides followed the A1/A2 format requirement. The debate was substantive but not especially memorable—both sides recycled their core comparisons rather than substantially sharpening the clash in later rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Huntington's worst-case scenario: PRO correctly identified the "all embryos mutant" situation as vanishingly rare in practice and offered donor gametes and adoption as genuine alternatives. This was the debate's most decisive single exchange because CON had repeatedly used this scenario as its sharpest concrete example of families condemned to perpetual genetic risk, and PRO's response shrunk its scope without dismissing it entirely—while CON's subsequent speeches never re-engaged this reduction.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the central distinction clear: somatic and PGD alternatives can address much disease risk without adding an irreversible, heritable, poorly understood intervention. B’s natural-mutation double-standard argument was strong rhetorically, but it leaned on overconfident claims that editing can be made comparable or safer than natural reproduction and did not fully answer A’s point about deliberate, recall-proof, lineage-wide risk. A also narrowed B’s Huntington benefit by pointing to PGD and alternatives, making the claimed upside less decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s most decisive rebuttal was that the Chinese CRISPR case and “no harm to date” do not prove safety; they illustrate precisely why long-term heritable effects remain unknowable and why regulation cannot solve recall-proof germline mistakes.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): PRO (B) wins because it maintained the core asymmetry of irreversible deliberate change versus natural risk, used the pressure questions round to force CON into edge-case defenses, and consistently showed PGD already solves most Huntington’s cases while highlighting consent and recall problems that CON never neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2 format) directly dismantled CON's tourism and PGD claims, restoring the precautionary and consent advantages that carried through the close.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won a competitive but clear debate by maintaining a stronger live route to victory across all rounds. The decisive clash turned on whether safer alternatives genuinely cover every case where germline editing would be used. CON identified the homozygous-carriers scenario early—where PGD can only discard all embryos—and sustained it under pressure. PRO's pivot to donor gametes as the answer came late (introduced in Q1, developed only in Rebuttal 2), and CON's counter—that donor conception severs genetic continuity valued by many families for cultural, religious, or identity reasons, and that rare haplotype availability is genuinely limited—remained unanswered in PRO's closing, which merely asserted "genetic continuity is not required to prevent hereditary illness" without engaging the subjective value at stake. CON's pressure questions were sharper and more incisive than PRO's, directly cornering the gaps in the alternative-solutions argument. CON also turned the precautionary principle effectively by applying it to inaction, pointing to the concrete suffering a ban would impose; PRO never squarely answered this inversion. PRO's strongest sustained point—the categorical distinction between selecting existing DNA (PGD) and manufacturing new heritable code (editing)—was never fully neutralized by CON's "both impose irreversible choices" counter, which side-stepped the distinct nature of unknown-sequence risk. But this advantage was insufficient to overcome PRO's failure to close the donor-gametes gap and failure to answer the harms-of-inaction argument, leaving CON with cleaner live routes to victory at the end of the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first pressure question (A3.1) was the most decisive exchange: it directly cornered the homozygous-carriers gap that PGD cannot solve, forcing PRO to introduce donor gametes as a late answer. CON's Rebuttal 2 then effectively countered that answer with the genetic-continuity and donor-pool-availability arguments, which PRO's closing never genuinely engaged, leaving the gap unresolved in PRO's favor.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): B narrowly won by making the blanket ban look overbroad. A had the cleaner precautionary case and better epistemic discipline, especially on irreversible heritable risk, but B more effectively exposed that PGD and somatic therapy do not cover all cases and that donor gametes are not an equivalent substitute for families seeking genetically related children. B's safety claims used some overprecise unsupported figures, so they receive limited credit, but the comparative point that regulation may be preferable to prohibition remained live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was pressing the homozygous-parent case: A answered with donor gametes, but B persuasively replied that this sacrifices genetic continuity and therefore undercuts A's claim that germline editing offers no unique benefit.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A built the stronger case overall by directly neutralizing PRO's key sufficiency claim (PGD + donors) through concrete counterexamples on homozygous cases, genetic continuity, and identity concerns; Side A's answers to pressure questions sharpened the live clash and exposed gaps that PRO's rebuttals did not fully close, while maintaining consistent weighing on regulation versus ban.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answers to B's pressure questions on donor alternatives and the selective-precaution distinction, which materially damaged PRO's core route by showing insufficient coverage for non-trivial families.
