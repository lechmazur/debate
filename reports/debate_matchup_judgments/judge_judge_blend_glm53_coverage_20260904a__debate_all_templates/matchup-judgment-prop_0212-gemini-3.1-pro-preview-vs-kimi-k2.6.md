### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): B won because it better defended the motion as written: broader access after immediate security risks, not reckless public dumping. A landed serious concerns about false accusations, privacy, and source protection, but too often treated any broader access as publication into the public square and drifted toward perpetual secrecy. B kept a live comparative case that archives are needed for families, restitution, accountability, and historical correction, and showed that tiered access and redaction are a more proportionate answer than state monopoly over the record.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s pressure on A’s no-cutoff position was decisive: A answered that human-source secrecy must be perpetual, which made its model look like permanent state veto over large parts of colonial and secret-police history rather than a targeted safety exception.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B built the stronger case by highlighting practical harms of stigma, administrative impossibility of safe broad release, and the need for perpetual source protection that PRO never fully neutralized; the clipped rebuttal and retreat from "broad" to tiered access further weakened A's position.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's direct answer to Pressure Questions on perpetual secrecy and forced publication of lies, followed by calling out A's narrowing of "broadly" in Rebuttal 2.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO wins by cleanly resolving the two decisive clashes. First, PRO turns CON's "toxic archives" premise into a case for access, arguing that cross-referencing and public scrutiny neutralize regime fabrications, whereas secrecy only lets rumors fester and prevents the falsely accused from clearing their names. Second, PRO dominates the security clash by holding CON to the motion's explicit boundary ("once immediate security concerns pass"). CON's attempt to link historical archive releases to modern informant deterrence overreaches, and when pressed, CON explicitly concedes to supporting perpetual secrecy for human sources. This admission collapses CON's stance into a defense of permanent classification, which directly contradicts the proposition. While CON effectively pressures PRO on the logistical tension between "broad access" and careful redaction, PRO's clarification of tiered release and ending default secrecy is operationally plausible and defensible. PRO's closing crystallizes the tradeoff more sharply, framing continued concealment as state self-protection rather than genuine harm prevention.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.3) directly answers CON's pressure on instant stigma and logistical feasibility. PRO explains how broad access builds the very cross-referencing network needed to debunk coercive lies, and clarifies that "broad" means ending default secrecy through sequenced, tiered release rather than an unfiltered data dump. This turn successfully neutralizes CON's central harm claim, resolves the definitional tension CON exploited, and pivots back to the moral burden shift that anchors PRO's case.
