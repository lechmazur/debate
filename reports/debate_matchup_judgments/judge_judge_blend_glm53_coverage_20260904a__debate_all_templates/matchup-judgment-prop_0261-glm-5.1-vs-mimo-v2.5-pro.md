### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): PRO built a stronger cumulative case through two decisive advantages CON never fully neutralized. First, PRO's account-sharing inconsistency in Rebuttal 1 — if non-transferability is the structural foundation of digital licensing, why do platforms routinely permit it during life? — was never squarely answered. CON said the living account holder "faces consequences," but account sharing typically violates EULAs too and platforms tolerate it; the death clause thus looks like opportunistic confiscation rather than principled licensing policy. Second, PRO's "who benefits from the death-termination rule?" framing in Rebuttal 2 and Closing was clean and unrebutted: the only proven beneficiary is the platform reselling content already paid for, since ebook prices track physical-book prices while stripping resale, lending, and inheritance rights. CON's strongest counterpoint — that inheritance law should transfer what was actually held (a non-transferable license), not manufacture a new right the transaction never included — is structurally coherent and was its best late-game move. CON also landed a real point distinguishing digital licenses from leases by noting that lease inheritance binds heirs to ongoing enforceable obligations whereas digital licenses impose none, making the analogy asymmetric. But PRO's A1 response (estates are counterparties, heirs can decline, executors administer mixed-rights assets routinely) was adequate, and PRO's framing that the "correct remedy at point of sale" argument conveniently ignores that the harm is irreversible once the buyer dies provided a live counter. On balance, PRO's rhetorical sharpness, the unanswered account-sharing point, and the memorable "only platforms benefit" crystallization outweigh CON's structural argument, which was sound but never converted into a winning frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 observation that account-sharing is routinely tolerated during life — if non-transferability is truly the structural foundation of the licensing model, why is it enforced only at death? — was the single most damaging exchange. CON's response that living account holders "remain bound by terms and face consequences" didn't engage with the fact that account sharing also violates terms and is widely permitted. The point exposed the death clause as platform opportunism rather than principled licensing policy, and it was never recovered.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by making the central distinction sharper: inheritance normally transfers the bundle of rights the deceased actually held, while A's proposal would create transferability where the original digital license excluded it. A was strong on consumer expectations, corporate double dipping, and probate counterparties, and partially neutralized B's no-counterparty point. But B more effectively argued that the remedy for misleading or overpriced digital sales is point-of-sale regulation, not posthumous expansion of licenses, and kept the economic tradeoff live despite A's attacks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive exchange was reframing A's no-marginal-cost answer as missing the point: the issue is not server strain but whether a one-lifetime license should become a transferable ownership-like asset. That undercut A's physical-library analogy.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): CON successfully showed that guaranteeing inheritance of non-transferable digital assets would retroactively create a transferability right the deceased never purchased, distinguishing digital licenses from arrangements with ongoing obligations. PRO's consumer-expectation and pricing arguments remained live but could not overcome CON's sustained framing that inheritance law should transfer the bundle of rights actually held, not manufacture new ones at the expense of the licensing model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer distinguishing inherited leases and insurance (which bind heirs to ongoing obligations enforceable by a counterparty) from digital licenses (which impose no obligations and thus constitute a windfall mandate), directly neutralizing PRO's estate-counterparty analogy.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): PRO won by building a coherent moral core—no other category of purchased asset simply extinguishes at death—that CON never squarely neutralized. CON repeatedly defended the contractual status quo, but PRO correctly identified this as circular: the proposition is precisely asking whether the law should override those terms. The decisive exchange was PRO's introduction of Apple's Digital Legacy program in Rebuttal 2, which directly answered CON's two strongest technical objections simultaneously: it demonstrated that purchased-content libraries can already be separated from private account data, and that the mechanism already triggers at death rather than merely for living users. CON's attempt to flip this—"voluntary programs prove the market works without mandates"—was weaker, because it left unaddressed the core vulnerability PRO identified: voluntary programs can be revoked and are unavailable to users who don't opt in. CON's pricing-model argument was economically coherent, but PRO's responses (inheritance is singular; law limits inherited assets through taxes, terms, and restrictions constantly; family sharing already survives without collapsing pricing) were adequate, and CON's generational-cascade slippery slope was undermined by PRO's observation that inheritance law in every other domain proves lines can be drawn. CON's enforcement concern was real but PRO reasonably answered it by noting that estate administration already handles far more complex verification. The clipping of several PRO speeches was a mild execution cost, but the main arguments came through in each round. Both sides were competent, but PRO's arguments progressed more cleanly across the exchange, adapted better to live pressure, and left the cleaner reason to vote for the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q1 (the "actual model" challenge): citing Apple's Digital Legacy program as a concrete, functioning example of purchased-content inheritance that separates libraries from private data and activates at death. This directly neutralized CON's two main technical objections—privacy separation and death-triggered mechanism—with a real-world precedent, and reframed the remaining dispute as purely about voluntary versus legally guaranteed rights rather than technical feasibility.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate. B had a coherent economic and implementation case, but A more directly neutralized the key objections: inheritance need not become an unlimited resale market, statutes can draw transfer limits, and privacy concerns can be handled by separating purchased libraries from personal account data. B's pricing collapse claim remained too speculative and slippery-slope dependent, while A kept the core unfairness of paid digital libraries vanishing at death central. A's repeated clipping was an execution cost, but not enough to overcome the stronger comparative answers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's most decisive rebuttal was that a death-triggered transfer to next of kin is not the same as open resale or indefinite market circulation, and that inheritance law can limit further transfer rather than accepting B's all-or-nothing framing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side A won by sustaining a powerful moral framing—paid digital assets should not be the only purchased property to evaporate at death—and by landing a decisive factual answer to B's technical feasibility challenge through Apple's Digital Legacy program. A effectively neutralized B's pricing-collapse fear by distinguishing a one-time inheritance transfer from an open resale market and noting that inheritance law already limits transfers across every other asset class. B's case remained grounded in legitimate economic distinctions, but its slippery-slope generational-ownership claim and privacy objections lost force after A showed that separation mechanisms already operate at death. A's multiple clipped speeches were a real execution cost that prevent a larger margin, yet the visible material built a cleaner live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's use of Apple's Digital Legacy program to answer B's pressure-question demand for an actual death-triggered separation model. This rebuttal deflated B's claim that platforms have no mechanism to separate purchased libraries from private account data upon death, forcing B to retreat from technical impossibility to the weaker argument that voluntarism suffices.
