- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO carried the debate on the key conceptual clash. The core question was whether "similar to the Antarctic Treaty System" requires cloning its claims-freeze or adopting its governing logic (binding cooperation over rivalry). PRO defined the latter consistently from opening through closing; CON kept attacking the former, which was always a straw man. PRO's best sustained point—"title is not governance"—was never squarely answered: CON listed UNCLOS, the Polar Code, and two Arctic Council agreements repeatedly but never demonstrated how those tools address cumulative cross-border harms, military confidence-building, or comprehensive environmental prevention. CON's best counter—that stripping the claims-freeze leaves PRO's treaty indistinguishable from what already exists—was real but insufficient because CON itself never explained how the current patchwork handles the specific gap PRO identified about failures when rivalry intensifies. PRO's Q1 pressure trap was decisive: forcing CON to say it would still oppose a sovereignty-preserving treaty exposed that the "dead on arrival" objection was not about mechanism design but a preference for the current fragmented system, which PRO then crystallized effectively in closing. CON's "dead on arrival" framing is also a weaker normative argument in a "should" debate—it concedes desirability to defend feasibility. PRO's two clipped speeches are real execution costs that narrowed the margin; the arguments that survived the clips remained coherent and strong. CON was cleaner in format compliance and grounding, but the argumentative direction of the debate consistently favored PRO.

PRO had laid the conceptual groundwork that "similar" means governing logic, not clause-copying; CON's admission that it would oppose even a sovereignty-preserving version unmasked the real dispute as being about whether any comprehensive binding framework is desirable, and PRO exploited this cleanly in its closing to reframe the entire debate around that core comparative: enforceable common rules versus hoping a fragmented, sovereignty-first patchwork holds under pressure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): This was a genuinely competitive debate. PRO built a coherent case around the governance vacuum in the central Arctic basin, the Cold War Antarctic precedent, and the exclusion of Indigenous communities. CON's core moves—that the Arctic fundamentally differs from Antarctica, that enforcement follows power rather than aspiration, and that the status quo already channels rivalry through legal mechanisms—were consistently well-grounded. The decisive exchange came when CON's Q1 forced PRO to clarify that the treaty would cover only the central Arctic Ocean high seas and seabed, leaving EEZs and territorial seas to states. CON then drove home the "narrow versus broad" dilemma in B4.4–B4.6: a treaty limited to the high seas basin avoids the zones where shipping, extraction, military infrastructure, and most enforcement actually reside, meaning it misses the core drivers PRO originally named; but a treaty broad enough to freeze meaningful claims and restrain military behavior in those contested zones is one states with sovereign and homeland-defense stakes will not sign or obey. PRO's response—mutual catastrophic risk mirrors Cold War Antarctic cooperation—is a real argument, but it never resolved how that logic applies specifically to the central high seas while leaving the politically sensitive coastal zones untouched. PRO's scoping concession cost them their original framing without fully replacing it. CON's alternative (targeted regional agreements) remained underdeveloped and PRO's Q2 exposed a genuine tension there, but that weakness was not enough to overcome CON's stronger live route through the dilemma. The two clipped CON speeches (Rebuttal 1 and Closing) are real execution costs, but the decisive content—the dilemma and its framing—appeared in the unclipped portions of CON's Rebuttal 2. PRO's Indigenous-communities point was the strongest unanswered blow. On balance, CON's workability challenge survives the full exchange more intact than PRO's governance-vacuum case after the scope narrowing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.4–B4.6 in Rebuttal 2, where CON identified PRO's scope clarification (treaty limited to central high seas) as a significant retreat and then crystallized the dilemma: a narrow treaty misses the real levers of Arctic rivalry, while a broad treaty that actually matters to the competition dynamics will be rejected by states treating those zones as sovereign rights and homeland defense. PRO never fully escaped this constraint.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by establishing a fatal dilemma regarding the treaty's scope, forcing Side B to concede that the treaty would only cover the central Arctic high seas, which Side A then successfully argued leaves out the coastal zones where the vast majority of shipping, extraction, and military infrastructure are actually located.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was the decisive moment, as it capitalized on Side B's answer to the pressure questions to trap them in a dilemma: a treaty narrow enough to be signable misses the core drivers of rivalry in the EEZs, but a treaty broad enough to cover them will be rejected by states protecting their sovereignty.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B won by forcing and then exploiting Side A's narrowing of the treaty to central high seas only, exposing the dilemma that it would then miss the core drivers of rivalry while still facing sovereignty rejection if broadened.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2, which weaponized A's scope concession against the original crisis narrative.
