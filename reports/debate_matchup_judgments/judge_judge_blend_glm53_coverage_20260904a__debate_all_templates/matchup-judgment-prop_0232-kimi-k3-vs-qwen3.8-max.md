### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by exploiting the motion's modest requirement: legalization need only be a strategy that weakens organized crime, not eliminate it. Side B persuasively showed that diversification and weak institutions could limit the effect, but mostly established possible substitution rather than showing that replacement would fully offset lost cannabis profits. Side A gave the stronger marginal comparison: revenue finances coercive capacity, alternative crimes carry costs and risks, and prohibition guarantees criminals the cannabis market. Side A's clipped first rebuttal and overbroad claims about legal businesses reduced the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest response was that diversification is not costless adaptation: losing a relatively low-risk revenue line and moving toward more dangerous or heavily policed crimes still imposes organizational weakness. Side B repeatedly argued that replacement could preserve guns and territory, but did not establish that replacement would be complete enough to defeat the motion's limited burden.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO won a clear but competitive debate by locking the burden to the motion’s actual word—“weaken,” not abolish—and forcing CON’s own metrics (guns, bribes, territory, resilience) to run through conceded cannabis revenue. CON’s poly-crime, capture, and weak-state objections stayed live and serious, but too often raised the bar to full organizational collapse while under-defending prohibition’s guaranteed income stream; PRO’s status-quo comparison and design-vs-destiny framing therefore gave the cleaner reason to adopt the strategy. A’s clipped R1 was a real execution cost but did not erase the comparative edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2/closing turn of CON’s capability list: if guns, bribes, and territory are revenue-purchased, conceded cannabis-revenue loss is weakness under CON’s own terms, and “replacement” into hotter crimes is cornering rather than free adaptation.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO kept the motion's "weaken" standard central and linked revenue to every item on CON's own metric of strength. CON's live route - poly-crime replacement and weak-state capture - never squared how guns/bribes/territory persist without the budget that buys them, or why abandoning safe cannabis for riskier extortion/cocaine is resilience not cost. A's pressure answers on margins, heat, and concentrated regulation were direct; B's answers asserted portfolio adaptation and moved-elsewhere without explaining voluntary abandonment of a profitable crop. Despite A2 clipping, PRO controlled weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 A1-A2 plus closing: revenue = armed capacity, so "bookkeeping is disarmament in slow motion" and diversification = cornered into higher-risk, higher-enmity crimes, answering B's core bookkeeping vs disarmament charge.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B won by showing that removing a relatively minor cannabis revenue stream may preserve territorial control through extortion while weak institutions struggle to displace illicit supply or prevent regulatory capture. A established some weakening in principle, but never adequately answered the implementation evidence, the Paraguay comparison, or whether substitution leaves organized crime weaker overall.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B decisively exploited A's concession that replacement through extortion would be uglier and more harmful, arguing that this preserves the territorial control A had identified as the key measure of criminal power.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): CON won by consistently turning PRO's own framing against it: cannabis as street-level territorial power, then showing substitution into extortion preserves that power while making crime uglier for citizens. CON also owned the capacity and precedent clash (California illicit market, Paraguay tobacco fronts, no clean success case) and forced PRO into concessions that regulation trades a consensual market for predatory control with unproven net weakening. PRO offered a coherent mechanism and useful pressure on "least valuable" vs. passive absorption, but never squared the metric inconsistency or the affirmative duty of weak-state regulation, so CON made the decisive comparative easier to resolve against the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON Rebuttal 1 (B2.1-B2.3): accepting PRO's "daily presence/social control" metric then showing that extortion substitution is the same territorial power, only uglier—detonating PRO's "they'll just be poorer" reply.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): CON won the core weighing. PRO reframed minor revenue as territorial infrastructure, but CON showed substitution into extortion preserves that same daily presence and social control by PRO's own metric, and PRO conceded it is uglier. Paraguay tobacco as a regional capture precedent went unanswered from opening to close, and PRO offered no success case where regulation shrank criminal power while having to answer why weak states would outperform California and Uruguay.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2 and B4 - B exposed PRO's contradiction: cannabis matters because it funds street power, but extortion replacing it is dismissed as gangs being poorer, though extortion is street power. Combined with the unanswered Paraguay laundering-front example, it collapsed PRO's definition of weakening.
