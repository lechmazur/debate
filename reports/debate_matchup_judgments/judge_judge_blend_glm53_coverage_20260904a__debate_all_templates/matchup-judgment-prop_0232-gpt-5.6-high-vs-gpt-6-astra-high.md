### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built the more analytically disciplined case by consistently separating "lost retail revenue" from "reduced criminal power," pressing the point that territorial gatekeepers can tax legal cannabis through extortion just as easily as illegal cannabis, and identifying a concrete unresolved tension in PRO's plan: legal sellers need low taxes to undercut illicit competitors, yet the state also needs funding and investigative capacity to police ownership, proxies, and coercion. PRO (B) offered a coherent core mechanism (market displacement drains criminal revenue) and answered pressure with a plausible "extortion is a fraction of gross revenue" claim, but never squarely resolved the tax/enforcement-capacity tension A kept raising, instead repeating that released enforcement capacity is "not wholly additional" without explaining why it would suffice. A's closing crystallized this gap cleanly, while B's closing largely restated its opening frame. B's opening was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost per the judging rules. On balance, A's rebuttals did more damage to B's live route than B's rebuttals did to A's, giving CON a clear, competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2/A4's repeated distinction between "purchases migrating to legal shops" and "criminal organizations losing net power," especially the point that gross revenue and net criminal profit differ and that ownership scrutiny requires investigators the plan never funds, was the most damaging unanswered pressure in the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A won a competitive exchange by owning the decisive baseline: prohibition (and CON’s decriminalization alternative) structurally guarantee criminals sole supplier status, while national regulation at least contests that monopoly. A’s pressure Q2 forced B to concede decrim does not transfer customers/revenue; A then converted that into a clean comparative: ending ordinary supply crimes frees capacity that decrim never unlocks, and forcing gangs from full illicit margin to partial extortion/proxies is weakening even without perfect territorial liberation. B’s best live route—independence requires protection investigators that legalization itself burdens, so displacement is not automatically a net capability loss—was sharp and never fully dissolved, especially on proxy/extortion retention and the tax-vs-oversight trade-off. But B stayed mostly defensive and conditional, repeatedly raising the bar toward durable independence rather than showing why preserving the illegal market is the stronger organized-crime strategy as written. A’s later speeches narrowed rather than widened, crystallizing “contest the revenue that sustains coercion” more decisively than B’s “permission ≠ independence” caution. Opening clip was a mild A execution cost; substance still favored A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Q2 + A4 answers: decrim leaves the entire adult cannabis chain criminal, so CON’s alternative never does the market-displacement work the motion requires; legalization removes exclusivity and forces residual income into already-available extortion/proxy tools.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully problematizing the assumption that a legal market operates independently of criminal territorial control. Side A's distinction between the permission to trade legally and the state capacity to trade independently is a highly persuasive, contextually grounded critique for Latin America, where cartels routinely extort legal agriculture. Side A also effectively wielded the decriminalization alternative to show that the specific regulatory apparatus of legalization carries heavy enforcement costs that might outweigh the marginal displacement of criminal profits. Side B offered strong economic logic and solid responses, but Side A's sharper focus on the opportunity costs of regulation and the nature of coercive power made for the more decisive comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal effectively dismantled Side B's claim that legalization merely forces criminals to rely on extortion, pointing out that breaking exclusivity requires the very investigative and protective state capacity that is scarce, and that gross revenue loss does not equal net criminal profit loss when proxies and coercion remain viable.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly without fake sourcing, but CON (A) built the sharper decision rule—net coercive capacity of dangerous organizations rather than gross retail sales displaced—and sustained it across every round. Its strongest moment was turning PRO's "both approaches require functioning institutions" concession into a point favoring prohibition-plus-decriminalization in weak-state contexts, a rebuttal PRO never fully neutralized. PRO's case (mechanism: lawful competition removes criminal revenue and enables focused enforcement) was well-constructed and consistently defended, especially the diversification-limits-but-doesn't-reverse-benefit line, but it kept resting on "any lost income helps" without squarely meeting CON's reframed burden that partial market substitution must reach the powerful, violent organizations the motion targets, not just independent growers. PRO's closing acknowledged CON's concession on upstream competition but only argued it "limits" rather than "erases" the benefit, which was less decisive than CON's final framing distinguishing gross revenue shift from actual coercive weakening. CON's opening was clipped for length, a real execution cost, but the substantive content lost was minor and CON's later speeches more than compensated with sharper comparative framing and rebuttal precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4, where CON turns PRO's "both approaches require functioning institutions" point back against PRO by arguing that in weak/captured institutional settings, adding a second regulated market multiplies corruption opportunities without necessarily reducing cartel power—an objection PRO's subsequent closing did not fully answer.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won a competitive debate by keeping the motion’s causal burden live: showing that national legalization weakens organized crime, not merely that some sales move to licensed shops. Across rebuttals and closing, B repeatedly forced the missing link—who is displaced, whether major groups lose coercive capacity amid exports/other rackets, and whether licensing consumes scarce capacity in captured settings. PRO offered a coherent supply-chain competition story and answered pressure questions cleanly, but never fully closed that net-weakening gap against CON’s diversification, black-market, and capacity critiques; CON’s direct-enforcement-plus-decriminalization alternative stayed comparatively more reliable on the stated anti-crime standard.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s sustained “displacement may hit small sellers, not cartel power” pressure, answered then re-weighed through B4/B5 against PRO’s upstream-competition concession.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully severing the causal link between cannabis legalization and the weakening of organized crime in the Latin American context. Side A persuasively argues that major cartels are highly diversified and that legalizing domestic cannabis retail primarily displaces small-scale local dealers rather than crippling the powerful organizations that threaten the state. Furthermore, Side A effectively highlights that building a regulatory apparatus in low-capacity environments drains scarce enforcement resources and creates new targets for corruption, making their alternative of decriminalization and targeted enforcement much more compelling. Side B relies too heavily on general economic arguments about legal competition without adequately addressing the specific mechanics, export reliance, and diversification of Latin American cartels.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they sharply distinguish between "legal conversion" (measuring retail substitution) and actual "criminal power" (territorial control and diversified revenues), effectively neutralizing Side B's claim that a narrower criminal market equates to a weaker cartel.
