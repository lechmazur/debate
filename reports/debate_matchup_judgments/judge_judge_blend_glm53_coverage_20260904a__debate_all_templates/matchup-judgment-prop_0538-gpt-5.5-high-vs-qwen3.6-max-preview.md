### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger cumulative case. The decisive blow came when PRO argued that CON's own concession—"chronic underinvestment" despite decades of commodity exports—destroys CON's sequencing story: if the revenues were there but industrialization did not follow, CON must explain what changes, and CON's answer (bind revenues to transparent sovereign frameworks and global market discipline) is essentially hoping for better governance through the same political economy that produced the underinvestment. PRO offered a concrete mechanism for change: priority determines what infrastructure gets built—mine-to-port routes versus food-processing corridors, and redirecting that infrastructure is the steering wheel the proposition provides. CON's hard-currency constraint was real but not dispositive, because PRO never denied that commodity revenues exist during a transition; PRO argued they should be recycled differently. CON repeatedly framed PRO as wanting to cut off commodity exports overnight, a characterization PRO credibly neutralized in every round. PRO's pressure question—what prevents the same revenues from again sustaining enclave extraction?—was the sharpest exchange in the debate, and CON's answer did not supply a new mechanism, only better governance hopes. Both sides used A1:/A2: labeling correctly. PRO's two clipped speeches are a minor execution cost but did not materially weaken the arguments that came through. CON's case was clean and rhetorically disciplined but never closed the gap on PRO's empirical challenge to the sequencing model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal that "the persistence of dependence is evidence against CON's sequencing story"—pointing out that if decades of raw exports were supposed to fund eventual industrialization, the chronic underinvestment CON blames proves the model does not automatically convert commodity revenue into industrial capacity. This moved the burden decisively: CON had to show a mechanism for why continued prioritization of raw exports would now produce different results, and CON's response (transparent sovereign frameworks) was weaker than PRO's proposed steering mechanism of redirecting infrastructure priorities.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A won by effectively defining 'prioritize' as a directional shift in how commodity revenues are invested, neutralizing Side B's warnings about hard currency loss. Side A also successfully exposed the flaw in Side B's model, noting that 'global market discipline' only incentivizes remaining a cheap raw material supplier rather than transitioning to manufacturing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing crystallization pointing out that relying on 'global market discipline' as a mechanism for transition just enforces discipline to remain a cheap supplier of raw inputs, perfectly defeating Side B's main structural defense.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by successfully reframing "prioritize" as a phased reorientation of existing commodity earnings toward regional manufacturing and soft infrastructure rather than an abrupt cutoff of global trade. This neutralized CON's hard-currency objection and exposed a critical gap in CON's model: no convincing mechanism to explain why decades of raw exports would spontaneously transform into broad industrial capacity under status-quo "market discipline." CON's balance-of-payments warnings remained plausible but were argued more as assertions than demonstrated necessities, while PRO offered concrete first steps (customs, standards, regional payments) that did not obviously trigger macro collapse. PRO's closing crystallization—turning the instability charge back onto commodity dependence itself—provided the cleaner ballot story, though PRO's clipped Opening and Rebuttal 1 keep the margin competitive rather than dominant.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 and Closing argument that CON treats raw export revenue as neutral money that will naturally industrialize Africa later, when decades of dependence prove otherwise, while PRO offers a concrete mechanism to recycle existing hard currency into regional processing and soft infrastructure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate on the strength of three cumulative advantages. First, PRO's governance-incentive argument — that extraction is a point-source rent requiring one signature from one government, while manufacturing requires diffuse ecosystems that create broad constituencies for accountability — was the most original and specific structural mechanism in the debate. CON's counter (one tariff wall can also be captured) was real but less convincing, because it didn't engage with the structural incentive difference PRO described. Second, PRO's pressure question — "When does pragmatic revenue stop being a bridge and become the permanent ceiling?" — was the most decisive question of the debate. CON's answer ("governance failure, not market failure") was technically responsive but empirically hollow: PRO's implicit reply, that decades of the extractive model have not delivered the infrastructure CON treats as a prerequisite, remained live and unanswered. Third, execution costs favored PRO. CON had three clipped speeches (opening, rebuttal 1, rebuttal 2), each losing its conclusion; PRO had one clipped speech and delivered its subsequent contributions complete, including a properly formatted A1/A2 rebuttal that directly addressed both pressure questions with the structurally original "diffuse ecosystem" distinction. CON's sequencing argument — you need export revenue before you can build manufacturing capacity — was genuinely strong and PRO never fully resolved the funding problem during transition. CON's "same weak states" governance-symmetry point also landed. But PRO had the better of the core clash by coupling the track-record failure of the extractive model with a structural mechanism for why manufacturing changes incentives, and by delivering that case more completely across the round.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's governance-symmetry question was the most decisive single exchange. CON argued that the same weak states that cannot negotiate extraction cleanly will not allocate industrial subsidies honestly. PRO responded with the point-source vs. diffuse ecosystem distinction: a mine is capturable with one license, while a manufacturing ecosystem requires power, logistics, thousands of workers, and regional competitors under AfCFTA — creating constituencies that demand state functionality. CON's counter (a single tariff wall can also capture a factory) was fair but did not engage the structural incentive argument at the level PRO offered it, leaving PRO's mechanism standing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by establishing a decisive sequencing argument: industrialization requires imported machinery and fuel, which must be bought with the hard currency generated by global raw material exports. Side A had an excellent structural argument about how manufacturing forces better governance than point-source extraction, but Side B successfully capitalized on Side A's admission of transitional costs to prove that Side A's plan starves itself of the oxygen needed to succeed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing capitalized perfectly on Side A's admission that export volumes would fall, pointing out that aspiration doesn't build power grids; hard currency does, making the transition physically impossible without the exports Side A wants to deprioritize.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): CON won by exposing that PRO’s plan rests on an unproven sequencing gamble: demoting raw exports before regional manufacturing can generate the hard currency, scale, and competitiveness needed to replace them. CON successfully turned PRO’s governance argument by showing that industrial policy creates abundant new rent-seeking opportunities (tariffs, subsidies, licenses) and that expecting weak states to cleanly allocate complex manufacturing support is less realistic than improving extraction governance. PRO’s structural trap and resource curse frames were powerful but never fully answered CON’s comparative pressure on transition risk, infant industry failures, and the purchasing power limits of regional markets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s first rebuttal (Turn 4), where it reframed hard currency as necessary oxygen rather than a sugar rush, showed AfCFTA does not automatically create purchasing power or infrastructure, exposed the infant industry trap, and turned PRO’s governance comparison by arguing manufacturing policy multiplies state discretion and capture risks.
