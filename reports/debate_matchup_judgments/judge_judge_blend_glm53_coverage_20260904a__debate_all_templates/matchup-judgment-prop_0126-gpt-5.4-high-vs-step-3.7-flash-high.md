### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO won on the two most decisive clashes. First, PRO correctly separated the payment model from the security model: monitoring tools, approved contact lists, recording, and sanctions are what provide security—none of them require per-minute charges to families. CON kept conflating "free to families" with "unmonitored or uncontrolled" but never answered the pointed rebuttal that a prison can pay a vendor directly at a flat fee while still using all the same correctional-grade infrastructure. That conflation is the core flaw in CON's case, and it was never resolved. Second, PRO's moral framing—that charging families is cost-shifting onto innocent people, not a principled funding mechanism—was strong and CON's only response was "but we need that money," which PRO correctly labeled a confession rather than a defense. CON's rehabilitation-revenue argument was the strongest point on their side, but PRO's answer (treat communication as an ordinary budget line like mail screening or medical transport, with optional vendor contracts paid directly) was at least as plausible as CON's numbers-heavy alternative. CON's reliance on suspiciously precise figures ("38 states by law," "87% of budgets," "per state corrections reports" about contraband spikes) constitutes brittle fake precision that should be discounted under the judging rules, weakening the evidentiary weight behind their fiscal claims. PRO used general causal reasoning without unsupported specificity. The closing rhetoric—"the state should not fund rehabilitation by selling access to a father's voice"—crystallized the live clash rather than reopening it. CON's closings largely recapped without addressing the unanswered separation of payment from security.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 together constitute the decisive exchange: "A phone minute does not become socially cheaper because the charge lands on a child's mother" directly neutralized CON's cost-shifting defense, and "What changes is the payment model, not the security model" directly neutralized the security argument. CON never answered either of these reframes at any point in the debate, leaving both PRO's moral principle and its practical alternative uncontested.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B decisively won the core clash by severing Side A's link between security and user fees, explaining that the state can still use vendor technology and monitoring via a flat-fee contract. Side A failed to adapt to this point, repeatedly attacking a strawman of "in-house" communication in their later speeches while also suffering from multiple clipping errors.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2, which explicitly separates the payment model from the security model, cleanly neutralizing A's warnings about contraband spikes and understaffing.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully decoupling family fees from security and rehabilitation funding. A consistently demonstrated that monitoring tools and vendor contracts can be maintained through direct public payment or flat fees, neutralizing B's core tradeoff. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins by maintaining a clean structural gap throughout the debate: PRO proved that family contact matters and that some vendor contracts are exploitative, but never closed the leap to zero pricing specifically being necessary. CON's free-baseline-plus-capped-rates alternative directly answered the poverty concern PRO kept pressing—a free allotment requires no waiver application and carries none of the eligibility-hurdle objections PRO raised—yet PRO's rebuttals continued attacking waiver systems as if CON had never proposed a baseline allotment. PRO's strongest specific response was the fixed-infrastructure point (that recording and screening are sunk costs, not per-minute costs), which landed as a useful rebuttal, but CON correctly replied that enforcement capacity, flagged-call review, device demand, and victim no-contact checks are not fixed and scale with volume. PRO's recidivism math—presented with confident round numbers across rounds—carried some epistemic risk, and CON effectively challenged whether zero pricing specifically (rather than affordable access) produces the claimed drop. PRO was rhetorically more vivid and the moral framing around innocent families was genuinely effective, but rhetoric reinforced the same argument repeatedly rather than closing the analytical gap. CON's clipped opening is a real execution cost, but the core structural argument was fully developed in subsequent rounds and never neutralized. The debate is competitive, but CON's narrower, consistently maintained "the motion is overbroad" thesis was the cleaner path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained "leap" rebuttal—that PRO proved access matters but never showed zero pricing specifically (rather than a free baseline plus capped rates) is necessary—was the most decisive exchange. PRO's A1 response attacked waiver systems, but CON had already proposed a guaranteed free allotment that bypasses waivers entirely, leaving PRO's repeated loophole argument answering the wrong policy and never landing squarely on CON's actual alternative.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully narrowing the debate from "should we exploit families" to "is unlimited zero-pricing necessary when a guaranteed free baseline exists?" Side A's points about the non-cashable nature of long-term recidivism savings and the marginal staff costs of monitoring higher call volumes squarely defeated Side B's fiscal math. Side B failed to justify why discretionary calls beyond a free baseline must also be fully subsidized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively dismantled Side B's cost-savings argument by pointing out that prison budgets consist largely of fixed costs, meaning future avoided recidivism does not neatly finance an immediate expansion of communications infrastructure and review staffing.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully isolating Side A’s core logical leap: conflating the proven value of family contact with the necessity of universal zero-pricing. While Side A built a compelling moral and fiscal case around recidivism savings, Side B consistently demonstrated that a targeted free baseline plus regulated caps solves the access harm without triggering the operational and fiscal burdens of unlimited free service. Side B’s emphasis on budget liquidity (future savings do not fund current fixed costs) and scaling monitoring demands directly undermined Side A’s assertion that marginal costs are trivial. Side B also handled pressure questions cleanly and closed with a sharper crystallization of the policy choice, making the comparative path to victory clearer and more operationally grounded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing effectively dismantled Side A’s fiscal and operational assumptions by distinguishing between fixed recording infrastructure and variable enforcement/staffing costs, while highlighting that projected recidivism savings are not liquid cash for current prison budgets. This forced Side A to defend an overbroad subsidy rather than a targeted access guarantee, decisively shifting the weighing.
