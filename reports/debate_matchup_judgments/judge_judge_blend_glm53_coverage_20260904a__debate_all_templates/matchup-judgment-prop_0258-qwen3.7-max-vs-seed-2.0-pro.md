### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): A won by making the cleaner comparison between an accessibility layer and an enforcement layer. B powerfully framed unreadable law as unfair and exclusionary, but leaned heavily on unsupported ratios and never fully defeated A's point that plain-language guides can inform laypeople while precise statutes preserve enforceable rights. A also showed how vagueness can be exploited by powerful litigants, which directly answered B's vulnerable-person framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was the response that public guides can translate law for ordinary people, but the statutory source text must remain precise so landlords, corporations, or the state cannot exploit vague plain-language standards in court.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side B won by successfully separating the 'discovery' of law from its 'enforcement.' By arguing that third-party guides already solve the public accessibility problem, Side B neutralized Side A's primary offense while powerfully demonstrating that sacrificing statutory precision would create semantic loopholes that heavily favor well-resourced corporations in court.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 brilliantly flipped Side A's '70% vs 95%' analogy, arguing that a plain-language mandate does not actually increase discovery since guides already exist, but merely drops the enforceability of rights down to 50% by introducing fatal ambiguity.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won by exposing a stubborn tension Side B never resolved: if citizens are strictly liable for the statutory text, it is unjust for that text to be unreadable, and third-party guides do not fix the unfairness because errors in translation still leave citizens liable for the opaque original. Side A also landed the decisive comparative weighting that a right frequently used but occasionally clarified by judges is more just than a perfectly precise right the vast majority of people never discover, and Side B never meaningfully answered Side A's demonstration that supposedly precise terms already generate everyday judicial ambiguity. Side B's enforceability argument remained live but could not overcome the access bottleneck.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (Turn 7), where A4.1 dismantled the myth that current legal precision eliminates judicial discretion by showing even "24-hour written notice" spawns routine interpretive disputes, and A4.4 introduced the decisive 70/95 comparative weighing that crystallized the tradeoff and left Side B asserting unsupported percentages rather than engaging the comparison.
