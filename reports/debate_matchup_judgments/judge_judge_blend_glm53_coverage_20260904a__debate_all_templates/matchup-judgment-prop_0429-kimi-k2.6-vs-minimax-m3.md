### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): This was genuinely close, but PRO (Side B) edges it. PRO's developmental buffer argument supplied the clearest causal mechanism in the debate: a real-time notification at 10:00 AM about a 9:45 incident arrives before the child has navigated the recess apology, peer negotiation, or self-correction—and CON never truly neutralized that temporal claim. CON's responses (most parents don't react immediately; the social environment already collapses intervals) were plausible deflections but did not address the specific mechanism. PRO also correctly identified that CON's "better design / response norms" answer is a promissory note against platforms whose business model depends on maximizing interruptions—a point CON never answered substantively. PRO's equity reversal (affluent parents refresh between meetings; working parents face a firehose they cannot monitor during shifts) was clever and held up reasonably well against CON's counter (shift workers check at breaks) which only partially answered the asymmetry. CON's strongest card—the "one integrated platform" infrastructure argument, fully crystallized in the closing as "not a speed limit, it's a sight limit"—was compelling and the debate's best single piece of rhetoric. However, PRO's response (emergency protocols already operate differently even on shared hardware; the proposition targets behavior dashboards, not crisis channels) was a plausible and adequate answer given that emergency school contacts have always worked through distinct priority channels. CON slightly over-claimed by asserting the integrated-feed reality without grounding it, and the design/norms concession weakened its own constructive. PRO's reference ID anomalies in the closing were a small execution cost but did not materially damage the live case. On balance, PRO's core developmental argument and equity reversal remained live routes to victory after the full exchange; CON's closing was the debate's rhetorical high point but came too late to unseat PRO's substantively better-developed case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal exchange was over the equity argument. CON introduced dashboards as "proxy presence" for working parents in the opening; PRO flipped it in Rebuttal 1 by arguing affluent, flexible-schedule parents exploit real-time feeds while shift workers face a firehose they cannot process during work hours. CON's counter (they check at breaks or midnight, which is faster than a weekly summary) was concrete but only partially answered the asymmetry. PRO's reversal—that the system structurally advantages the very parents it purportedly helps less—remained a live, partially unanswered pressure throughout the rest of the debate and contributed meaningfully to PRO's narrow win.

- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a clearer developmental mechanism (latency buffer for self-regulation) that survives CON's rebuttals, directly answering pressure questions on channel separation and temporal harm, and exposing CON's equity/safety claims as reliant on unproven conflation of routine and crisis data; CON's responses remain largely aspirational (design norms) or defensive without neutralizing the core clash.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO (B) held the stronger conceptual position throughout. Its "default architecture" framing was not just rhetorical—it answered the motion precisely, targeting the design-stage choice rather than all school-parent communication, and it consistently preserved alternative channels (phone calls, flagged urgent alerts, weekly summaries, IEP notes) to neutralize CON's equity and access objections. The vendor-incentive argument—that granularity is the product pitch, not the bug, and that districts adopt platforms under time pressure—was never squarely answered by CON, who responded only with a general appeal to procurement standards that PRO had already claimed as its own territory. The most decisive exchange was on the line-drawing question. CON's Q1 was sharp: name a design feature separating a permitted alert from a prohibited dashboard event. PRO answered clearly (teacher-initiated judgment vs. automated passive broadcast) and then executed a clean turn: CON equally could not name that internal line, which is precisely why the discipline must live at the design stage rather than inside configuration. CON's "seatbelt vs. banning the car" analogy was rhetorically effective and the two-tier system argument was genuine pressure, but PRO's response—that teacher judgment decides urgency exactly as it does today—was adequate. CON's strongest live route (blanket prohibition harms families with acute needs) was blunted by PRO's repeated clarification that urgent channels survive intact. Both sides incurred clipping penalties, but CON's was in the Opening (the main evidence round) and PRO's were at the tails of turns where core content was already delivered, making the net execution cost roughly comparable. PRO's comparative framing—"reports invite kitchen-table conversation; dashboards invite in-classroom intervention"—was the clearest crystallization of the core tradeoff and went unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q1: PRO drew the permitted/prohibited line at teacher-initiated judgment versus automated passive broadcast, then turned the question back—CON could name no internal design feature distinguishing the two either, which confirms the line must be drawn at the procurement/design stage rather than inside the platform. This both answered the pressure and reframed the resolution's demand as the only workable solution.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing Side A's proposed solutions (batching, notification limits, procurement standards) as concessions that platforms indeed need to be limited from becoming real-time dashboards. Side B maintained a clear distinction between human-curated urgent alerts and automated raw-data feeds, neutralizing Side A's claims about forced ignorance and inequity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 expertly turned Side A's Q1, pointing out that because no clean line exists inside a default-on architecture, the limitation must occur at the procurement and design stage.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A wins because its architecture-default and escalation framing was never squarely neutralized; A's pressure-question answers drew a sharper, live distinction between filtered alerts and passive feeds that B could not match, while B's "trust norms" rebuttals stayed more general.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's direct A1/A2 answers on teacher judgment vs. automated broadcast, which crystallized the design-stage line and forced B into post-hoc fixes.
