### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, competently argued cases and followed the pressure-question format correctly, but CON (A) did more sustained damage to PRO's practical mechanism. A's overbreadth argument (NAACP, Sierra Club, tenant unions swept up by a "ban corporate donations" rule) was concrete and recurred unrebutted in substance—B's reply that grassroots groups "draw strength from members, not corporate treasuries" doesn't actually answer how a literal corporate-donation ban avoids catching incorporated advocacy groups. Similarly, A's incumbency-entrenchment argument (challengers need seed money to overcome incumbents' free media and name recognition, which caps don't touch) was pressed consistently from opening through closing; B's answer—"mega-donors mostly fund incumbents, so caps help challengers"—never engaged the specific mechanism of non-monetary incumbency advantage, leaving that clash unresolved in A's favor. On pressure questions, B's answer that "the motion itself" strips constitutional protection from shell IE groups was more assertion than mechanism, and A's closing correctly flagged that this requires restricting independent citizen association beyond what the motion specifies—a point B never squarely rebutted. B's normative equality framing was strong and rhetorically clean, but the debate turned more on implementation and second-order effects, where A's concrete examples and sustained incumbency mechanism gave it the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's incumbency argument (free media/name recognition advantage untouched by caps) versus B's repeated "money flows to incumbents" reply—B never directly answered why removing challengers' main tool to overcome structural incumbency advantages doesn't hurt them more than it helps.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by exposing that A's stated donation rules were simultaneously overinclusive and easy to evade. B also preserved concrete harms to incorporated civic groups and challengers needing seed funding, which A answered mainly by assertion rather than comparative analysis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive response was that rejecting money-as-speech merely permits further regulation; it does not itself close shell-group or independent-expenditure routes. This forced A either to leave major loopholes open or defend broader restrictions on association and self-funding beyond the proposition's specified policies.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, plausible cases, but A (CON) executed the later rounds more effectively. A exploited a real textual gap in B's case: the motion bans "corporate political donations" without exception, yet B tried to narrow this to "shareholder-first" firms only, leaving unresolved how union/civic-group treasuries (as opposed to individual $1,000 member gifts) survive the ban. A also turned B's own concession (that corporate media retains full editorial freedom) into a sharp asymmetry argument in Rebuttal 2 and Closing that B never directly answered. A's dark-money mechanism (contribution caps historically push spending into unaccountable independent-expenditure vehicles) is grounded in plausible real-world campaign-finance dynamics and was never fully rebutted, partly because B's Rebuttal 2 was cut off by the word-limit before it could respond to that point—a real execution cost per the judging rules. B's causal-reversal argument on incumbency (unlimited donations currently favor incumbents 2-to-1) was a strong opening move, but A's "non-financial incumbency advantages dominate" response, while not perfectly engaging the causal claim, was reinforced by the media-asymmetry point in later rounds, and B's Closing mostly recapped earlier claims rather than directly neutralizing A's sharpened attacks. The Pressure Questions exchange was well-matched and both sides labeled A1/A2 correctly, but B's answers, though logically framed (the content-neutral contribution/speech distinction was a genuinely solid point), were undercut by the clipped follow-up and a closing that didn't fully close the loop on civic-group treasuries or the dark-money rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 turning B's own concession in [B4.1] (that independent editorial/media speech remains unregulated) into an asymmetry argument—civic associations lose their collective treasury tool while corporate media keeps full influence—was the most decisive and least-answered exchange, compounded by B's Rebuttal 2 being clipped before it could respond to the dark-money and union-treasury points.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that the proposed restrictions could entrench incumbents, disable collective civic funding, and redirect influence toward media owners or less transparent channels. Its case also leaned heavily on unsupported precise figures and was clipped in Rebuttal 2.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response on incorporated civic groups was decisive: the motion contains no clear exemption for unions or nonprofit associations, while Side A’s distinction between shareholder corporations and civic organizations was asserted rather than derived from the proposition.

On corporate ban, PRO's press-exemption vs direct-contribution distinction and for-profit vs voluntary association carve-out survived CON's overbroad reading, while CON's media-privilege point was mitigated. PRO also handled pressure Qs more directly.
