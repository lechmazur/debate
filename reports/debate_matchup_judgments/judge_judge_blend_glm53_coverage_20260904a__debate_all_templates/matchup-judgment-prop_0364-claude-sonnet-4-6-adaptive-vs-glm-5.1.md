### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (PRO)): B won because it made the core fairness principle clearer: when compensation depends on reach, the payer should not be the only party defining and reporting the metric. A landed a strong hit that cross-platform comparability is not needed for contract-specific audits, but B recovered by reframing standardization as externally anchored metric categories rather than one universal metric. A's audit-right alternative did not fully solve definitional manipulation or unequal bargaining power, and its competitive-intelligence objection overstated what targeted standardized disclosure would require.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's decisive rebuttal was that A's audit-right concession is incomplete: auditing data is not enough if the platform can define the compensable unit itself. A effectively exposed the weakness of B's early comparability claim, but B's later merchant-and-weights framing better answered the central burden.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): CON successfully pressed PRO on how standardized metrics would work across diverse business models, forcing PRO to clarify that multiple metrics would exist but be 'externally defined.' CON capitalized on this by pointing out that such external definitions require regulatory bureaucracy that picks winners, while demonstrating that contract-specific negotiations (like the 2023 strikes) already provide adequate, tailored verification.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 effectively dismantled PRO's 'multiple standardized metrics' clarification by showing it either collapsed back into contract-specific definitions or required an intrusive regulatory body.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON showed that PRO's call for standardization either collapses into contract-specific audit rights (making the mandate redundant) or requires an unspecified external body to define metric categories in ways that distort competition. PRO never resolved who would set these "externally defined" standards or how that authority would avoid advantaging some business models over others. Meanwhile, CON offered a concrete alternative—negotiated, contract-specific audit rights—that protects performers without flattening legitimate platform diversity. The 2023 strikes evidence cut both ways, but CON's use demonstrated that tailored contractual solutions already emerged without imposed universal metrics. PRO's closing being clipped for length left key burdens unanswered, while CON crystallized the clash cleanly in the final speech.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4) exposing that PRO's softened definition of standardization—allowing multiple metric categories to coexist—either devolves into the very contract-specific terms CON advocated or empowers a regulator to pick competitive winners, gutting the practical case for mandatory intervention.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. B kept the central fairness claim focused: when pay depends on audience reach, creators need access to the underlying count in a verifiable form. A had a strong challenge that standardization could become a blunt regulatory apparatus and that contract audit rights are preferable, but B persuasively narrowed standardization to methodology, granularity, and format sufficient for verification rather than one universal view metric. A also incurred a notable execution cost by not directly answering B's pressure questions, especially the demand for a specific competitive harm from non-public disclosure to the compensated creator. B's closing was clipped, but its core crystallization was already visible and consistent with the winning route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive rebuttal was that ordinary audit rights are hollow if the platform controls the form, aggregation, and methodology of the data being audited. That directly undercut A's contract-law alternative and made standardized disclosure look like a precondition for meaningful enforcement rather than an unnecessary add-on.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully boxing Side A into a corner regarding the definition of 'standardized'. When Side A conceded that different contracts could use different metrics and that courts would enforce the rules, Side B capitalized on this to prove Side A was effectively just arguing for well-drafted contractual audit rights under the guise of a 'mandate', which would only add regulatory bureaucracy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, effectively weaponizing Side A's answers from the pressure questions to show that Side A's mandated 'form' of disclosure would require the very regulatory apparatus Side B warned about.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO established that symmetric information is a prerequisite for fair contracting when compensation depends on metrics, and showed that contractual audit rights are structurally hollow if the paying party unilaterally controls the methodology and format of disclosure. CON never overcame this "fox counting chickens" problem, and their warnings of regulatory bureaucracy and competitive leaks remained speculative and underdeveloped compared to PRO's concrete account of exploitation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal that audit rights without standardized disclosure amount to "a right to inspect a locked room," which directly undermined CON's central claim that existing contract law and negotiated audit provisions are sufficient.
