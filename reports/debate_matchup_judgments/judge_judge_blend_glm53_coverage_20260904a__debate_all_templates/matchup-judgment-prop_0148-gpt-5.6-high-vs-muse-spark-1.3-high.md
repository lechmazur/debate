### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases and converged on the same real crux: what happens when parent and teenager disagree without hard proof of danger. A's strongest and most consistent contribution was the causal mechanism that routine surveillance drives concealment and destroys the disclosure channel that actually catches risk, plus a memorable "ordinary Tuesday" framing of the burden question. B's case was slightly more disciplined and cumulative: it repeatedly showed (B2.5, B4.4-4.6, B5.4) that A's own concessions—curfews, known whereabouts, contextual exceptions for impulsivity/depression/danger—already require the same parentally-discretionary judgment calls that CON's "conditional privilege" model uses, effectively collapsing the distinctiveness of the "right" framing A was defending. A's answer to this (distinguishing "behavioral cues" from "content dragnets") was a fair line but never fully dissolved the deeper point that both sides ultimately rest on parental discretion under uncertainty. B also closed with an unanswered asymmetry argument (irreversible concealed harm vs. reversible caution) and the "anxious parent" critique that A's model fails to constrain unreasonable parents while burdening reasonable ones. That combination of sustained structural pressure and a stronger final unanswered point gives CON a narrow but real edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's repeated "collapse" argument (B2.5, reinforced in B4.4-4.6 and B5.4) — showing that PRO's own contextual exceptions require the same parental judgment calls as CON's conditional-privilege model — was the most damaging sustained rebuttal, since PRO never fully separated its "growing right" from ordinary discretionary parenting.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically exposing the contradictions in Side A's 'suspicion-first' requirement. B successfully argued that A's exceptions (impulsivity, contextual risk, parent deciding day-to-day) either collapsed A's model into B's 'conditional privileges' or dangerously delayed parental intervention until after harm had occurred.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange. By leveraging A's concession that parents decide day-to-day, B proved that A's model fails to constrain the 'anxious parent' while simultaneously blocking reasonable preventive checks.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A won by clearly defining the core clash as a shift in the default burden of proof (right vs. privilege) and effectively demonstrating how Side B's "conditional privilege" model functionally permits indefinite monitoring driven by parental anxiety. Side A's "ordinary Tuesday" framing in the closing crystallized the practical difference between the two models, while Side B struggled to provide a concrete limiting principle to parental oversight that didn't just rely on the parent's own subjective judgment of proportionality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they answered the pressure questions by distinguishing between day-to-day parental decision-making and the underlying default rule, effectively neutralizing Side B's claim that their exceptions collapsed their principle.
