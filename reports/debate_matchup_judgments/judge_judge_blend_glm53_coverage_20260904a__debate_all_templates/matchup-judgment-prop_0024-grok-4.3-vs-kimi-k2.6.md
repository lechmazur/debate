### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Both sides ran competent, grounded cases, but CON (Side A) built the more convincing comparative path by round's end. CON's decisive move was converting PRO's own "medium term" admission into a structural flaw: PRO conceded relief is not immediate, and CON correctly pressed that households facing eviction now cannot wait years for filtering to work. This timing argument was never neutralized—PRO's Rebuttal 2 answered the displacement question with "total stock, not individual paths," which is abstractly defensible but tone-deaf to the proposition's affordability framing. CON's complementary arguments—financialization absorbing new units before they filter down, demolition of existing affordable stock counteracting gross supply gains, and "rationing by wealth is already happening"—formed a coherent cluster that PRO did not systematically dismantle. PRO's economic logic (filtering, competition among tiers, population math) is sound and harder to refute in the abstract, and PRO deserves credit for grounding and handling the A1/A2 format cleanly. But PRO's case felt aggregate and distant while CON consistently tied affordability to concrete, vulnerable people. CON's metric reframing—"certainty, speed, who benefits"—is a legitimate reading of the motion rather than an evasive swap. The clipped CON Rebuttal 1 is a real execution cost, and the "buildings transfer to housing associations" point in A2 is somewhat speculative, which limits the margin. Still, CON's vivid framing and exploitation of PRO's timing concession gave it the cleaner comparative reason to vote for it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's exploitation of PRO's "medium term" concession in Rebuttal 1 [A2.2] was the most decisive exchange. PRO had volunteered that supply relief operates over the medium term; CON immediately turned this into a structural indictment—a family receiving an eviction notice today gains nothing from abundance that may arrive five years hence. PRO never recovered a satisfying answer to what happens in the gap, making this the pivot point that most damaged PRO's live route to victory.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A won by keeping the debate on the proposition's core comparative mechanism: adding supply addresses the scarcity driving prices, while rent control and tenant protections mainly ration existing stock and risk reducing future rental supply. Side B was more vivid and raised real concerns about luxury construction, displacement, and immediacy, but leaned on broad unsupported claims and partially reframed affordability as only current protected tenants. Its answer to growing demand relied on vague European examples and social or nonprofit housing mechanisms that were outside the main policy comparison. Side A was somewhat generic, but its resource-base weighing survived better.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was pressing how rent control handles growing demand without rationing or exits, then arguing that B's displacement examples do not defeat the citywide net-capacity logic. B's response that buildings transfer to public or cooperative ownership was underdeveloped and introduced a different housing policy rather than defending rent control and tenant protections alone.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully reframing "effectiveness" around certainty, speed, and distributional impact, making PRO's aggregate supply metrics feel disconnected from lived affordability. CON's critique of filtering—highlighting how financialization and short-term rentals absorb new luxury units—directly undermined PRO's core causal chain. While PRO offered coherent traditional economic arguments about equilibrium and total stock, it struggled to answer displacement and time-horizon pressures, notably dismissing individual relocation paths in favor of abstract "net capacity." CON handled pressure questions cleanly with plausible mechanisms (European mixed models, nonprofit/co-op transfers) and maintained sharper, more memorable weighing ("certainty beats speculation") throughout. PRO's case was competent but overly abstract and repetitive, leaving CON's immediate-relief framing as the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 directly answered PRO's pressure questions by citing European mixed-market systems and explaining how exited landlord stock transfers to housing associations, effectively neutralizing the "supply contraction" threat while reinforcing the immediacy and certainty of tenant protections over PRO's medium-term filtering bet.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B built and sustained the cleaner route to victory across all five rounds. Its core logic—scarcity is the root cause, rent control rations a static pool while supply reform ends the zero-sum—was introduced crisply in the opening and never abandoned. The vacancy-chain/filtering mechanism was challenged by Side A but never convincingly refuted: Side A's counter (investors absorb units and prevent filtering) was undercut by Side B's observation that investors rely on rental yields and therefore must rent rather than hoard, meaning abundance still softens rents. The most live burden—what rent control offers newcomers who lack a protected lease—was posed cleanly in Q1 and Side A's answer in A1 (natural turnover provides access) was weak and unexplained, leaving the exclusion problem standing. Side B's "cascade of patches" framing in the closing was the most rhetorically effective moment in the debate and accurately crystallized the tension already on the table: each regulatory fix Side A proposed was itself evidence that capping rents creates downstream problems requiring more intervention. Side A's Rebuttal 2 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, which is a real execution cost on a pivotal speech. Side A's strongest argument—immediate relief for current renters versus uncertain multi-year construction lags—is genuine, but Side B neutralized it with the "speed is the wrong metric if the medicine is toxic" turn, and Side A never explained why its immediacy benefit extended to anyone without an existing protected lease. Overall, Side B answered the key pressure, maintained coherent framing, and left more live damage on the board.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, specifically the A2 answer: "Rent control delivers speed only for the incumbent. For the young worker or newly formed household who needs a home today, it offers nothing but a smaller, costlier market." This directly turned Side A's strongest claim—immediacy—into a liability, reframing speed as a benefit that excludes the very people affordability policy should most protect. Side A never recovered a response that covered newcomers.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): A won with the clearer causal story and better weighing: scarcity is the root driver, supply raises vacancies and serves newcomers, while rent control mainly protects incumbents and risks rationing, maintenance decline, and reduced rental stock. B raised real concerns about filtering, investors, land-cost capitalization, and immediate relief, but mostly asserted them and answered the newcomer and exit problems with additional regulatory patches rather than a convincing mechanism for broad affordability. A also handled the pressure questions more directly, while B's second rebuttal was clipped and less effective.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered investor and timing objections, reframed speed as relief only for incumbents, and turned B's habitability and anti-conversion measures into admissions that rent control creates problems supply expansion avoids.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a clearer causal story and a decisive weighing framework. B successfully framed the clash around the insider/outsider trade-off, arguing that rent control rations a shrinking stock for incumbents while excluding newcomers, whereas supply reform expands access for all. B sharply answered CON's investor and filtering critiques by explaining vacancy chains and yield compression, and effectively characterized CON's reliance on anti-conversion and habitability mandates as regulatory patches that compound market distortions rather than solve them. Side A made competent points about immediate relief and land-cost capitalization, but failed to adequately resolve the long-term exclusion problem or explain how heavy regulatory burdens would not further chill supply. B's rhetoric was also more memorable, making the comparative stakes easier to grasp.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of the speculation/filtering critique and the closing's "patches for self-inflicted wounds" frame. B cleanly explained how abundance compresses rental yields and cools hoarding, turning CON's investor argument on its head, while the closing crystallized why CON's compounding regulations implicitly admit the policy's core flaws instead of fixing them.
