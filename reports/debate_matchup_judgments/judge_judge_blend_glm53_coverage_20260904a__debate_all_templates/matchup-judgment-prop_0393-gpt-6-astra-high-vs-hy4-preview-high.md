### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO won a competitive debate by keeping the clash on lifecycle suitability and cheap-now versus impossible-or-costly-later installation, then converting CON’s own total-spending metric. CON landed the sharpest pressure by forcing the subsidy concession and reframing resource allocation, but never neutralized the core claim that targeted funding still leaves most units as a retrofit lottery across a building’s life. PRO’s clearer comparative path—finance the modest premium rather than export larger later costs—decided the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s R2 seizure of PRO’s amortization concession (both approaches now need resources; prioritize shortages) was the debate’s sharpest hit; PRO recovered by answering that untargeted units remain a disruptive lottery and that subsidizing the premium beats exempting it.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO won the total-suitable-housing comparison that both sides accepted. CON's strongest hit - that a per-unit premium isn't dissolved by a mortgage - forced B to concede amortization doesn't create feasibility, but B's subsidize-not-exempt answer kept supply and accessibility together. CON's alternative - fund only demonstrated shortages with no fixed share - conceded disruptive transfers for tenants who acquire disabilities [A4.1] and left most new units unchanged, becoming a lottery. Given the motion's modest-cost concession, paying for zero-step entries, wide doors, and blocking before drywall is cheaper than later demolition and displacement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.2-A2.3: CON showed spreading a premium across dozens of units and a long mortgage doesn't eliminate the upfront financing gap, forcing PRO in B4.2-B4.3 to abandon the "dissolves" claim and depend on subsidies.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A built a much stronger and more intuitive case around the lifecycle costs of construction, effectively arguing that modest upfront premiums prevent wildly expensive retrofits and displacement later. Side A also handled the pressure questions better, offering a concrete policy mechanism (subsidies and density bonuses) to address the feasibility gap, while Side B's defense of a "targeted" approach conceded that it would leave most units vulnerable to disruptive retrofits or forced moves. Side A's framing of accessibility as long-term infrastructure rather than a one-time accommodation was highly persuasive and largely unanswered by Side B's focus on upfront marginal financing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they directly answered the pressure questions, exposed the "lottery" nature of Side B's targeted approach, and successfully defended the mandate with a subsidy mechanism for marginal projects, neutralizing Side B's primary affordability objection.
