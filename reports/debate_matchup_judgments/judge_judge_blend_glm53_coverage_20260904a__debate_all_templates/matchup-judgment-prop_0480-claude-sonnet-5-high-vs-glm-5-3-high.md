- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the equal-cost comparison on high fixed costs, missing supply, and risk-pooling across the listed services, and by forcing B to repeatedly abandon pure unconditional cash for insurance, improvisation, or informal care. A’s “cash is only as good as the market” frame resolved the clash more cleanly than B’s “retreat” charge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2/closing pattern: every concrete need pulled a non-cash answer from B, so the insurance/improvisation pivots concede collective provision where markets fail.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): A wins because it successfully anchored the debate to the motion as written—universal services versus unconditional cash—and trapped CON into repeatedly proposing restricted, pooled, or informal non-cash mechanisms (insurance, co-ops, neighborly rides) whenever concrete needs arose. PRO showed that cash is only as good as the market beneath it, and in the core examples of the motion—childcare deserts, transit gaps, catastrophic health risk—cash cannot create supply or pool catastrophic risk without becoming earmarked provision, which abandons the unconditional cash alternative. CON's heterogeneity and rigidity critiques stayed live but never overcame the fundamental market-failure problem that a flat transfer cannot summon a daycare or bus route into existence. PRO's progression from scale economies to the insurance-trap reframe was sharper and more coherent across rounds, while CON's closing restated claims without resolving the tension that its own solutions were not unconditional cash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7) was decisive: it exposed that CON's resort to subsidized insurance and informal care arrangements were not unconditional cash transfers, but rather earmarked or collective provision dressed in different labels. By showing that CON kept retreating to restricted, pooled, or improvised non-cash answers under pressure, PRO proved that even its opponent instinctively reached for provision-over-cash when needs became concrete.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a much stronger and more cohesive case by focusing on market failures, missing supply, and the inherent limitations of unconditional cash in sectors with high fixed costs or natural monopolies (transit, broadband, childcare, healthcare). Side A's pressure questions brilliantly forced Side B into advocating for conditional mechanisms (insurance) and inadequate market substitutes (informal care, buying a used car). Side A then effectively framed these answers as concessions that unconditional cash fails to solve the actual problems posed by the motion. Side B's attempt to distinguish between government funding and government operation was overly semantic and failed to overcome the core, unrefuted issue that unconditional cash cannot summon missing infrastructure or risk-pooling into existence in a desert.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side B's reliance on "insurance" and "informal care" by pointing out that insurance is restricted/conditional spending (thus conceding the principle of earmarked services over unconditional cash) and that paying a neighbor is an inadequate substitute for actual public infrastructure, effectively trapping Side B in their own rhetorical contradictions.
