### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides engaged the "achievable" burden directly, but A's central mechanism—that partition requires less ongoing trust because separated failures are containable while shared institutions require continuous daily trust ("divorce needs a witness, marriage needs love")—proved more resilient under pressure. B's Q1/Q2 pressed A on veto points and trust, and A's A1/A2 answers (phased, verifiable, distance-monitored steps vs. daily shared army/courts/schools) were reasonably concrete. When A pressed B on how the same immovable settler voters would ever accept shared army/courts/right-of-return with even less trust, B's answer (they wouldn't do it immediately, but might move toward "equal residence" more easily than evacuation) was less concrete and relied on an assumption not fully defended—that shared civil equality is inherently less traumatic than territorial withdrawal, despite B's own portrayal of one side controlling all levers. B's model also never fully answered the pointed critique that its "joint commissions" and "monitored disarmament" still leave the stronger party holding the levers, which A hammered effectively in closing. A's closing line reframing the core tradeoff (contained failure vs. failure "inside your parliament, your barracks, your school") was the most memorable and functionally decisive image of the debate, and B never landed an equally clean rebuttal to it before the debate ended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to B's Q2 pressure ("thin trust is different from thick trust"—verifiable, distanced partition failure vs. continuous shared-institution failure) combined with A's closing metaphor was the most decisive exchange, since B's rebuttal to it (in B4.4/B5.2) asserted symmetry ("if it cannot, partition collapses too") without explaining why shared army/courts require less trust than a monitored border, leaving A's asymmetry claim largely standing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A won by making the comparative achievability test decisive. It conceded severe obstacles but plausibly reduced them through land swaps, phased withdrawal, and third-party verification, while showing that Side B’s alternative inherits those same disputes and adds continuous shared governance between hostile national movements. Side B strongly exposed A’s vague reliance on “sustained cost,” but never adequately established why equal shared rule would be politically easier than partial territorial separation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s thin-trust versus thick-trust distinction was decisive: borders and demilitarization can be externally verified and failures partly contained, whereas shared courts, policing, security, and political authority require daily cooperation. Side B’s commissions and phased integration did not neutralize that added burden.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A won by making the decisive comparison clearer: if trust and veto politics make partition hard, they make equal-citizenship/federation harder still, and partition fails safer by containing cheating at a border. B’s strongest live attack—achievability and “mask for domination”—landed repeatedly, but never neutralized A’s dual self-determination burden or showed why Israelis who reject evacuation would accept minority status/shared coercive institutions. B’s incremental equal-residence path stayed underspecified relative to that clash; A’s thin-vs-thick trust frame and higher-bar counter resolved the motion more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (A4.3–A4.5): thin trust can verify phased separation while thick shared institutions cannot, so B inherits every veto plus more.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built serious, concrete cases, but A's pressure question ("if unresolved forever, that's domination; if resolved later, that's the final divorce you call impossible") exposed a real structural incoherence in B's "postpone finality" alternative that B never fully escaped. B's answer (A1 in Rebuttal 2) reasserted that trust-building makes later resolution easier without explaining why the same structural obstacles (settlements, security, identity) that make immediate finality impossible would dissolve later. A also effectively pressed that B's model, by design, never becomes a genuine "outcome" as the motion requires, but remains an open-ended management scheme vulnerable to the same core disputes—an argument B's closing did not rebut, instead repeating the "verifiable restraint today" framing. B did land real points on settler-evacuation politics, checkpoint-pierced sovereignty, and the Gaza/Lebanon security record, and A's responses to the Gaza-vacuum distinction were comparatively thin, relying on assertions of "reciprocal, monitored" design without explaining why West Bank withdrawal avoids the same vacuum risk. Overall, though, A's dilemma about domination-versus-finality was the more decisive, unresolved thread carried through to the closing, giving A the cleaner path to victory on the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question (A3.1) forcing B to choose between "permanent domination" and "the final divorce you call impossible," and B's incomplete answer (B4.1/A1) that never resolved the dilemma, was the most decisive exchange in the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A won a close debate by showing that B offered an indefinite process rather than a defensible long-term outcome. B powerfully challenged partition’s territorial and security feasibility, but never resolved whether permanent self-government under ultimate Israeli control avoids domination. A provided a clearer comparative endpoint and plausible, if difficult, mechanisms for making partition achievable over time.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure question exposed B’s central dilemma: leaving borders, Jerusalem, and refugees unresolved entrenches unequal control, while resolving them later restores the final settlement B called impossible. B’s answer shifted the timing but did not escape that dilemma.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): PRO won by keeping the motion comparative: every live alternative either collapses into domination, endless pressure without legitimacy, or a one-state trust demand larger than partition. CON’s achievability and Gaza attacks were serious and well pressed, but PRO’s replies (design/verification vs unilateral exit; swaps-and-blocs vs maximal expulsion; endpoint vs permanent non-sovereignty) kept two states as the only structure that converts the national clash into bounded interests. CON never stabilized a long-term political destination once Jerusalem, borders, force monopoly, and peoplehood stay open forever.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal plus pressure line forcing CON’s postpone-or-divorce dilemma, then A4 crystallizing self-rule without sovereignty as a pressure cooker rather than a stable least-bad endpoint.
