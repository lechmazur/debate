### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): B won by making the counterfactual decisive: denied borrowers still need money and may be pushed toward worse payday or informal credit. A had a vivid debt-spiral case, but it depended on broad, under-supported claims that high-interest cards generally cause collapse and that borrowers “go there anyway.” B effectively exposed that A’s argument only covered borrowers who spiral, while ignoring borrowers who would use costly credit temporarily and repay. A’s late “nine lives ruined for one saved” framing was unsupported and weakened its grounding. B’s clipped closing was a small execution cost, but its comparative path remained clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was answering A’s “stacking” argument: even if some borrowers use cards before payday loans, removing the card does not close the payday option and harms the subset who would have repaid and used the card as a liquidity buffer.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully defending the liquidity value of high-interest cards for some borrowers and enforcing strict epistemic discipline. Side A relied on an invented 9-to-1 failure ratio to dismiss the borrowers who successfully use and repay these cards, which Side B correctly called out as rhetorical invention. Side B also effectively defeated Side A's "lead paint" analogy by pointing out that unlike removing a hazard, removing a credit card does not remove the underlying financial emergency; it merely redirects it to worse, unregulated options.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal decisively damaged Side A's case by calling out the invented 9-to-1 destruction ratio and dismantling the "lead paint" analogy, proving that the cap simply redirects foot traffic rather than removing the hazard.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully turning Side B's core premise. While B argues that high-interest cards merely drain borrowers before they hit payday lenders anyway, A effectively demonstrates that this overgeneralizes and ignores the marginal borrower who uses the card as a short-term liquidity buffer to bridge a gap and repay. A shows that under the isolated motion, capping cards removes this buffer and accelerates access to worse, unregulated alternatives for those denied. B's analogies (lead paint, armed robbery) fail because they ignore the real liquidity benefit credit provides, which A sharply exploits. B's moral framing is rhetorically polished but relies on an unsupported ratio and cannot adequately answer A's weighing that the cap relocates and accelerates harm rather than preventing it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 dismantling the "they go to payday anyway" claim. A correctly identifies that if cards stack with payday loans, removing the milder option just strips the liquidity buffer and sends denied borrowers to the worse alternative faster. A also effectively calls out B's "armed robbery" analogy by highlighting the tangible benefit of liquidity for emergency expenses, shifting the weighing decisively toward preserving the regulated option.
