### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): CON built the tighter case by isolating the exact gap in the motion's text: transparency and debris-control obligations address disclosure and hazard, not the allocation/depletion problem that recognition of commercial title triggers. CON's "depletion without exclusion" mechanism (first movers can exhaust accessible ice without any formal claim) was introduced early and never squarely neutralized; PRO's rebuttals largely argued that ambiguity is no better, which is a comparative point but not a rebuttal of the mechanism itself. The pressure round crystallized this: PRO's A1 conceded that "no actor can conclusively impose a pause" when a licensing state refuses to yield on a contested deposit — a direct admission that PRO's proposed safeguards do not resolve the central harm CON identified, and CON exploited that concession decisively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing. PRO's strongest thread — that denying legality only deters compliant, licensable firms while state-backed actors proceed regardless — was a real and live point, but CON's answer (recognition increases total legitimized extraction without fixing allocation, and firms/governments will politically entrench interim gains before a treaty) kept that argument in tension rather than resolving it outright, making the exchange closer than a blowout but still favoring CON's framing. PRO's case also suffered an execution cost: its opening, first rebuttal, and second rebuttal were all clipped for exceeding word limits, cutting off its own synthesis exactly where it needed to land the final comparative point. CON delivered complete, disciplined speeches throughout and closed with the cleanest crystallizing line. On balance, CON answered the decisive pressure more directly and left PRO's central safeguard argument (title conditioned on transparency/debris control) looking under-inclusive relative to the harm CON kept foregrounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's A1 concession in Rebuttal 2/Closing — highlighting that PRO admitted "no actor can conclusively impose a pause" when a licensing state refuses to yield — was the most decisive exchange, since it showed PRO's own proposed mechanism failing exactly in the contested-deposit scenario CON had been pressing since the opening.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side A won by demonstrating that the PRO framework’s transparency and debris-control conditions, while valuable, failed to address the central harm of resource depletion and competing claims. Side B conceded that when a licensing state refuses to pause extraction, no actor can conclusively impose one, and that sold resources cannot be recovered by future rules. Side A effectively showed that legal recognition would attract additional commercial extraction before allocation disputes are resolved, spending leverage rather than preserving it. Side B’s multiple word-limit clips and reliance on an “impossible standard” frame could not overcome the live burden that conditional recognition authorizes irreversible transfers without an agreed authority to adjudicate them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal (Turn 8) seized on Side B’s admission that no international actor can force a pause when a licensing state rejects an objection, then combined it with the concession that recognition attracts hesitant firms to show the policy actively increases extraction under unresolved allocation conflict—making visibility an inadequate substitute for governance.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won the central allocation clash. Depletion without formal exclusion showed transparency/debris-control does not govern who may exhaust a contested deposit. PRO (B) conceded no actor can conclusively impose a pause and never supplied an allocation rule, and three clipped speeches cost completeness. PRO's leverage argument was turned: withholding title restrains precisely the market actors recognition would add, while irreversible extraction undercuts revisability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's depletion-without-exclusion turn in R1/R2: even without claiming territory or safety zones, selling the accessible ice physically forecloses later arrivals, which transparency merely records.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): The pivotal exchange came in Pressure Questions/Rebuttal 2. B's Q1 forced A to admit that conflicting national licenses have no binding resolution mechanism absent a treaty, and A's answer conceded that governments must "negotiate compatible access" and add extraction caps, priority rules, and suspension authority beyond transparency/debris control. B correctly identified this as a concession that the motion's stated two safeguards are insufficient to do the work A's own framework requires, and that ad hoc site-by-site negotiation among unequal regulators is exactly the unstable, first-mover-advantaging sequence B warned against. A's closing reasserts that "recognizing legality doesn't mean every operation is permissible," but never resolves who adjudicates contested sites in the absence of a treaty—which was the crux of B's pressure question. B's error-cost framing (reversible delay vs. irreversible depletion/entrenchment) was clearer and went less rebutted; A's response that "controlling removal before it happens" answers this somewhat, but doesn't specify what authority does the controlling pre-treaty, which is the very gap B exploited. B's closing crystallizes this cleanly, while A's closing restates its distinction without closing the authority gap. B also did slightly better answering A's questions with direct A1/A2 structure despite the clipped final rebuttal costing some execution credit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 in Pressure Questions ("which authority decides between conflicting national licenses...and why should its decision bind the other state?") and A's answer conceding governments must "negotiate compatible access" and add caps/priority rules beyond the motion's stated conditions—this exchange exposed that A's framework silently required exactly the pre-existing global coordination machinery it claimed was unnecessary.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON wins because it successfully showed that the proposition’s stated conditions—transparency and debris control—are insufficient to prevent irreversible harm from uncoordinated extraction, and that PRO repeatedly had to supplement the motion with unwritten safeguards (extraction limits, ad hoc diplomacy, priority rules) that presuppose the very treaty framework PRO claims is unnecessary. CON’s irreversibility and sequencing argument remained live: waiting delays investment reversibly, while premature legal recognition entrenches first-mover control and depletes scarce resources before shared rules exist. PRO’s distinction between owning extracted material and controlling territory was blunted by CON’s demonstration that saleable title creates the revenue and incentive to secure de facto exclusion through infrastructure and depletion. PRO’s answers to pressure questions conceded that competing licenses and depletion limits still require negotiation and restraint, which confirmed CON’s central point that the motion’s two safeguards cannot justify legality on their own.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1 and the follow-through in A4.1–A4.3: CON exposes that PRO must add extraction caps, dispute resolution, and site-specific negotiation outside the motion’s text, and then shows that PRO’s own answers to pressure questions concede the need for those extra rules, collapsing PRO’s claim that transparency and debris control alone suffice.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): B (PRO) wins the sequencing clash. It reframes the motion correctly as imposing transparency/debris as necessary conditions, not the only rules, preserving non-appropriation and allowing site-specific withholding. A (CON) needed a blanket ban from scarce-site risks and irreversibility, but B shows sale of lawfully recovered material does not equal a right to exhaust deposits or bind other states, and targeted limits plus withheld permission handle contested sites without a full global treaty. A’s clipped Rebuttal 2 also costs execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 answers: ownership of extracted material is not an unlimited extraction warrant; preventing depletion via license limits and distinguishing experimental removal footprint from sale, directly neutralizing CON’s irreversibility and allocation objections.
