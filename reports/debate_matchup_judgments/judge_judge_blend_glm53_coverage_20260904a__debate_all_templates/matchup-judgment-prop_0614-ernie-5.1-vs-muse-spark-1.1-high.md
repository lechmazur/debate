### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON (A) built and sustained a tighter mechanistic case: zero-days/spyware are non-scarce, replicable goods, so a ban does not stop the purchase but only destroys the one lever (licit, auditable, revocable vendors on Western infrastructure) that gives any leverage over abuse. A's recurring line—"you cannot revoke what you banned out of existence"—directly answered PRO's strongest rebuttal (that leverage like the NSO Entity List action came too late) by showing that a ban guarantees zero leverage rather than late leverage, and that substitution to Chinese/Russian/Emirati sellers is worse, not better, for accountability. PRO's case leaned heavily on moral framing ("complicity," "weapons of repression") and asserted that licit sales "normalize" and "fund" the black market, but never convincingly rebutted the substitution mechanism or explained why a cash-rich buyer wouldn't simply switch suppliers at equal cost. PRO's answer to the Mexico/Spain consistency question (democratic accountability exists there) was reasonable but didn't resolve the practical point that the targeted journalist is equally harmed either way, leaving the definitional/consistency pressure only partially addressed. Both sides executed the Q&A format correctly, but A's answers to B's pressure questions were sharper and more directly responsive, and A's closing crystallized the decisive asymmetry (loss of any off-switch vs. preserved leverage) more clearly than B's closing, which largely restated moral framing without closing the substitution gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 point that PRO's own admission—leverage only works on firms with US ties—means banning those firms guarantees permanent loss of audit/revocation power, paired with the closing line "you cannot revoke what you banned out of existence," most decisively undercut PRO's central enforceability and complicity argument.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by making the decisive on-balance comparison clearer: zero-days are weightless and replicable, so a ban does not dry up supply—it renounces the only vendors democracies can audit, license-expire, blacklist, or cut off (AWS/Entity List logic), while regimes still buy from opaque Chinese/Russian/Emirati sources and substitute coarser methods. Side B’s moral framing and “refuse complicity” close were forceful, and the democratic-deficit answer to Mexico/Spain was a solid save, but B never neutralized the enforceability/leverage clash or showed that late off-switches are worse than permanent blindness. A’s progressive answers on ongoing (not merely retroactive) control and scalpel-vs-sledgehammer substitution resolved the live dispute more cleanly for a third-party reader.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s response to B’s arms-control analogy and leverage claim (A2.1–A2.5 / A4.1–A4.3): zero-days are not trackable bullets; cutting the only revocable vendors eliminates the off-switch while supply continues underground.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): PRO won by exposing that CON's central leverage argument was empirically hollow: visible Western firms like NSO sold for years after abuse was documented, and the celebrated "off-switch" (Entity List, license curtailment) only arrived after scandal broke, not in time to protect victims. This turned conditional licensing from a practical alternative into a delayed permission slip. PRO also successfully defended the democratic-deficit boundary against CON's selective-enforcement push, showing that targeting non-democracies is a principled limit on complicity rather than an impossible taxonomy. CON's counter that bans push buyers to black markets remained live, but never overcame the complicity frame, especially since CON could not prove that supplying precise tools actually prevented the feared "sledgehammer" substitutes. The debate narrowed to whether democratic legitimacy matters more than theoretical visibility, and PRO's moral-practical synthesis held firmer through the final speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's dismantling of the conditional-licensing leverage claim by showing NSO's years of documented abuse without real-time revocation, reducing CON's "ongoing control" to after-the-scandal punishment that arrives too late for jailed journalists.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a more coherent case across rounds: a concrete enforcement mechanism (registered firms become criminally liable, unbankable, cut off from cloud/app-store infrastructure) that plausibly differs from failed discretionary licensing, plus a consistent distinction between intelligence-sharing and selling offensive tools. CON's case contained an unresolved internal tension — claiming zero-days are "just code" that crosses borders uncontrollably while also arguing commercial spyware "requires continuous vendor infrastructure" that black markets can't replicate — and PRO's pressure question exposed this directly. CON's most damaging moment was its own concession in A4.1 that "no post-sale audit has ever stopped a secret police," which undercut its own preferred alternative (licensed partnership/leverage) without CON ever explaining how partnership constrains behavior if no audit works. PRO seized on this concession in closing to devastating effect, while CON's closing repeated points about severed diplomatic leverage without repairing the contradiction or naming any working oversight mechanism. Both sides argued the motion as written and executed the Q&A format correctly, but PRO's rebuttals did more real damage and its closing crystallized the clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's use of CON's own admission in A4.1 ("no post-sale audit has ever stopped a secret police from targeting a journalist") as a self-defeating concession, turned into the decisive closing argument that CON's licensing/leverage alternative has no actual mechanism to prevent the harms it claims to mitigate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won by making the decisive clash cleaner: licensing has already failed with zero post-sale audits, so the claimed "leverage" and "partnership" are illusory, while a ban targets the real chokepoint (registered firms needing banks, C2 infrastructure, maintenance) and ends democratic complicity without needing perfect border control. CON raised real costs around vacuum, partner loss (Jordan/Egypt), and shifted suppliers, but the contradiction pressure stuck (tools both unstoppable yet dangerously lost) and the explicit A4.1 concession that no audit ever works undercut the alternative path more than CON could recover. Later rounds narrowed rather than widened the dispute in PRO's favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO B4/B5 seizing CON's A4.1 admission that "No post-sale audit has ever stopped a secret police" to collapse the leverage claim: if you cannot audit you cannot shape behavior, so licensing was only a permission slip.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A wins because it exposed a fatal structural flaw in Side B's case: there is no post-sale audit that prevents abuse, a point Side B explicitly conceded. Without that accountability, Side B's "licensing with leverage" framework collapses into mere complicity. Side A offered a coherent enforcement model targeting banking and infrastructure dependencies, while consistently highlighting Side B's contradictory claims that these tools are both unstoppable smuggled code and indispensable Western infrastructure. Side B never recovered from the audit concession or resolved this tension, and its closing misattributed its own admission to Side A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Pressure Question demanding evidence of any working post-sale audit, which forced Side B to concede that none has ever stopped secret police abuse and thereby destroyed the core claim that licensing provides meaningful leverage.
