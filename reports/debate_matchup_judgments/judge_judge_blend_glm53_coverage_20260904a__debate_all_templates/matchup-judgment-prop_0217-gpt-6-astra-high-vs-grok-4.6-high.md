### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-organized cases, but CON did more damage in the decisive exchanges. PRO's essential-allowance-plus-marginal-pricing framework was clear and principled, and its "total demand, not equal sacrifice" test was a reasonable metric. However, under pressure PRO conceded (A4.1) it would not promise to recover a wealthy irrigator's gallons and (A4.2) that allowances should not be set "tight enough to close the shortage." CON weaponized these concessions decisively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, reframing PRO's backstop restrictions as the mechanism that actually "delivers the gallons," turning PRO's own safeguard concept into evidence that pricing cannot be the true main instrument in recurring shortages. CON's memorable framing ("rationing by wallet," "who may keep irrigating IS the conservation policy," "an auction held while the reservoir falls") gave the debate its sharpest, most damaging rhetorical turns, and CON's willingness to directly bite the bullet on PRO's Q1 ("Yes") rather than dodge strengthened its credibility. PRO's closing rebuttal—that CON demands uniform prohibitions even when unnecessary—was a fair counter-frame but did not fully neutralize the earlier concession-based attack. The debate was close and competitive throughout, but CON's rebuttal sequence more effectively converted pressure into decisive leverage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turn (B4.3–B4.5), which converted PRO's own admissions from the Pressure Questions round ("I do not promise prices can force every wealthy household to stop," "do not set essential allowances tight enough to close a shortage") into proof that restrictions, not pricing, are what actually close a recurring shortage—reframing PRO's "backstop" as the real main instrument.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) kept the live route of protecting essential use and pricing discretionary excess while retaining bans as backstop, and directly answered equity/timeliness pressures with allowance adjustments, leak relief, advance notice and demand monitoring. CON (A) answered B's decisive Q1 with 'Yes' - preferring bans even if steep tiers achieve required savings while protecting essentials - which crystallized the debate as uniform prohibition over water-security outcome. That concession plus B's point that bans also need enforcement and that a known marginal price acts before use gave B the cleaner comparative win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Pressure Q1 asking if CON would still ban a wealthy lawn when savings target and essential protection are met, CON's A1: 'Yes,' and B's use of that admission in closing to frame CON as demanding uniform inconvenience even when unnecessary.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by effectively exposing the physical and equitable flaws in Side A's pricing model. B correctly identified that if wealthy users simply pay the higher rates, the water is still consumed, and the burden of meeting conservation targets shifts to price-sensitive lower- and middle-income users. B's rhetorical framing—that a bill arrives after the water is gone, while a ban stops the withdrawal—was highly persuasive and decisively answered the core clash. Side A's reliance on "total demand" targets failed to address B's equity critique, and A's closing mischaracterized B's stance as mere spite rather than a critique of burden-shifting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where B directly answers the pressure questions by pointing out that if the wealthy keep irrigating, the "required savings" are simply shifted onto those who cannot afford to pay, exposing Side A's "total demand" metric as a mask for rationing by wallet.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B built and sustained the more decisive structural point: pricing "hopes" for a reduction while restrictions directly require it, so "paying is compliance" even when the target withdrawal cut is missed. A never rebutted this beyond restating "make the price sharp enough," which B correctly flagged (B4.3) as asserting the outcome rather than establishing the mechanism. B also exploited a real inconsistency in A's own case: A's opening equity argument claimed flat bans hit the careful apartment as hard as the wasteful estate, but A's own pressure-question answer conceded the apartment "cuts nothing" under a targeted pool/lawn ban — collapsing A's signature fairness framing and letting B reframe "universal restrictions" as capable of preserving essential use while still limiting discretionary use, undercutting A's blunt-vs-precise dichotomy. A's strongest material — enforcement decay in recurring shortages, and revenue funding future resilience — was real but got matched by B's symmetry response ("restrictions also face compliance problems; you haven't shown deterioration is inevitable while price responsiveness is assured"), leaving A's uniqueness claim about "restrictions leak, prices don't" unproven. A's closing mostly restated earlier lines rather than resolving the "paying is compliance" clash, while B's closing sharpened exactly that distinction. This makes B's path to the vote cleaner and less contested at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3–B4.5: catching A's pressure-question answer as an unfalsified assumption ("states your desired outcome, not how officials know the required price") and using A's own concession that the apartment "cuts nothing" under a targeted ban to dismantle A's founding equity argument.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON wins on the central clash of water security vs billing. PRO never neutralizes the "paying is compliance" point: a steep tariff can succeed at raising revenue while missing the volumetric cut essential reserves require, so "set price high enough that use collapses" assumes its conclusion. PRO's leakiness attack on bans is symmetrized and then broken by CON: cheating is a violation to stop, not permitted use. CON also neutralizes PRO's fairness story by showing a citywide pool/lawn ban does not tax the careful apartment, while a price does let an estate buy scarce outdoor water. That leaves CON with a live route to victory protecting essential supply as backbone, prices as supplement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.1/A4.2-4.4 sequence that tariffs can hit revenue target while missing water target because paying = compliance, versus restrictions where continuing use stays proscribed — this turns PRO's funding argument into a concession.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by establishing the decisive analytical point that "paying is compliance" under a pricing scheme, meaning the physical water is still lost if wealthy users simply pay the surcharge. While Side A argued effectively that restrictions suffer from enforcement fatigue during recurring shortages, Side B successfully demonstrated that pricing introduces unacceptable uncertainty for essential physical reserves and conflates revenue generation with actual conservation. Side B's rebuttals were sharper and more logically rigorous, particularly in dismantling Side A's equivalence between unpaid cheating and paid exemptions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, specifically the distinction that "tariffs can miss the water target even with perfect compliance, because paying is compliance," which effectively neutralized Side A's core mechanism by highlighting the physical reality of water scarcity versus market clearing.
