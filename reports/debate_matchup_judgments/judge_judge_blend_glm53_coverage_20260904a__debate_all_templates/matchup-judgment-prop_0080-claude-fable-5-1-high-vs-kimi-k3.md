### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): A won narrowly by preserving the central distinction between contesting how imposed rules are applied and having athlete representation negotiate those rules. B created serious pressure on feasibility, fragmented bargaining units, and antitrust immunity, but its individual-arbitration alternative never supplied an athlete-side party able to shape the cap, roster rules, or standard contract. B also treated a collectively bargained ceiling as effectively predetermined, underplaying that athletes could negotiate or reject it. Both sides incurred clipping costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to B's arbitration alternative was decisive: an athlete selecting a neutral can challenge the application of a rule but cannot negotiate whether that rule should exist. This exposed why B's hardened compliance model did not provide the representation required by the proposition.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive, high-substance round by turning PRO's feasibility answers into live concessions and preserving a cleaner comparative. PRO framed the clash well—unilateral rulemaking versus a real table, and “setting terms” versus merely appealing them—and landed strong pressure on who picks CON's neutrals and on the pending antitrust exemption. But CON answered both questions directly, opposed the exemption, and then flipped PRO's unit and instrument replies: football/MBB-only units undercut “college athletes,” and voluntary conference recognition left the table's existence up to schools, so the cure for unilateral authority still needed management's permission and could not reliably deliver the labor-exemption durability PRO called decisive. CON's path—court-backed settlement architecture, athlete-controlled arbitration, and retained antitrust leverage available now—was easier to vote for after those exchanges than a conditional table that locks a lawsuit-proof cap over two sports in friendly jurisdictions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4–A4.7): converts PRO's unit (“follow the money”) and “SEC voluntary recognition” answers into concessions that the table is school-permitted, narrow, and legally incomplete, then reweighs against court-enforced individual process plus intact leverage.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B kept two live routes: unavailable/fragmented and durability trap. PRO's decisive instrument shifted to voluntary conference-level recognition, which CON showed is permission-based unilateral authority and, without employee status, cannot deliver the labor exemption that made [A1.5]'s stability claim decisive. PRO's unit answer conceding football/men's basketball only also narrowed the motion and invited Title IX pressure that went unanswered, while B directly answered A's Pressure Questions about arbitrator selection and exemption.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B R2: [A4.4] voluntary recognition is school permission and without employee status earns no labor exemption, so PRO cannot deliver the lawsuit-proof cap that was its core durability promise.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B won by separating direct payment from legal employee status and showing that collective bargaining necessarily triggers a classification and state-law problem that ordinary licensing contracts do not. A compellingly emphasized consent, enforceability, and antitrust protection, but “partial representation is better than none” did not resolve fragmented coverage, conflicting labor terms, or the exclusion of non-revenue athletes from plausible bargaining units. B’s tax and visa language was somewhat overstated, and two clipped rebuttals imposed an execution cost, so the result remains competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was the answer to A’s claim that payment itself settles employment. B distinguished licensing payments from employment, then used that distinction to argue that unions make classification costs certain where bargaining is available while still leaving athletes in restrictive states without bargaining rights. A never fully restored its foundational premise.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by making employment status the decisive precondition and keeping fragmentation live: half the flagship publics cannot bargain, conference multi-employer units largely fail, and PRO’s Big Ten exception does not produce one rulebook for transfer/medical terms or one championship. PRO never fully dissolved the tax/visa cascade as a cost of volunteering employee status, and the wrestler/unit point undercut PRO’s non-revenue protection claim. PRO landed cleaner “fruits without the tree” attacks on settlements and pressed the antitrust/ratification gap well, but the wash on the shield for non-union states plus CON’s uniform court-settlement path left the clearer vote against the motion. Word-limit clips slightly hurt CON’s later rebuttals without reversing the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 1 ([A2.1]–[A2.3]): employment is legal status, not the check; the union petition triggers the cascade that PRO treated as inevitable money-costs.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON won the definitional clash that decides the proposition. PRO needed direct revenue sharing to equal employment; CON showed it can be structured as licensing and that the union petition is what triggers employee status, taxes, visa loss, and workers' comp. That turned PRO's tax/visa costs from inevitable to voluntary, undercut the claim that employment was presupposed, and made the fragmentation point stick: public-sector bans bar bargaining in TX/AL/GA/FL, so at best one conference (Big Ten) can form a multi-employer unit, leaving two rulebooks for one championship on transfer/eligibility that PRO itself put in bargaining scope. CON also exposed PRO's inclusion promise – wrestlers with no revenue share are not employees and cannot be in the revenue-employee unit. Settlement alternative provides uniform, court-enforced terms without the entry fee.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON B2.2-B2.3: payment as licensing vs employee – employment is legal status, not a check; universities pay fellows without employing them; the cascade is the entry fee for the bargaining table, not the cost of the money itself – which reversed PRO's premise in A2.1.
