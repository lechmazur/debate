A's response—asserting users retain agency—was rhetorically forceful but analytically shallow against B's specific mechanism. The compliance-cost and Big Tech consolidation argument was introduced early but never developed into a decisive weighing point. Both closings were somewhat repetitive, but B's shorter closing crystallized the comparison more cleanly. The margin is clear but competitive; A's rhetorical polish and some strong individual moves keep this from being a dominant win for B.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won on the central clash: whether existing consumer law already covers the specific harm of emotional engineering in simulated intimacy. CON repeatedly asserted that FTC dark-pattern and unfair-trade-practice rules already prohibit manipulative AI design, but could never identify a specific statute that bans simulating emotional withdrawal to prevent churn—as distinct from financial deception. PRO's pressure question Q1 directly exposed this gap, and CON's A1 answered only at the level of "designs that manipulate emotional responses to keep users paying," which still sounds anchored to financial harm rather than relational engineering. By contrast, PRO's own A1 made a clean mechanistic distinction: unfair-trade rules stop hidden fees; they do not stop an algorithm from simulating a breakup. The "wallets vs. minds" framing in Rebuttal 2 and the closing crystallized this distinction memorably and without overreach. CON's best live argument—enforcement vagueness—was partially answered by PRO's mechanics-based definition (ban objective design patterns like hiding artificiality or engineering dependency loops, analogous to prohibiting predatory-lending clauses without measuring borrower sadness). CON's grief/streaming comparison also never recovered from PRO's counter that you can walk away from a TV show without emotional cost in a way you cannot walk away from a simulated partner. PRO's originality in framing (trust vs. transaction, wallets vs. minds) and sharper answers to the pressure round gave it a clear but not dominant margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q1 ("Unfair-trade rules stop hidden fees; they do not stop an algorithm from simulating a breakup to spur spending… You cannot walk away from a relationship without emotional cost. That asymmetry is the gap duty-of-care fills") was the most decisive exchange. It concretely distinguished financial-fraud coverage from relational engineering, directly answered the structural pressure CON placed on PRO's gap claim, and CON never produced a specific statutory counter-example that covered the exact mechanism PRO described.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): Side B wins: it established a live gap between transactional consumer law and relational manipulation that CON never closed, with stronger weighing on vulnerability and consent; CON's equivalences to entertainment were damaged by B's interactivity rebuttals and direct answers to pressure questions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 answers to B's pressure Qs, showing the statutory gap on simulated intimacy and defining objective harmful patterns without subjective overreach.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): PRO established a clean categorical distinction between transactional entertainment and relational AI companions, arguing that existing consumer law protects wallets while leaving a gap for psychological exploitation. CON countered that dark-pattern and unfair-trade statutes already cover emotional manipulation, but never fully overcame the asymmetry of parasocial bonding—PRO showed that when a product simulates a relationship, the harm lies in the design architecture itself, not merely in deceptive billing. PRO’s rebuttals were sharper and more memorable, crystallizing the clash around consent-bypassing mechanisms and the “product is connection” frame, while CON’s analogies to streaming services weakened as the debate progressed. PRO’s closing most cleanly sealed the conceptual gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s first rebuttal dismantling of the agency argument with the line “You cannot consent freely to a mechanism designed to bypass consent” and the distinction that “AI companions sell relationships where the user is the product, not the customer” decisively reframed the debate away from consumer choice toward exploitation of vulnerability.
