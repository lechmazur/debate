### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but PRO (B) did more to resolve the decisive clash. CON's (A) strongest weapon was the "chilling effect" argument: mandatory floors will make organizations stop signing marginal/developmental players, especially minors, leaving them with worse informal alternatives. PRO blunted this by (1) reframing the motion as demanding enforceable targeted floors rather than a maximalist global labor code, which is a defensible reading of "stronger protections" as written, and (2) landing a sharp catch in Rebuttal 2 — noting that CON's own proposal to make transparency and review "enforceable when tied to league licensing, publisher sanctions, visa sponsorship, or prize payouts" [A2.4] already concedes the enforcement mechanism PRO needs, without requiring a new global bureaucracy. CON's response to this (that mandatory publisher enforcement is either revocable/private or itself the sweeping mandate) is a reasonable dilemma but did not fully escape the sense that CON's own remedy already looks like what PRO is defending. PRO's closing line reframing the "opportunity" being destroyed as "the opportunity to be exploited" was the more memorable and functionally load-bearing resolution of the exclusion-cost debate, though CON's rebuttal that removing the formal contract pushes players into worse unregulated alternatives remained a live, unresolved point rather than a fully answered one. Pressure questions were sharp on both sides, but PRO's questions extracted a more damaging concession from CON, and PRO's answers (A1/A2) engaged that concession head-on rather than dodging it. Overall a close, competent debate, but PRO's narrowing and use of CON's own concessions gives it a clear, if not dominant, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 catch that CON's own proposed remedy (transparency/review enforced via league licensing, sanctions, visas, and prize payouts) already concedes the enforcement mechanism PRO needs, forcing CON into an awkward dilemma between calling it private/revocable or admitting it is the sweeping mandate it claims to reject.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A won because the proposition requires stronger protections, not a single uniform global labor code. Side B’s concession that enforceable minor-specific review, education, medical, and grievance safeguards are desirable substantially conceded the motion. Its opportunity-loss argument was plausible but depended on treating the motion as an indiscriminate package of wage and roster mandates that Side A never advocated. Side A also supplied a credible enforcement mechanism through access-controlling publishers and leagues.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions directly, showed that targeted enforceable safeguards still qualify as stronger labor protections, and turned Side B’s league-licensing proposal into a concession that both enforcement levers and beneficial protections already exist.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully argued that exploitative formal contracts function as legal traps rather than genuine opportunities, dismantling CON's core claim that any contract is better than informal play. PRO also effectively turned CON's own proposed enforcement mechanisms (publishers and leagues) into a realistic vehicle for the motion's stronger protections.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized CON's chilling effect argument by pointing out that if a team won't sign a minor due to basic rest and medical requirements, the contract's only value was the abuse.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, escalating cases, but CON's "relocation" thesis—that raising compliance costs on the visible, accountable formal sector pushes vulnerable young players into wholly unregulated informal circuits—was pressed consistently from opening through closing and never fully neutralized. PRO's strongest counter, that publisher/league gatekeeping (roster eligibility, licensing, prize payouts) offers a more realistic enforcement lever than courts, was a genuinely clever pivot in Rebuttal 2, but CON directly answered it by noting gatekeeping only governs what publishers already control and cannot touch the informal pipeline, producing a "two-tier system." PRO's final answer—that fewer exploitative formal offers "is the point"—is a values reframe rather than a rebuttal of the total-exploitation metric, and it concedes the practical mechanism CON attacked. CON's closing "risk laundering" framing crystallized this gap memorably, while PRO's "quality vs. quantity" framing, though reasonable, left the empirical relocation concern unresolved. Pressure questions were well-used by both sides with proper A1/A2 formatting, but CON's answers integrated more cleanly into its throughline (fragmentation → enforcement capacity → relocation) than PRO's did.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's publisher-gatekeeping enforcement mechanism ([A4.6]), distinguishing between what leagues can control (formal registration) and what they cannot (the informal pipeline), directly undercut PRO's strongest structural claim and set up the decisive "two-tier system" framing carried into the closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won a competitive debate by supplying the clearer preventive mechanism: publishers and leagues can condition roster registration, tournament access, and payouts on contract review, minor safeguards, and rest standards. Side B made a strong displacement argument, but largely asserted that compliance costs would drive enough players underground to increase total exploitation. Its preferred enforcement of existing law also did not solve the informal-sector problem it emphasized. A therefore better established that imperfect coverage is not a reason to preserve abuse within the sector that can be governed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest response distinguished reactive generic law from preventive league gatekeeping: refusing registration of an abusive contract is more feasible than requiring a teenager to litigate after harm. This directly weakened B’s claim that new protections necessarily depend on the same ineffective enforcement structure.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON wins by effectively turning PRO's core mechanism against them. While PRO convincingly argued that publisher gatekeeping could enforce structural protections in the formal sector, CON persuasively demonstrated that shrinking the formal sector would not reduce the total number of young players seeking to go pro, but merely push them into the unregulated informal circuits that PRO conceded were worse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal and closing, which framed PRO's policy as "risk laundering" that decreases the number of supervised teenagers by shifting them into the unregulated pipeline.
