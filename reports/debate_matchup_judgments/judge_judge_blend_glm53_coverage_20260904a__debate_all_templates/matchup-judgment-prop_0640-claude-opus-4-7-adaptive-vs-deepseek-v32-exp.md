### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's causal links and demonstrating that the cap targets a symptom rather than the disease. B effectively proved that A's identified harms (price signaling, profit maximization) apply to any premium buyer, and that B's targeted regulations would be easier to enforce on highly visible institutional fiduciaries than on the local consolidating farms that would otherwise buy the land.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 completely neutralized A's strongest point by showing that A's "thin market comparable" mechanism applies to any wealthy buyer, and correctly noted that visible pension funds are easier to sue than local shadow LLCs.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): Side A wins by making the decisive clash concrete: the cap redirects land to less accountable buyers while destroying exit values and leasing paths for family operations, with superior enforcement analysis showing institutions are easier to regulate than anonymous successors. Side B never answers where the land actually goes post-cap and relies on overstated mechanisms that A consistently neutralizes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers directly then flips the enforcement paradox and shows PRO's cap merely swaps one premium bidder class for the real driver of consolidation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): CON demonstrated that the cap mis-targets the actual drivers of rural consolidation and food-system vulnerability, while imposing real costs on retiring farmers and beginning-farmer leasing pathways. CON's sharpest turn was flipping PRO's enforcement argument: institutional investors are highly visible and auditable, whereas capping them redirects land toward opaque family LLCs that are harder to regulate. PRO never overcame the decisive "where does the land go" frame, relying instead on unsourced specifics and a price-signaling mechanism that applies to any premium buyer. CON's toolkit approach offered a more coherent, less distorting path to the same stated goals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's reversal of the enforcement paradox in Rebuttal 2—arguing that pension funds and institutional owners are the most regulable actors in agriculture, so displacing them toward shell-LLC family operations makes stewardship covenants harder, not easier, to enforce.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.7 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by exposing a critical internal tension in Side A's case: institutional investors cannot be simultaneously too small to matter for retiring farmers' exit values yet dominant enough to drive massive regional price inflation. Side B's substitution argument ("where does the land go the day after the cap passes?") is decisive, showing that the cap would simply redirect land to consolidating neighboring farms operating through opaque LLCs, which are harder to regulate than auditable pension funds. Side B effectively flips Side A's enforcement argument and provides a targeted toolkit that addresses the actual harms (soil degradation, concentration, beginner lockout) without the collateral damage of a blunt ownership cap. Side A's responses rely on romanticized assumptions about family farm stewardship and fail to resolve the substitution problem, leaving Side B with the cleaner, more logically consistent path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which dismantled Side A's pricing mechanism by showing that any premium buyer sets regional comps, and flipped the enforcement argument by highlighting that regulated pension funds are easier to police than the anonymous LLC structures that would absorb the capped land.
