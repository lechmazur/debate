### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that police query profiles voluntarily opted into matching, use relatives only as leads, and independently confirm the suspect. Side A identified a genuine privacy concern but never established that genetic inference constitutes a search of every relative or that tailored safeguards are inadequate enough to justify a strict ban. Its categorical claims about warrants and regulatory failure were also insufficiently grounded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B decisively turned Side A's shared-home analogy: a relative cannot authorize a bedroom search but can give police information pointing toward someone. Combined with the CODIS comparison, this undermined Side A's claim that an unknown-suspect database query is inherently a general search.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully deconstructing Side A's core Fourth Amendment argument, convincingly demonstrating that non-consenting relatives are not actually 'searched' or sampled. Side B effectively flips Side A's analogies and weighs concrete justice for victims against an abstract privacy impact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 flip of the 'bedroom' analogy—pointing out that while a brother cannot authorize a search of your bedroom, he can tell police where you live, perfectly mirroring how IGG functions as a lead rather than a search.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B wins by systematically dismantling Side A's core analogies. Side A relied heavily on comparing investigative genetic genealogy (IGG) to eyewitness tips, CODIS trawls, and wiretap regulations. Side B sharply distinguished IGG from CODIS (noting CODIS relies on direct, full-profile matches of offenders, whereas IGG exploits partial matches of civilian relatives) and effectively turned Side A's wiretap analogy against them in the closing. Side B pointed out that wiretap frameworks exist precisely because the underlying act is a search, and that such regulatory frameworks historically fail to prevent the exact mission creep Side A dismissed. While Side A delivered strong emotional appeals regarding cold-case victims and argued that relatives suffer no physical intrusion, Side B maintained a tighter logical grip on the structural nature of the genetic dragnet and the inadequacy of uploader consent to cover non-consenting networks. Side B's direct, precise answers to pressure questions further solidified their advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of the CODIS and wiretap analogies, particularly in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, where they proved IGG is structurally distinct from direct database matching and that proposing a wiretap-style regulatory framework inherently concedes the practice constitutes a general search prone to expansion.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B won the motion’s categorical burden. Side A compellingly explained why genetic matching is unusually intimate and affects nonconsenting relatives, but did not establish that serious-case limits, judicial authorization, minimization, and deletion must inevitably fail. Side B therefore preserved a credible regulated alternative while weighing it against the concrete loss of a uniquely useful tool for grave cold cases. The clipped opening and loose warrant analogy kept the result competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest response was that mission creep supports enforceable fences rather than field abandonment. Side A answered mainly with nonconsent and a prediction that limits would erode, but never decisively showed why tightly confined access could not reduce the privacy harm enough to defeat an absolute ban.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's legal framework. While Side A powerfully argued that DNA is uniquely intimate and collective, Side B successfully demonstrated that probable cause attaches to the crime under investigation, not to intermediate leads, and that traditional police work routinely implicates non-consenting relatives. Side B's push for strict regulation over categorical prohibition provided a logically consistent and pragmatic resolution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal decisively neutralized the core of Side A's case by clarifying how probable cause functions and pointing out that demanding consent from every relative touched by a lead would nullify standard investigative techniques.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully reframing the debate around proportionality and regulation versus absolute prohibition, and by delivering a decisive reductio ad absurdum in Rebuttal 2. Side A effectively demonstrated that Side B's theory of "collective" privacy rights would invalidate standard, accepted investigative techniques like DMV lookups and phone record subpoenas, which also implicate non-consenting relatives. Side B's rhetorical polish was exceptional, but they failed to adequately defend their core Fourth Amendment claim against Side A's analogies, and their insistence on prohibition even with perfect safeguards made their position seem dogmatic. Side A's minor clipping in the opening did not materially damage their comprehensive case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they applied Side B's "collective consent" theory to traditional investigative tools (DMV lookups, phone subpoenas) to show its incoherence, effectively neutralizing Side B's primary legal argument.
