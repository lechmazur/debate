- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that self-classification does not resolve either vague wellness claims or the interpretive enforcement problem.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 most decisively answered B's pressure: brands use vague language to avoid substantiation, not classification, while enforcement can protect teenagers without requiring them to decode a taxonomy. The fraud-law analogy also cleanly explained why case-specific adjudication can be workable even when universal pre-classification is not.

PRO argues wellness-weasel words exploit a gap in the medical/cosmetic binary and that forcing a category tag extracts a confession upstream. CON shows the gap is substantiation, not categorization: a compliant "wellness claim: supports barrier" remains vague but gains a regulatory stamp, and catching a mismatch still requires proving implied efficacy — the same work deception law already does. CON's pressure about parasocial trust (A1.4 vs A2.7) and its A2 rebuttal that enforcement makes teens beneficiaries while tags make them instruments — directly contradicting A1.5 — is never cleanly resolved, and A's decisive second rebuttal is clipped, leaving administration (young-audience threshold, ephemeral surfaces) under-defended. The closing laundering-vs-confession trade favors CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 A2 — prosecution protects teens without asking them to decode taxonomy, so the label makes the child the instrument of protection contrary to PRO's own incapacity premise, plus paid-tag feasibility example is also its failure example.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side A (CON) built a decisive and highly persuasive case that the labeling mandate would function as a compliance shield rather than a consumer protection tool. CON brilliantly argued that sophisticated brands would "harmonize downward" into vague wellness categories, effectively laundering their ambiguous claims with a regulatory stamp. When PRO attempted to reframe this as a victory, CON's closing crushed the argument by pointing out that this "confession" requires a 14-year-old to decode legal taxonomies—directly contradicting PRO's foundational premise about youth vulnerability. CON also cleanly resolved PRO's contradiction trap by distinguishing the scale of targeted enforcement from universal pre-sorting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing exchange over the "harmonize downward" scenario. PRO tried to claim that forcing a wellness tag strips the claim of medical authority, but CON devastatingly countered that it merely launders the ambiguity, provides a compliance defense for the seller, and improperly shifts the burden of interpretation onto the child.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won a competitive debate by separating blurry products from the specific claims sellers choose, defending special protection for parasocially influenced young audiences, and weighing a modest disclosure burden against substantial consumer confusion. Side A raised a serious chilling-effect concern and effectively challenged the supplement analogy, but overstated that the existing supplement regime is identical to an explicit in-content classification requirement and did not show that imperfect disclosure would be worse than leaving the ambiguity intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's claim-versus-product response was decisive: biological overlap does not make clear statements such as “supports appearance” and “treats acne” indistinguishable, while marginal cases do not defeat disclosure for easy and harmful cases. This substantially weakened Side A's foundational impossibility argument.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): PRO won the weighing. A established a vulnerable audience, concrete harm (barrier damage, wellness gummies, delayed care), and a low-cost remedy, then held the claim/product line: sentences can be sorted even if biology blurs. CON's strongest hit – supplement labeling as failed model – landed and was never fully repaired, but B's own distinction between binary back-end enforcement and ternary front-end mandate softened PRO's contradiction charge without proving the remedy useless. PRO's closer that sorting happens either way and that even defensive vagueness labeled "wellness" gives the child the kind of promise – vs the child becoming a regulator – gave the cleaner reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1/R2 turn of the supplement analogy – showing "supports immunity" is already a front-end label that coexists with implied disease claims – which directly undercut PRO's model and forced PRO onto a front-end vs back-end repair defense.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side A won decisively by flipping Side B's own supplement analogy. When Side B cited the supplement regime as a model for workable categories, Side A successfully reframed it as a cautionary tale of failure, proving that front-end labels like "supports immunity" do not stop consumers from inferring medical benefits. Side B's attempt to distinguish their rule as "front-end" versus "back-end" enforcement collapsed when Side A pointed out that supplement labels are already front-end disclosures. Side A's closing crystallized this clash perfectly, leaving Side B without a live route to victory on the mechanism's effectiveness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side B's "front-end vs back-end" distinction regarding the supplement regime, proving that stamping a category label on a product does not prevent audiences from inferring medical benefits, thus turning Side B's own model into proof of the proposition's failure.
