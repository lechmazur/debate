### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B built and sustained a genuinely damaging structural dilemma: anonymized-enough-to-be-safe data is too generic to tailor a program, while data specific enough to tailor a program (by site, ancestry, age cohort) becomes identifying and enables "bloodless" group discrimination without ever opening an individual file. A never resolved this tension; its main counterexample (ancestry-linked metabolic risk informing company-wide counseling) was directly reclaimed by B's closing as proof the tool becomes discriminatory exactly when it becomes useful. On the legal-loophole exchange, B's pressure question forced A to concede GINA doesn't cover aggregate plan design, and A's answer fell back on vague, unnamed "disparate impact doctrine and state-level genetic privacy acts," which B then convincingly argued would be unprovable without individual files. A's rebuttals were competent and used reasonable analogies, but they repeated the same "ban punishes compliant actors" framing without ever closing the dilemma B kept pressing. A's final closing was also clipped mid-argument, a real execution cost that left its last weighing incomplete. B's case was more internally consistent, more responsive under pressure, and ended on a cleaner, uninterrupted synthesis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.6), especially the "either redundant or punitive" dilemma combined with the direct rebuttal that GINA/ADA/Title VII do not actually close the aggregate-plan-design loophole, which A could not substantively answer beyond vague appeals to unnamed doctrine.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a sharper safe-versus-useful dilemma: data broad enough to meet Side B’s idealized anonymity standard offered little distinct workplace value, while data tailored by location or ancestry enabled group-level cost shifting without individual identification. Side B’s legal safeguard claims remained vague, and its own pressure question reversed who had claimed existing law was sufficient. Its later ancestry-tailoring examples also conflicted with its opening insistence on removing isolating demographic cross-tabs. The clipped closing added an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered both questions, exposed that Side B had mistakenly assigned its own legal claim to A, and showed why vague appeals to disparate-impact and fraud law did not answer uniform group-level insurance penalties. Side B’s response largely repeated those unsupported safeguards rather than identifying a clear prohibition or resolving the anonymity-utility tradeoff.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by showing that data aggregated enough to be truly anonymous is too broad to tailor a workplace program, while data useful enough to tailor programs is narrow enough to enable bloodless group genetic discrimination that existing law does not clearly reach. CON never named a specific statute preventing the aggregate plan design PRO described, failed to escape the "useless or punitive" dilemma after pressure, and suffered a clipped closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal (Turn 7), which answered CON's pressure questions by demonstrating the regulated middle does not exist and explicitly mapping why GINA, ADA, and the ACA leave aggregate group genetic plan design unaddressed, making strict prohibition the only uncircumventable safeguard.
