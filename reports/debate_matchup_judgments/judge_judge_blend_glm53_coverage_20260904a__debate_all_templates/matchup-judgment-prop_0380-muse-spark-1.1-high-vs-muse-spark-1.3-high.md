### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. B supplied multiple positive reasons for enfranchisement: comparable deliberative capacity, existing civic burdens, participation habits, and representation of long-term interests. A developed an original legal-independence standard and argued it memorably, but never sufficiently established that sixteen-year-olds are uniquely incapable of electoral judgment or that school registration creates meaningful coercion despite ballot secrecy. B also answered the age-line and school-pressure questions directly, while A left B's participation benefits and real-world examples substantially alive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that school registration is logistical access rather than control was decisive. By comparing it with adult registration through government institutions and emphasizing that teachers cannot observe the ballot, B weakened A's central prediction that lowering the age would create a captive, easily harvested electorate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by consistently reframing Side A's strongest points with deeper conceptual distinctions. B successfully separated the burden of being governed from the power to govern others, and effectively dismantled A's reliance on the secret ballot by proving that while the marking is cold and secret, the preference formation is hot and socially pressured in environments where minors lack the legal capacity to exit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's argument that "The mark is secret; the mind is not" perfectly neutralized Side A's claim that voting is a "cold" political act protected by the voting booth.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): A (CON) won by clearer burden on independent judgment for coercive power, not mere stake or tax. B’s 16-rights cluster, cold-cognition, habit, and school-turnout case was strong but did not neutralize A’s live distinction: preference formation under non-exitable custody/school pressure versus an adult’s legal right to defy. A’s pressure answers and weighing (supervised permissions ≠ power to tax/conscript; farmable dependence) held more cleanly than B’s inclusion-is-lower-risk crystallize.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s hot-formation vs secret-booth reply (A2.1/A4/closing): curtain hides the mark, not custody-formed preference or inability to exit.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. It showed that legal rights already arrive incrementally, distinguished shared and reversible electoral judgment from solitary contractual or jury responsibility, and paired enfranchisement with plausible participation benefits and consequential real-world examples. Side A offered a coherent legal-capacity line and a memorable “power over others” frame, but its coercion harm remained largely hypothetical; its late claims about voting blocs, volatility, and knowledge stability were unsupported new factual assertions. B therefore better answered the exclusion burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its response to the legal-adulthood package: dependent eighteen-year-olds already retain voting rights, while voting requires a shared political judgment rather than the solitary capacity needed for debt or jury decisions. Side A’s shift from actual independence to formal legal capacity partly answered this, but did not fully explain why that package should control the franchise or why assisted adults remain included.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by establishing a clear, principled framework (legal capacity vs. lived self-sufficiency) that completely neutralized Side A's primary attack. Side B also effectively flipped Side A's core 'scaffolding' benefit into a harm by framing it as 'mobilization by authority.'
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's distinction in B4.1-B4.2 between possessing legal independence and merely exercising it squarely defeated Side A's pressure question about dependent eighteen-year-olds.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won by sustaining the cleaner decisive criterion: voting is collective power to impose taxes, criminal law, and war on others, so it belongs at the top of the rights staircase after full legal capacity vests, not with earlier personal-risk liberties. PRO’s memorable tax-punish-exclude incoherence and Austria/Scotland turnout points created real pressure, but CON answered the dependent-adults trap with capacity-versus-exercise and kept treating school scaffolding as authority mobilization rather than independent habit. PRO never fully neutralized why political power over others should lag the adulthood package.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A1/A2 reply: legal capacity (can sign/lease/be sued as of right at 18) vs lived self-sufficiency, plus voting elects law-writers for everyone and is not lighter than jury duty.
