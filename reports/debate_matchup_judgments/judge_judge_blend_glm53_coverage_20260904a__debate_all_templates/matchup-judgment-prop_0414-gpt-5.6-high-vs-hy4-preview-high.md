### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON's institutional critique proved more durable across the exchange. CON's fungibility/substitution argument, sharpened by the "dilution vs. dependence" reframe and the volatility/perverse-incentive points, forced PRO to bolt on maintenance-of-effort rules and reserves to make earmarking work. CON then landed the decisive blow in Rebuttal 2: those patches concede that the earmark itself does no independent work—the enforcement comes from external legal rules that could equally protect resident priorities inside a general fund. PRO never answered why earmarking specifically (rather than a spending mandate applicable to any revenue source) was necessary, leaving its "first claim" framing rhetorically appealing but structurally hollow. Both closings were clipped for length, but PRO's cutoff landed mid-comparison on its central claim, while CON's complete closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("cure financed by disease," "budget by need, not receipt"). CON's pressure questions also extracted a useful concession (that no fiscal rule is truly irreversible) that CON exploited effectively afterward, while PRO's questions were answered but didn't reshape the debate. Overall CON provided the more resilient, mechanism-level case and better closed the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point (B4.1/B4.5) that PRO's maintenance-of-effort and reserve fixes prove the earmark itself is inert—since external legal constraints, not the lockbox, do the real protective work—directly undercut PRO's central mechanism and went largely unanswered before the debate ended.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully defending its model against Side B's practical attacks (fungibility and volatility) and by winning the comparative weighing on accountability.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 dismantled the 'perverse incentive' argument by pointing out that a housing agency would welcome a rental cap if it directly returns homes to residents, showing that the goal is reducing net harm rather than just maximizing a revenue stream.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): The decisive thread was CON's fungibility/opportunity-cost critique, pressed consistently from the opening through closing. PRO's opening and early rebuttals were vivid (polluter-pays framing, concrete city examples, "give the city back" refrain), but once CON forced the question of how earmarking survives substitution, PRO had to build an increasingly elaborate mechanism (maintenance-of-effort baseline, audits, supermajority overrides, sunsets, repayment). CON's pressure question exposed the core dilemma cleanly (Q1 in Turn 6), and PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer, while substantive, was then turned back on itself in CON's final rebuttal: if the baseline is adjustable, substitution returns; if fixed, it blocks legitimate reallocation. PRO's closing tried to resolve this by saying the baseline is "adjustable, not frozen," but this concession essentially restates the dilemma without escaping it, and CON's closing line ("pricing a harm and allocating public money are separate decisions") crystallized the unresolved tension memorably. CON's case progressed more cleanly across rounds, converting each PRO patch into a fresh problem, while PRO's mechanism grew more complex without ultimately closing the gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.5–B4.8), which reframed PRO's new maintenance-of-effort mechanism as a dilemma (adjustable baseline permits substitution; fixed baseline creates rigidity), combined with PRO's closing concession that the baseline is "adjustable," which failed to escape that trap and let CON's closing land the final blow.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B wins by systematically trapping Side A in a dilemma between fungibility and rigidity. When Side A introduces complex rules like maintenance-of-effort and supermajority overrides to prevent substitution, Side B expertly leverages this to prove that the earmark would dangerously obstruct ordinary, non-emergency budgeting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4's dismantling of A's "maintenance-of-effort" rule, pointing out that an adjustable baseline permits substitution, while a fixed one ignores changing needs, and highlighting the immense bureaucratic complexity needed to save the earmark.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON maintained a live route: opportunity cost, volatility mismatch, and fungibility. PRO's answer – supplement-not-supplant with baselines, reserves, supermajority override – conceded rigidity and complexity, which CON turned into a dilemma: if adjustable, substitution returns; if fixed, ordinary non-emergency needs like water, fire, health are blocked. PRO never resolved that long-term housing/transit costs persist when tourism falls, nor why pricing harm must dictate spending.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2's dilemma that PRO's maintenance-of-effort floor must be either adjustable (ineffective) or fixed (harmfully rigid), plus the audit-can't-prove-value and emergency-override-doesn't-cover-ordinary-tradeoffs extension, which collapsed A's fungibility fix.
