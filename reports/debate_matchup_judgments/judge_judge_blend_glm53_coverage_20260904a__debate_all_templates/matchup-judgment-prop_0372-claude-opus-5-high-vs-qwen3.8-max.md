### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): A won narrowly by establishing a clear precautionary asymmetry: plausible developmental harm from a nonessential additive outweighs the limited cost of removing it. B offered sharper rhetoric and valuable institutional concerns, but leaned too heavily on speculative substitution harms and overstated how easily banned dyes would evade state sales rules. A also complied more cleanly with the format, while two B speeches were clipped.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange concerned substitution. B effectively exposed A’s reliance on federal guardrails, but A preserved its case by noting that a targeted ban mandates no particular replacement and can instead produce omission or reformulation. B never established that replacement risks were comparable to the accumulating evidence against the listed dyes.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON won by showing that the state ban does not escape dependence on slow federal processes: PRO conceded that substitute colorants are held to safety by existing federal food-adulteration rules, so the protection against replacement risks still ships on "federal time." CON also demonstrated that bans are porous (interstate commerce), hard to reverse (no examples of repealed state additive bans), and institutionally brittle (legislatures voting on contested toxicology), while state alternatives such as labels, procurement, and funding studies reduce exposure without those costs. PRO's precautionary frame remained live but could not overcome the substitution and reversibility burdens, and the closing attempt to minimize the cost of error as "losing a cosmetic colorant" was directly rebutted by showing that food stays colored with less-examined substitutes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal [B4.5-B4.8], where CON exposed that PRO's safeguard against dangerous substitutes is federal food law—the very institution the ban exists to bypass—and crystallized the clash as "speed purchased by removing the error-correction" versus "exposure reduction that survives being wrong."
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): PRO sustained the core justification route – nonessential cosmetic additive + plausible developmental risk + state power to reduce exposure now – and distinguished CON's fluoridation/vaccine slippery slope. CON landed sharp hits on substitution and reversibility and was more memorable rhetorically, but paid clear execution costs for two clipped turns and never fully neutralized the PRO asymmetry: if PRO is wrong society loses a color, if CON is wrong children keep eating a suspected dye through school years. That weighing plus cleaner burden handling gives PRO the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's B4.5-B4.7: that PRO's substitute guardrail is the slow federal food law the ban existed to bypass, and that the sunset/error-correction mechanism was imagined at the podium with no named reversal.
