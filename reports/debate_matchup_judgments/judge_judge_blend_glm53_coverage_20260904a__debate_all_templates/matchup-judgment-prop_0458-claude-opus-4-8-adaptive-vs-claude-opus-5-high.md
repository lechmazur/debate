- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON won because it kept the motion’s two metrics live and forced PRO into decisive concessions: restriction slows domestic gas retirement, and the short-run substitute for withheld US gas is dirtier suppliers or coal, not clean power.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s [B4.5–B4.6] parasitic lock-in turn: terminals cause climate harm only if demand is real, in which case restriction reroutes it to worse suppliers/coal.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO)): CON won both metrics in the motion. PRO never proved that restriction would cause mass demand destruction rather than substitution. On domestic decarbonization, PRO conceded that restricting exports would collapse domestic gas prices and slow clean-energy buildout, banking instead on future political will—a concession that undermined its own opening. PRO’s strongest argument, infrastructure lock-in, was analytically neutralized by CON’s “parasitic on substitution” rebuttal: a terminal only emits if there is demand, and if demand exists restriction merely reroutes supply. PRO’s retreat to political lobbying in the closing, while eloquent, effectively admitted the atmospheric case was weak. CON maintained cleaner internal consistency and sharper comparative weighing throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 argument that lock-in is “parasitic on substitution” [A4.5–6], showing that a terminal emits nothing on its own and only causes emissions if foreign demand is real—at which point restriction reroutes supply rather than preventing it, while if demand is not real the terminal becomes a stranded asset. This dissolved PRO’s central “concrete poured today” harm and exposed that PRO’s two remaining claims were mutually dependent in a way that left no independent terminal harm.

Side B was forced to concede this point and later admit that restriction would slow domestic gas retirement. Side A also effectively used the 2022 energy crisis to demonstrate that high global gas prices lead to coal burning rather than immediate renewable buildout, neutralizing Side B's elasticity argument. While Side B's remaining argument about infrastructure lock-in was strong, Side A's counter that cheap domestic gas builds a larger, more diffuse anti-climate consumer constituency was a sharp and persuasive reframing. Side A maintained better logical consistency and controlled the core clashes throughout the debate.
