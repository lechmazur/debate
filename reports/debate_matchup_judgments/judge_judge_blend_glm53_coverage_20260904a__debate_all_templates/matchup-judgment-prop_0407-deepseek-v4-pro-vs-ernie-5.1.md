### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won on the key clashes. First, it cleanly turned Side A's teacher-at-racist-rally example: because that conduct materially damages the school's mission, it falls squarely within the exception the proposition already contains—proving the filter works rather than undermining it. Side A never recovered from this turn, and its closing attempt to reframe the example as a "trap" (schools must keep the extremist or risk a lawsuit) was asserted without development. Second, Side B's burden-shifting framework—employee makes a prima facie showing, then the employer who holds the relevant information bears the burden of demonstrating concrete harm—was coherently developed across rebuttals and directly answered Side A's "prove a negative" concern. Side A's counter (discovery still costs small employers money) is true of all employment litigation and not uniquely damaging here. Third, Side A's floodgates argument was put in genuine jeopardy by Side B's observation that similar "material impact" and "legitimate business justification" standards already operate in retaliation and discrimination law without the predicted paralysis. Side A's only response was that the scope is "orders of magnitude larger," but this was asserted rather than demonstrated; it also ignores the natural filter that the employee must first show the firing was causally linked to the off-duty conduct. Fourth, Side B's normative core—the employment bargain should be about labour, not the purchase of a worker's off-duty identity—was vivid, consistently maintained, and never seriously answered by Side A, which treated at-will flexibility as self-evidently sufficient. Side B was penalised for two transcript clips (a real execution cost), and Side A's point about discrimination law protecting defined statutory classes versus any lawful activity is a genuine distinction, but not sufficient to reverse the overall balance. Side B made the debate easier to decide by steadily narrowing the live clash and providing workable answers to the strongest CON pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's turn on the teacher example in Rebuttal 1 [B2.1–B2.2]: rather than conceding the case, Side B showed that the teacher's off-duty activism causes demonstrable reputational and mission harm that satisfies the "material effect" exception—so the firing is permissible under the very standard PRO advocates. This converted Side A's centrepiece illustration into evidence for the filter's functionality, and Side A never cleanly answered it in subsequent rounds.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by successfully turning Side B's core example (the racist teacher) into a demonstration of why the proposition's 'material impact' filter works. Side B was forced to retreat from defending employer rights to arguing solely about litigation costs and administrability, which Side A handled well via burden-shifting analogies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal deftly flipped the teacher example, proving that the proposition already accounts for reputational harm and neutralized Side B's main substantive objection.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won by showing that PRO's "material impact" filter, while workable in narrow statutory contexts like discrimination law, becomes speculative and litigation-intensive when stretched across all lawful off-duty conduct. CON's rebuttals and closing crystallized that the proposition's vagueness creates defensive hiring and litigation traps that harm the very workers it claims to protect, and this practical burden was never fully dispelled.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's dismantling of PRO's court analogy by distinguishing the narrow, concrete anchors of discrimination law from the unbounded universe of "any lawful off-duty conduct," which undermined PRO's core claim that the standard is judicially manageable.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON's decisive move was a two-pronged paradox about the material-effect test: if early indicators like complaint logs and booking dips count as "material effect," the standard is so porous that employers can still fire workers for off-duty conduct whenever they can solicit or generate complaints—making the protection hollow; if those indicators do not count, employers must absorb documented but not-yet-metric harm, which is the concrete injury CON identified throughout. PRO never cleanly resolved this dilemma. PRO's answer in B4.3 that courts "already accept leading-indicator data in discrimination cases" does not address the risk that those same indicators essentially allow the status quo to continue under a different label. CON also landed the sharper pressure questions: A3's Q1 caught a real inconsistency in PRO's litigation-risk argument—the state-law evidence PRO relied on (California, New York, Colorado) is valid precisely because those laws retain business-interest carve-outs that PRO's proposal would strip, making the low-litigation comparison inapplicable. PRO's A1 response in B4 muddled the distinction between a higher evidentiary bar and the absence of a carve-out without cleanly resolving the gap. PRO's strengths—execution discipline (no clipped turns), consistent framing, and the nurse example—kept it competitive, and CON paid a real execution cost with two clipped turns. But the live, unanswered paradox and the caught inconsistency on state-law evidence gave CON the cleaner path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [A4.1] catching that PRO's own state-law evidence depends on business-interest exceptions the proposal removes, followed by CON's closing crystallization of the paradox: early indicators either collapse the material-effect test into status-quo discretion or, if excluded, force employers to absorb compounding harm before acting. PRO's B4.3 response acknowledged early indicators count but never addressed how that concession re-opens the door to the arbitrary firings the motion was meant to prevent.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B wins by successfully deconstructing Side A's 'material impact' standard and exposing a fatal dilemma. B shows that if A's standard includes indirect harms like complaints or shift-swap refusals, it permits the culture-policing A opposes; if it excludes them, employers are left paralyzed. Furthermore, B effectively dismantles A's empirical state-law comparison by pointing out the missing business-interest exceptions, a point A defends with convoluted logic. Finally, A completely drops B's compelling argument that this policy would lead to invasive defensive screening of applicants.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing brilliantly crystallizes the double-bind on the definition of 'material effect,' showing that A's concessions either collapse the protection entirely or force businesses to absorb catastrophic, unmanageable harm.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by exposing a fatal structural tension in PRO's proposal: the "material effect" test either captures legitimate business harms like team friction and patient complaints—in which case it collapses into the very culture-policing it claims to ban—or it excludes them, forcing employers to wait for metric-driven damage. This dilemma survived PRO's rebuttals. Moreover, PRO's empirical appeal to low-litigation states backfired when CON noted those laws contain business-interest carve-outs that PRO's broader proposal omits. PRO's response in B4.1 muddled the burden of proof and failed to explain why removing employer safe harbors would not increase litigation. While PRO offered a compelling rights-based frame, CON's practical critique proved more durable after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's turning of PRO's state-law evidence in A2.3/A4.1, showing that existing protections work precisely because they include business-interest exceptions that PRO's proposal strips away, leaving PRO without credible empirical support for their low-litigation prediction.
