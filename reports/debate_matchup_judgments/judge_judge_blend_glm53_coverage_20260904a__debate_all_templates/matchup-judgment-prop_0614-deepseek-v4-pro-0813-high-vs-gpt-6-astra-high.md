### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): The decisive clash centered on whether B's "conditional access, external control" exception could survive contact with the reality that spyware must operate on devices and networks inside the purchasing state. A's pressure question (Q1) directly targeted this: what stops a non-democratic regime from copying, reverse-engineering, or compelling the vendor once the tool is inside its borders? B's answer conceded the risk ("copying and reverse-engineering remain genuine risks... refuse the transaction") without ever explaining how independent technical review could actually verify containment for a tool that must touch regime-controlled infrastructure. A's closing exploited this concession cleanly, arguing the exception collapses into de facto refusal or bureaucratic transfer of a reusable capability — exactly the outcome the motion is meant to prevent. B's closing fell back to "if no transaction meets the safeguards, our rule authorizes none," which effectively concedes that in practice its alternative converges toward the ban it opposes, undercutting its own case for a categorical distinction. A's rebuttals were sharper and more mechanistic throughout, and its use of the pressure round did real work exposing a structural weakness rather than a cosmetic one. B built a genuinely thoughtful alternative policy and pressed real proxy-versus-scrutiny objections, but never closed the containment gap A kept hammering. A's rebuttal 1 was clipped for length, a real execution cost, but the lost material was mostly summary rather than substantive, so it only modestly tempers an otherwise clear edge for A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Q1 (copying/reverse-engineering once inside regime borders) and B's partial concession in Rebuttal 2 that such risk requires case-by-case refusal, which A then converted into a decisive closing argument that B's exception collapses in practice.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A successfully dismantled Side B's alternative of 'vendor-controlled' licensing by demonstrating that external control is technically impossible when the payload must interact with regime-controlled infrastructure, effectively forcing Side B's policy to collapse into either a de facto ban or a dangerous loophole.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Closing was decisive, crystallizing how the technical reality of spyware interacting with local chokepoints renders Side B's proposed independent containment safeguards physically impossible.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins by showing that the structural conditions of non-democratic regimes—no independent courts, free press, or oversight—make the CON’s conditional licensing regime illusory in practice. A grounded the debate in real-world abuse patterns and provided a compelling mechanism showing that once a tool must interact with local devices and networks, the regime controls the chokepoints, enabling reverse engineering, coercion, or repurposing. This collapsed B’s exceptions into either de facto refusal or bureaucratic error. B’s licensing alternative was logically coherent but remained hypothetical and could not overcome the decisive structural critique that vendor control cannot survive inside a closed regime.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s argument that “vendor control abroad is a promise, not a property right” and that closed regimes control local chokepoints, exposing that CON’s exceptions collapse into either refusal or error.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and executed the format cleanly, including proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 labeling. The decisive exchange was PRO's pressure question exposing a tension in CON's case: CON argued substitution makes bans futile against determined regimes, yet also argued licensed termination provides meaningful deterrent leverage. CON's answer (modest leverage vs. total control, "substitution weakens both mechanisms") was reasonable but never fully closed the gap, especially since CON conceded the worst regimes are "least likely to be delayed" by a ban, which undercuts the claim that regulated markets matter most where harm is worst. PRO capitalized on this in Rebuttal 2 and Closing by turning CON's own admission that losing licensed service imposes "higher cost" and "worse continuity" into evidence that bans achieve real preventive effect before any compromise occurs. PRO's irreversibility framing (exposed sources cannot be "un-exposed" by later termination) also went substantively unanswered beyond CON's assertion that governability matters more than prevention. CON fought hard and its governability/leverage framework is a legitimate alternative lens, but it left a live logical seam that PRO exploited more effectively across rounds, giving PRO the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question Q1 (if substitution is easy, why does termination threat function as leverage?) combined with its follow-up in Rebuttal 2 [A4.4], which CON's answer [B4.1-B4.5] only partially resolved, leaving a persistent inconsistency in CON's substitution-vs-leverage logic that PRO leveraged through to the closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by exposing and effectively exploiting a core tension in Side B's case: if illicit substitution is easy, conditionality offers no real leverage; if conditionality imposes meaningful costs (loss of support/updates), then a ban imposes those exact costs up front before irreversible harm occurs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly weaponized Side B's admission that losing a licensed vendor imposes "higher cost and worse continuity," proving that an upfront ban is more preventative than a post-harm kill switch.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by exposing a decisive tension in Side B’s case: if commercial support is valuable enough that terminating it creates real costs, then denying it outright through a ban also imposes meaningful constraints; conversely, if substitution is effortless, then B’s licensing safeguards are illusory. A also showed that prevention—stopping compromise before it happens—is preferable to after-the-fact remediation for irreversible harms like exposed dissident networks, and that B never proved its conditional safeguards are reliable when the buyer controls the targeting rationale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing reframed Side B’s reliance on licensing leverage by showing that conditional sales first deliver the very capability that makes leverage necessary, while B could not demonstrate that its modest post-sale controls outweigh the additional surveillance they enable.
