### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO won a competitive debate by tightening the clash from “oversight vs. laissez-faire” to “standardized public pre-deployment floor vs. bespoke confidential process,” then winning that narrower fight. CON’s strongest live route—that the motion compulsorily prioritizes a scorecard that neither establishes deployment safety nor requires remediation—stayed alive and sharp, especially on sandbox/integration mismatch and checklist crowd-out. But under pressure CON’s alternative grew statutory inspection powers, published adequacy criteria, fines for refusal, and written risk assessments for hospitals, which PRO fairly re-cast as mandate-minus-standardization/publication. PRO’s comparative case—that public comparable results multiply actors who can act, insure against capture of a single confidential office, and give deployers a baseline CON’s regime withholds—was cleaner and more decisive by the close. Clipping on two PRO turns was a real cost but did not erase the path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s pressure Q1 (what authority/criteria/penalties for confidential evaluators is not itself a standardized mandate) plus the Rebuttal 2/closing reframe of CON’s A1/A2 answers as “required evaluation with penalties, delivered as a document” that only still refuses standardization and publicity.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO won by showing that CON's alternative collapses into the same enforcement infrastructure—statutory authority, fines, mandated criteria—while withholding comparable information from downstream users and the public. CON maintained credible theoretical objections about sandbox-deployment mismatch and checklist crowd-out, but never proved those harms would outweigh the benefits of standardized transparency or explained why deployment-specific testing could not coexist with a pre-deployment floor. PRO's closing crystallized the clash cleanly: the debate resolved to who may read safety results, and CON never justified why secrecy beats sunlight when the mandates are structurally similar.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure questions (Turn 5) exposing that CON's confidential-evaluator model still requires statutory inspection powers, published criteria, and penalties—functionally the same mandated spine PRO defends—while denying hospitals and independent researchers any comparable, standardized safety information.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO kept a live path: standardized mandatory publication solves self-grading, creates comparable scorecard and distributed enforcement via fraud/liability and multi-regulator insurance, with tiered disclosure (domains, aggregates, elicitation, actor to public; prompts to regulator) handling the publication tradeoff. CON’s strongest critiques – sandbox vs deployment and checklist prioritization – were reframed as floor-not-ceiling and answered by visible vs invisible gaming, while CON’s alternative under pressure conceded statutory inspection powers, published criteria, fines, and written risk assessments, narrowing the clash to who gets to read results. CON answered deployment-specific warnings but did not neutralize comparability and capture-insurance value.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 answers to A3 pressure: distinguishing a general duty to cooperate with statutory inspection powers and targeted deployer warnings from a legally prescribed common evaluation battery plus universal publication, plus showing aggregate refusal rates cannot be falsified by API spot-checks without task distribution.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON won by progressively collapsing PRO’s mechanisms: adverse findings still allow same-day deploy; real substance shifts to confidential regulator review; rotating secret challenges break outsider comparability; and the residual public product is a category sentence plus a mandated-completion stamp that reads as clearance. PRO’s externality opening was strong, but its later answers (reputation after earlier dismissing it as too late; partial prevention without a block) left a thin residual case that CON reframed as a waiver and incumbency ritual. After full exchange, CON made the live choice clearer: imperfect seller discretion versus paperwork that neither stops harm nor informs defenders.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2 after pressure: turns B’s A1/A2 concessions (publish-and-ship; confidential full findings; secret rotating challenges) into the twin-horns/stamp/waiver collapse of the motion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON built the stronger path to victory by showing that PRO’s conceded requirements—allowing deployment after publishing adverse results, sending real findings only to regulators, and keeping test challenges secret—leave the public with little more than a generic category-level warning and a government completion stamp. CON persuasively argued that stamp would be read as clearance, creating false assurance and incumbency lock-in, while liability provides stronger ex-ante incentives to fix dangers. PRO’s preventive case collapsed under CON’s turn showing the motion converts warnings into waivers and relies on the very reputational mechanism PRO’s opening dismissed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal (B4), which turned PRO’s opening against them by arguing the motion converts a documented warning into a litigation waiver and that PRO’s reliance on reputational costs contradicted its own dismissal of after-the-harm punishment.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): PRO kept a live externality route: B conceded liability does not force pre-launch learning [B4.1]. A showed mandated, standardized disclosure brings evidence forward when fixes and defenses are still possible [A4.1][A5.2], without needing a licensing veto. CON's strongest hits – public tests get gamed, secret rotating tests break comparability, and a mandated exam reads as clearance [B2.2][B2.4][B4.5] – dent the standardization ideal but don't prove voluntary disclosure + after-harm liability leaves outsiders better protected. That early-warning gap decided it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4's package – no launch blocked, real findings in confidential file [A4.2], comparability dependent on secret prompts outsiders can't check [B4.5], public gets generic "assists attacks" + completion stamp that functions as clearance – was the most decisive pressure, converted PRO's admissions into the waiver/stamp frame.
