### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): CON won by maintaining a live burden throughout: PRO had to show that delaying coal retirement is *necessary* for manufacturing growth, not merely that India uses coal today. PRO never closed that gap. The most decisive structural damage came from three interlocking CON moves. First, the light-vs.-heavy industry mismatch: PRO's employment story promised millions of jobs in textiles and assembly while its power story rested on steel and cement; PRO's bridge (steel enables clusters) was partial, not dispositive. Second, CON's Q2 pressure question on the limiting principle was the sharpest exchange in the debate: PRO answered with "replace exact megawatt capacity," which CON correctly identified as making delay indefinite—"not a bridge, a veto"—and PRO never neutralized this. Third, CON's portfolio answer for reliability (hydro, pumped storage, flexible generation, transmission) was vague but sufficient to rebut the all-or-nothing coal claim; PRO had to show no alternative existed, and could not. PRO's strongest moves were the demographic urgency framing and the "poverty is the ultimate pollutant" comparison, which had real rhetorical force. But CON countered by arguing the poverty-vs.-pollution binary smuggles in the unproven premise that coal extension is the main lever for job creation, and the rebuttal landed cleanly. Side B had two clipped speeches, a real execution cost, but both were clipped at the tail end and the core arguments were visible. That cost is noted but does not reverse the outcome given PRO's persistent structural gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's response to PRO's limiting principle in Rebuttal 2 [B4.3–B4.4]: PRO answered Q2 by saying keep plants open until clean power replaces their "exact megawatt capacity." CON turned this into the decisive blow—real grids transition through portfolios, not unit-for-unit replacement, so PRO's standard makes delay effectively permanent. "That is not a bridge. It is a veto." This was the cleanest comparative move in the debate because it collapsed PRO's central framing (coal as a temporary bridge) and was never adequately answered in the closing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by exposing that PRO's limiting principle turns delay into an indefinite veto and by showing the jobs/power mismatch, neutralizing B's necessity claim after the pressure round. A answered questions directly and crystallized the motion's actual tradeoff; B defended dependence but not the required prioritization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal 2 used B's limiting-principle answer to prove indefinite delay and separated heavy-industry power needs from mass-employment sectors.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON dismantled PRO's core causal chain by proving that delaying coal retirement is not necessary for rapid manufacturing growth. The decisive blows were CON's exposure of PRO's "exact megawatt" limiting principle as an indefinite veto on transition [A4.3-A4.4] and the jobs-sector mismatch that undermined PRO's demographic dividend story. PRO never recovered from these structural problems, retreating instead to a repetitive poverty-versus-pollution frame that assumed—without proving—that extending aging coal assets was the only path out of destitution. CON's portfolio alternative and sharper closing crystallization provided the cleaner, more grounded route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's takedown of PRO's limiting principle in Rebuttal 2 [A4.3-A4.4], showing that replacing coal "exact megawatt for megawatt" is not how real grids transition and therefore converts "temporary delay" into an indefinite veto by legacy assets over industrial policy.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A wins by dismantling PRO’s necessity claim and exposing its limiting principle as an indefinite veto. While Side B framed the stakes compellingly around poverty and demographic urgency, it relied on a false dichotomy (coal vs. blackouts) and an unrealistic grid-transition standard. Side A’s portfolio reliability answer and jobs/power mismatch argument directly neutralized PRO’s core mechanism. Side A incurred a real execution cost for two clipped speeches, but its visible argumentation, sharper weighing, and cleaner resolution of the central clash secure the win.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO built a stronger and more consistently developed case across all rounds. The sequencing argument — that dependable round-the-clock power is the gatekeeper precondition for manufacturing investment, and that retiring existing coal before storage, transmission, and balancing are ready destroys that precondition — was never squarely neutralized. PRO's Q1 ("what firm, currently scalable source guarantees 24/7 power for heavy industry?") was the most decisive moment in the debate: CON's answer naming hydro, nuclear, gas, and a newer coal tier actually conceded that the clean replacement stack is still incomplete and still relies on thermal firm power. PRO's closing turned that concession cleanly against CON, making the sequencing point more decisive rather than less. CON's strongest live argument — green FDI mandates and CBAM penalties — had real force and was the best counter in the debate, but PRO adequately blunted it by distinguishing early domestic-demand-driven manufacturing from carbon-tariff-sensitive export tiers, and by arguing that investors prioritize reliability over purity in the near term. CON's health argument was answered by the comparison to costs of underemployment and the diesel backup point, which CON never resolved. CON's capital diversion argument (every rupee to coal starves storage) had some merit but did not answer how the power gap is bridged in the interim — PRO correctly noted this is future-tense advocacy, not a present substitute. CON was technically competent and landed some real punches, but never resolved the core production constraint PRO kept re-establishing. PRO's closing was notably cleaner and used the evolving exchange rather than repeating the opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing crystallization of CON's Q1 answer: CON's own identification of hydro, nuclear, gas, and newer coal as firm-power bridges during the transition functioned as an implicit concession that the clean replacement stack is incomplete. PRO used this to confirm that even CON accepts the need for dispatchable thermal support, making PRO's sequencing argument — keep usable existing coal as transitional insurance rather than prematurely retire it — the dominant frame. CON never recovered from this turn.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by keeping the debate on sequencing and reliability as the non-negotiable prerequisite, directly answering pressure questions, and weighing poverty/health costs against CON's future-facing claims; Side A lands solid CBAM and capital points but never fully neutralizes the immediate grid gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4) that forces CON to admit the clean stack is incomplete and keeps the gatekeeper comparison decisive.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by establishing that reliable, round-the-clock power is a gatekeeping precondition for manufacturing scale, and that CON's alternative stack—hydro, nuclear, gas, newer coal, and future storage—effectively conceded that the grid is not yet ready to absorb faster coal retirement without jeopardizing industrialization. PRO successfully framed the motion as a sequencing problem rather than a climate betrayal, and CON's later speeches, while sharp, repeated capital-diversion and trade-penalty claims without squarely solving the immediate reliability precondition or explaining how retiring existing coal faster would not strand manufacturing capacity before clean substitutes are deployable at scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO in Rebuttal 2 and Closing showed that CON's answer to the 24/7 power question—citing hydro, nuclear, gas, and newer coal—was an admission that firm thermal support remains necessary, undermining CON's core claim that existing coal can be retired faster without sacrificing manufacturing scale.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by successfully framing grid reliability as a non-negotiable prerequisite for industrialization, effectively turning CON’s clarification about alternative firm power sources into a concession that the clean energy stack is not yet ready. PRO’s sequencing argument—that India must secure manufacturing and fiscal capacity first to fund a sustainable transition later—proved more resilient than CON’s focus on future trade tariffs and capital diversion. CON made strong points about FDI mandates and the costs of aging plants, but PRO’s closing crystallization sharply exposed that CON’s own bridge solutions admitted the necessity of thermal support, securing the ballot on the motion’s core tradeoff.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing effectively leveraged CON’s clarification (that firm power would come from hydro, nuclear, gas, and newer coal) to demonstrate that CON implicitly conceded the clean grid is incomplete, thereby validating PRO’s sequencing argument and winning the reliability clash.
