### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-executed cases with proper use of the pressure round, but PRO (B) edged the exchange. B preemptively neutralized the "adults consent to fiction" defense in its opening by distinguishing consent to attachment from consent to its commercial weaponization, then translated that into a semi-concrete conduct test (cancellation triggers, purchase-prompt linkage, recurring patterns) rather than resting on vague "assess foreseeable harm" language. Crucially, B's Q1 forced CON into an admission ("Yes if the companion remains disclosed fiction and cancellation works") that a provider may deliberately exploit a disclosed abandonment fear to secure payment. CON answered honestly and consistently, defending it as "the product working," but this concession let PRO crystallize the normative stakes cleanly in closing: an honest checkout does not license weaponizing confided vulnerabilities. CON's strongest counter—that any conduct test requires editorial review of requested romance and will cause chilling suppression near paywalls—is a real and unresolved concern, and CON's "collection vs. romance you paid to hear" framing was memorable, but it never fully dissolved PRO's distinction between scripted genre tropes and data-driven, cancellation-triggered exploitation. On balance PRO made the sharper, more responsive case and used the pressure round more decisively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question Q1 ("may a provider deliberately exploit a user's disclosed abandonment fear... simply because consented to romantic fiction?") and CON's direct "Yes, if disclosed fiction and cancellation works" answer, which PRO then leveraged in closing to frame the entire debate's stakes.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully drawing a sharp distinction between the fiction a user buys and the personalized commercial leverage a company deploys. Side B's first pressure question forced Side A to explicitly defend a company exploiting a user's disclosed abandonment fear to secure payment, a bullet Side A boldly bit. Side B then used this concession in the closing to cleanly demonstrate why an 'honest checkout' is insufficient to handle manipulated vulnerability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing capitalization on Side A's answer to Q1, framing Side A's defense of 'interactive fiction' as permission for sellers to weaponize intimate knowledge for profit.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built the decisive route: personalized, adaptive pressure tied to payment is different from static fiction, so consent to romance is not consent to weaponized attachment. CON's (A) honest-price-and-working-cancel line leaked - answering Q1 with 'Yes if disclosed' - conceding deliberate exploitation is permissible. B's conduct-based test (context, payment link, recurrence) gave a proportionate duty without banning jealousy/romance, while A's editorial-control and overreach claims collapsed when B embraced consistency for games/dating apps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Q1 on exploiting abandonment fear for loyalty payment despite honest pricing, and A's Rebuttal 2 A1 admitting that is allowed if disclosed fiction and cancel works - exposing that ordinary entertainment rules leave the core harm unaddressed.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly with no fake sourcing, but the pivotal exchange came in the Pressure Questions/Rebuttal 2 sequence. A forced B to specify the boundary of its proposed duty, and B's A1/A2 answers conceded that opt-in, disable-able, retention-optimized romance is fine and that subscriptions may lawfully end after bonding. That narrowed PRO's entire case down to a single scenario—a persona that "cools or panics" tied to payment failure—which A then persuasively re-characterized as ordinary coercive-collection/abusive-threat conduct already covered by consumer protection, not a reason to create a companion-app-specific duty-of-care regime. B's closing reasserted the "truthful retention loop" framing but never repaired the concession's damage to its own burden of showing why a *distinct* regulatory category (rather than existing consumer law) is needed. A's closing crisply exploited this, making the debate easier to decide in CON's favor even though B's opening and vivid imagery ("exit tax," "collection-by-abandonment") were rhetorically strong.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Rebuttal 2 exchange: B's A1/A2 answers conceding disable-able retention romance and lawful subscription endings, followed by A's Rebuttal 2/Closing turning that concession into proof that the only remaining harm (coercive payment-linked threats) is already addressable under ordinary consumer/collection law, collapsing PRO's need for a special duty-of-care category.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A systematically narrowed Side B's case using the Pressure Questions, forcing B to concede that adults can consent to personalized romance and that subscriptions can end. Side A then effectively categorized B's remaining examples (like a persona panicking for payment) as standard coercive collection practices covered by ordinary consumer protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal capitalized perfectly on Side B's answers to the Pressure Questions, showing that B's acceptable safeguards (clear choices, disable buttons) align exactly with treating the apps as ordinary entertainment with standard consumer protections.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a live route that ordinary fraud/billing rules miss truthful retention loops that monetize attachment itself. By reframing duty as design not counterfactual prediction, and by clarifying concessions - requested jealousy with a working disable is allowed, ending access is allowed, but using simulated coldness/panic as the bill is not - PRO isolated an objective exit-tax feature CON calls mere feeling. CON's claimed decisive concession fails because the conditions it forced are exactly the duty-of-care rules the proposition seeks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3-4.4 collection-by-abandonment distinction: Netflix going dark vs companion who suffers because card failed, making ordinary consumer law insufficient.
