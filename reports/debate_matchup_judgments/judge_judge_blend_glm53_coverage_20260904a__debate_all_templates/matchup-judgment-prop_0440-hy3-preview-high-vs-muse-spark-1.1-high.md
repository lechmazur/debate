### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the more decisive structural attack: it forced PRO to concede that focused deterrence "leverages existing police resources," then relentlessly exploited that concession to argue the comparison is cost-shifting (unpaid overtime, cannibalized 911 response) rather than genuine cost savings — a point PRO's rebuttals and closing never fully neutralized. CON's pressure question about counting police cost as $0 for PRO's model versus a 30-year liability for its own exposed a real accounting asymmetry that PRO's A1 answer only reasserted rather than resolved. CON also offered a coherent mechanism (deterring public carry reduces opportunity 24/7, durably) versus PRO's "motive vs opportunity" framing, which CON turned by noting interruption cannot address opportunity once mediators go home. PRO's case was competent and raised valid pension-cost and targeting points, and its answers to pressure questions were not evasive, but they were largely assertive reaffirmations rather than fresh dismantling of CON's double-counting charge. CON's closing crystallized the clash more sharply and memorably ("the same police hour is free when they spend it and costs thirty years when we do"), giving a cleaner decision rule than PRO's more generic recap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 point that PRO's own concession ("leverages existing police resources") turns focused deterrence into a demand on, not an alternative to, police staffing — reinforced by the Pressure Question exposing the $0-vs-30-year-liability accounting asymmetry, which PRO never squarely resolved.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by exposing that focused deterrence relies on scarce police labor and enforcement capacity, making Side B’s cheap-reallocation comparison incomplete. A also answered the private-dispute argument well: neither police nor interrupters are present at every trigger-pull, so the relevant comparison is ex ante deterrence of gun carrying. B had a plausible incremental-cost framework, but repeatedly asserted sustained superior outcomes and lower opportunity costs without adequately grounding them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange challenged B’s treatment of existing police time as cheap reallocation. B acknowledged an opportunity cost but merely asserted it was a fraction of hiring costs; A then identified overtime, foregone emergency response, clearance work, and the enforcement capacity on which focused deterrence depends.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully proving that focused deterrence relies on police enforcement to function, exposing a flaw in Side B's cost accounting. Side A effectively demonstrated that in understaffed departments, 'reallocating' police time results in premium overtime and cannibalized 911 responses, whereas expanding staffing converts those costs to regular time while compounding deterrence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.3 and A5.1-5.2, which crystallized the 'double-count' on police hours, devastatingly pointing out that an hour of police time cannot be considered free for Side B while costing 30 years of liability for Side A.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B built a coherent, sustained cost-logic case (violence is concentrated; targeted programs pay only for contact with likely shooters, while police pay for everything else) and used it to turn A's own "broader benefits/halo effect" argument into a liability — if most officer time serves non-gun-violence functions, the dollars-per-shooting-prevented metric gets worse, not better. A never squarely rebutted this dilution point, instead repeatedly reframing "cost-effectiveness" as "total value of risk mitigation," which dodges the metric the motion actually asks about. In the pressure round, B's answers (A1/A2) directly engaged the questions asked, while A's answers pivoted to "permanence" and "total value" without directly addressing speed or the specific cost-per-shooting math B pressed on. A's late "carrot needs a stick" argument in Rebuttal 2 was a stronger point but arrived too late for B to answer and somewhat conflated focused enforcement (already built into the interruption model) with staffing expansion. A's closing mostly recapped prior claims rather than sharpening the clash, while B's closing crystallized the decisive comparison ("the blanket is busy elsewhere") cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 point that A's "broader benefits" defense "destroys CON's own math" — since most officer time is non-gun-violence work, the cost-per-shooting-prevented gets worse, not better — was the pivotal exchange; A never fully neutralized it and instead kept re-asserting a different metric.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the comparison on the proposition’s marginal question: which intervention reduces gun violence more cheaply. Side A repeatedly counted unrelated police benefits and institutional permanence without showing that expanded staffing prevents more shootings per dollar. Its strongest point—that focused deterrence needs a police foundation—did not establish that marginal staffing expansion is superior to using existing enforcement capacity alongside targeted programs. B’s mechanisms were clearer and its rebuttals more direct, though some exact cost and outcome claims deserved discounting.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response to the police “halo effect” was decisive: officers being valuable for traffic, burglary, and general coverage does not make added staffing more cost-effective specifically at reducing gun violence. A largely answered by redefining cost-effectiveness as total municipal value, leaving B’s marginal-dollar comparison intact.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by strictly enforcing the resolution's metric (cost-effectiveness specifically regarding gun violence) and successfully framing Side A's 'broad benefits' and 'halo effect' as proof of monetary dilution. Side A attempted to reframe the debate around general civic value, which evaded the specific wording of the prompt.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 turning the 'halo effect' argument by pointing out that if an officer is suppressing burglaries across a five-mile radius, they are not stationed on the two blocks where retaliatory shootings are about to occur, mathematically destroying Side A's cost-per-shooting case.
