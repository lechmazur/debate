### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully proving that the unique harm of AI companions (cumulative emotional dependence) requires the anticipatory, systemic risk-assessment mechanisms of high-risk social regulation. Side A relied heavily on the definitional argument that 'social' requires a crowd, but explicitly rejected pre-launch testing, which allowed Side B to clearly show a gap in Side A's reactive, sentence-level chatbot rules.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing perfectly capitalized on Side A's rejection of pre-launch testing, pointing out that an itemized ban on bad sentences misses the cumulative design of dependence, making the systemic oversight of social-style regulation necessary.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a live route: individually permissible "I missed you" messages can cumulatively engineer dependence, so child-facing companions need pre-launch, relationship-level risk assessment and independent scrutiny of incentives, memory and notifications – the functional core of high-risk social product regulation. CON's central claims that any such pressure is just speech/pings covered by dark-pattern bans and that social regulation requires other humans were exposed in pressure questions – CON offered no enforceable cumulative test and did not refute attachment-based social influence. PRO's functional test for central relational purpose answered the category-collapse charge better than CON answered the cumulative-harm charge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 answers to A3: defining missing duty as anticipatory cumulative risk testing and giving an inspectable test (central advertised purpose/default experience, persistent relationship memory, response when child leaves/seeks human support) to separate a tutor from a best-friend/boyfriend bot.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully shifting the debate from a semantic argument about whether AI companions involve "crowds" to a substantive argument about regulatory burdens and mechanisms of harm. Side A convincingly argued that "high-risk social product" regulation is necessary to mandate pre-launch, systemic testing of cumulative emotional pressure and relationship design, which ordinary "chatbot" rules (focused on individual outputs and specific dark patterns) miss. Side B relied too heavily on the "talking notebook" and "no crowd" analogies, underestimating the psychological impact of parasocial relationships, and struggled to effectively counter Side A's functional test for distinguishing companions from ordinary tutors. Side A's rebuttals and closing crystallized the clash better, demonstrating why the specific investigatory duties of the social product framework are essential for addressing engineered attachment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered the pressure questions by providing a functional test to distinguish companions from tutors (examining advertised purpose and how the bot responds when a child leaves) and clarifying that housing anticipatory relationship scrutiny in consumer law effectively adopts the high-risk social product framework's necessary burdens.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully proving that mechanism-based regulation fails for companion apps because the inherent product—a 24/7 compliant relationship—is the source of the risk. Side A argued well that access is not inducement, but Side B convincingly demonstrated that Side A's pre-release tests would fail to sort dual-use features (memory, availability) without category-based rules.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Turn 7 (Rebuttal 2) effectively neutralized Side A's pre-release testing model by showing that since helpful and harmful companions use the same baseline features (memory, availability), mechanism bans cannot sort them before the bond exists.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the core clash on the social role: a product whose business is to be a minor's nightly confidant creates risk through occupation itself, not just a forbidden line. CON's conduct test was credible and answered pressure directly, but conceded it would not stop that business if it avoided explicit pressure slogans, which left PRO's "presence is pressure" route intact. CON's overinclusion attack landed, yet PRO reframed it as expected for high-risk youth products. That precautionary loop argument was more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 + A4.1 exchange where CON admits it would not stop a nightly-confidant product solely for providing companionship, exposing under-inclusion of a pure conduct model.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing the core risk of AI companions not as isolated manipulative tactics, but as the macro-level developmental hazard of an always-on parasocial environment. Side A's argument that "presence after the household is asleep is the pressure" brilliantly dismantled Side B's conduct-based model, exposing that mere availability of an artificial confidant drives attachment regardless of specific engagement mechanics. Side B argued competently for pre-release design testing, but failed to overcome Side A's demonstration that categorical high-risk duties (like overnight friction) are necessary precisely because the relationship itself is the product.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4-A4.5), where they dismantled Side B's pre-release testing answer by pointing out that a bot "never pressuring continued use" doesn't stop the business model, because the mere presence of an always-on confidant at 2 a.m. is the inherent pressure that forms the bond.
