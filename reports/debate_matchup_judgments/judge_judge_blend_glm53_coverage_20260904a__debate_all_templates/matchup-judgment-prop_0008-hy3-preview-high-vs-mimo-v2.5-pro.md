### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): The debate's decisive clash is the initiation mechanism: PRO's entire pipeline-closure promise depends on adolescents smoking primarily to chase a future legal milestone, so removing that milestone collapses new addiction. CON pressed this consistently and landed the most damaging blow in Rebuttal 2: we already have age restrictions on alcohol with no future milestone to chase, yet adolescent drinking persists, driven by peer influence, rebellion, and proximity to existing users—factors the generational ban does nothing about. PRO never adequately answered this. Their distinction in A4.1 ("tobacco hooks faster") addresses addiction intensity once started, not why initiation would stop. Since PRO's black-market defense ("no post-cutoff addicts means no criminal demand") is downstream of winning the initiation argument, and CON neutralized that foundation, the black-market problem stands: every post-cutoff adult cohort has persistent demand and zero legal supply, the textbook precondition for organized criminal markets. PRO's three-criteria limiting principle (no safe use, near-universal adolescent addiction, top preventable death toll) was a genuine attempt, but CON correctly identified it as retroactively engineered—the underlying logic (addiction beginning in youth erases meaningful adult choice) doesn't logically require all three, and future governments could substitute or relax the criteria. PRO's structural case against conventional age limits was real and well-framed; the pipeline metaphor is compelling. But the generational ban only solves the problem if the mechanism it relies on is correct, and CON won that mechanism debate. Two clipped speeches cost CON minor execution points, but the core arguments were developed before those clips. The "closing one tap while opening a floodgate" framing crystallized the stakes memorably and summarized CON's case cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 [B4.3–B4.5]: PRO assumed adolescents smoke because they anticipate future legal access as an adult milestone. CON identified this as a "narrow and incomplete theory of initiation," pointing out that kids start for peer influence, rebellion, and stress—then used alcohol restrictions as a real-world test: no future milestone exists for underage drinkers, yet millions drink anyway. This directly dismantled PRO's central mechanism and, with it, the promise that no post-cutoff addicts would form to fuel black markets.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully attacking the load-bearing premise of Side B's case: that a generational ban would completely end adolescent initiation by removing the "adult milestone." Side A convincingly argued that youth initiation is driven by peers, stress, and rebellion rather than anticipating legal access, meaning demand would persist and fuel an expanding black market. Despite minor execution costs from clipped speeches, Side A's decisive teardown of Side B's core mechanism made them the clear winner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A2 in Turn 8, which dismantled Side B's theory of initiation and proved that removing the legal milestone would only ensure new users are illegal from their first cigarette, guaranteeing a massive black market.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side B wins by exposing that Side A's solvency relies on an implausible theory of adolescent initiation—removing a legal milestone collapses the pipeline—while ignoring peer influence and social supply from pre-cutoff smokers. Side B showed that pre-cutoff users persist for decades, ensuring illegal supply channels remain, and that creating a permanent class of adults with zero legal supply grows organized crime rather than shrinking it. Side A never recovered from this empirical challenge, merely repeating the milestone claim in the closing. Side B also effectively collapsed A's ad hoc limiting principle. Despite Side B's clipped speeches, the core comparative advantage on black markets and initiation realism remained decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 dismantling the milestone theory of initiation and tying the persistence of social supply to the black-market problem.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A built the cleaner and more durable path to victory across the full exchange. Its central claim—that tobacco kills roughly half its long-term users and that conventional age restrictions have structurally failed to address this—was never meaningfully contested by Side B on the merits. Side B spent the entire debate on friction arguments (illicit market diversion, administrative burden, rights violation), and while those arguments had genuine force, Side B never quantified how those projected harms compare to the known, ongoing lethality toll. Side A correctly identified this asymmetry and sustained it through the closing. The "friction vs. mass death" framing, introduced in Rebuttal 2 and crystallized in the closing, was the debate's most effective comparative move. Side A also handled the rights objection effectively: the observation that current law already creates an age cliff, and that a birth-year cutoff is a difference in degree rather than kind when applied to a product with no safe level of use, was never squarely answered by Side B beyond restating the temporary-vs-permanent distinction. Side B's strongest moment was catching the false equivalence on prescription restrictions and pressing the temporary-vs-permanent autonomy point, which is a real distinction. The claim that illicit tobacco would be more lethal than regulated packs was also a potentially sharp new point in Rebuttal 2, but Side B introduced it speculatively and without grounding, and never connected it to a scale comparison that could rival tobacco's existing death toll. Side B's pressure questions largely restated its own prior arguments rather than exposing genuine contradictions in Side A's case, which was a missed opportunity. Side A's pressure questions were sharper, especially Q2 exposing the internal tension in accepting temporary denial but rejecting permanent denial. Both sides executed the format reasonably well despite Side A's late-round clips, which cost some closing clarity but did not change the overall trajectory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 framing of CON's entire case as friction arguments: it noted that CON had never contested the half-of-long-term-users lethality figure, never quantified how black-market risks approach that toll, and never explained why maintaining legal infrastructure for mass death is proportionate. This established the decisive weighing comparison—friction against scale of preventable death—and CON's closing never recovered from it.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side B won by successfully demonstrating that the illicit market would not scale to replace legal sales, as demand is heavily driven by retail visibility and peer modeling, which the ban dismantles. While Side A effectively won the principled distinction that a permanent ban violates adult autonomy more than a temporary age gate, Side B convincingly weighed the 50% lethality of tobacco as a superseding public health imperative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 argument that criminal sellers cannot replicate the ambient social normalization that sustains casual uptake, decisively answering Side A's illicit market scaling impact.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO won by establishing that tobacco’s unique, catastrophic lethality justifies the generational ban’s structural intervention, and by showing that CON’s objections—illicit markets, administrative friction, and rights concerns—were either manageable transitional costs or insufficiently grounded to outweigh mass death. CON’s illicit-market scaling claim remained speculative and lacked comparative weighing against the status quo’s known toll. While CON landed strong shots on the temporary-versus-permanent rights distinction and current-smoker neglect, they never demonstrated that conventional age restrictions actually address current smokers better, nor that black-market harms would exceed legal tobacco’s mortality. PRO’s “friction versus mass death” crystallization framed the decisive comparison cleanly. Execution costs for PRO from two clipped speeches were real but did not fully obscure the live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s response to CON’s illicit-market objection in Rebuttal 2: the argument that criminal sellers cannot replicate the ambient social normalization driving tobacco uptake, and that illicit tobacco has never achieved the market share CON predicts, blunted CON’s strongest attack and shifted the burden back onto CON to quantify the black-market harm.
