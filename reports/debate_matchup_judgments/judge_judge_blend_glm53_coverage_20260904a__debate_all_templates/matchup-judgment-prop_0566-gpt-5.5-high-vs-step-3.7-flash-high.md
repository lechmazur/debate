### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central logical clash and the principle argument more cleanly across the debate. The most decisive move was PRO's Q1 exposing an internal tension in CON's position: if legacy preference is only a "small tiebreaker," it cannot simultaneously be powerful enough to motivate major multi-generational donations that fund 10–20% of operating budgets. CON's attempt in Rebuttal 2 to thread this needle—arguing the preference is "modest individually but a meaningful signal for sustained relationships"—was coherent but circular and never fully resolved the contradiction PRO had opened. PRO also landed a cleaner logical structure in the closing: "if legacy status doesn't change outcomes, sharp limits do no harm; if it does, a qualified outsider loses to ancestry." CON never escaped this conditional trap. CON's strongest argument—concrete harm to low-income students from reduced donations—is a genuinely compelling counterintuitive point that PRO never fully neutralized empirically. However, CON repeatedly deployed the "10–20% of operating budgets" figure as if it were a verified fact rather than a plausible estimate, weakening its epistemic authority, and PRO's principled response ("money that depends on an admissions advantage is exactly the kind of money universities shouldn't build their admissions system on") was sufficient to at least contest it. PRO was more epistemically disciplined throughout, relying on principle-based logic rather than unverifiable specific numbers. CON also never answered PRO's point that if legacy status predicts campus engagement, applicants can demonstrate that through essays, interviews, and demonstrated commitment—without a hereditary signal. That point stood unanswered and was a live route to PRO's victory at the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 in the Pressure Questions round ("If legacy preference is only a 'small tiebreaker,' why would alumni donations collapse without it? Is it too minor to affect fairness, or powerful enough to motivate major giving?") was the most decisive exchange. It exposed a genuine internal tension in CON's case that CON tried but failed to resolve cleanly, and PRO converted it into the conditional logic trap that framed the rest of the debate and the closing.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1–B4.3: cleanly exposed and resolved CON's tiebreaker contradiction while rejecting the unsubstantiated collapse claim without widening the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by exposing a decisive tension in Side B’s case: legacy and donor preferences cannot simultaneously be a trivial “small tiebreaker” and the load-bearing financial pillar of operating budgets and need-based aid. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built and sustained the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Its two most decisive live points—that legacy/donor preferences systematically displace higher-qualified, less-privileged applicants through substantial boosts, and that schools which have rolled back legacy preferences reported no meaningful drop in alumni giving—were both raised clearly and never squarely neutralized by CON. The financial-tradeoff argument was CON's most important load-bearing claim, and PRO's empirically plausible counter (no giving drop at schools that cut legacy) left it undefended. CON's sharpest move was exploiting PRO's Rebuttal 2 concession—that legacy can function as a tie-breaker and that institutional variation is acceptable—to argue the dispute had narrowed to mechanism, not fundamental legitimacy. This was a real hit, but CON never converted it into a positive victory argument: if the disagreement is merely cap-vs.-discretion, CON still had to show "disciplined discretion" outperforms a hard cap, and its best response—that holistic admissions cannot be carved into clean percentages—applied with equal force to CON's own proposed guardrails. CON was also clipped more frequently (three speeches) than PRO (one), a real execution cost. PRO's principled distinction—that legacy/donor preference is uniquely corrosive because it has no pedagogical or diversity rationale, unlike arts or geographic factors—held up well against CON's symmetry argument, since CON never demonstrated that alumni connection positively enriches the student body's intellectual or experiential range the way other holistic factors do. The debate was genuinely competitive and CON made meaningful pressure with the wealth-in-credentials argument and the concession exploitation, but PRO's unrebutted financial point and cleaner causal story across rounds gave it the clearer overall win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 claim that schools rolling back legacy preferences saw no meaningful drop in alumni giving directly gutted CON's primary institutional-finance argument, and CON never produced a substantive response across the remaining rounds. This was the most consequential unrebutted point in the debate, leaving CON's core tradeoff claim—sacrifice equitable access to sustain philanthropy—without factual grounding.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): B wins narrowly by forcing a clear line on codifying inherited advantage and offering an enforceable cap that directly meets the motion; A’s discretion defense is repeatedly shown as vague and status-quo preserving.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 directly answers the pressure questions with a workable “5–10% cap” definition, exposing A’s discretion as lacking guardrails.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by demonstrating that legacy and donor preferences formally codify inherited advantage without any pedagogical or diversity upside, and that a sharp limit is necessary because Side B’s "disciplined discretion" offered no enforceable safeguard against routine displacement of higher-qualified, less-privileged applicants. Side B’s admission that substantial preferences overriding plainly stronger files should not be defended corroborated Side A’s core harm story, while Side B’s heavier pattern of word-limit violations (three clipped speeches versus one) compounded its inability to complete its institutional case.
