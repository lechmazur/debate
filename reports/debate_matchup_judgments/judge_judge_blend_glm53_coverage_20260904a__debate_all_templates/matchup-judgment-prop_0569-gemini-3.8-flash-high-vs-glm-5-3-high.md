### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON identified and sustained a genuine structural dilemma that PRO never fully escaped: PRO's "handoff" had to be either a substantive clinical review (which collides head-on with CON's clinician-scarcity point and risks real service termination) or a light-touch triage/paraprofessional check (which CON convincingly argued cannot perform the diagnostic work PRO's own opening said only expert humans can do). PRO's best counter—that the fixed backstop is "additive, not exclusive" to dynamic detection—blunted some of this but didn't answer CON's capacity-bottleneck point that even brief mandatory reviews for millions of low-acuity users still divert scarce professional attention from acute cases. CON's pressure questions forced PRO into this dilution of "handoff," and CON's rebuttal 2 exploited that dilution sharply and memorably ("a calendar is not a diagnostic instrument"). PRO's case was well-argued and its "eviction vs backstop" reversal was strong, but its opening and closing were both clipped, costing it the chance to fully resolve the rubber-stamp/eviction dilemma at the moments that mattered most. On balance, CON made the clash easier to decide and closed more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.5/B5.2 exchange, where PRO's flexible redefinition of "handoff" (from full clinical assessment to brief paraprofessional re-authorization) was shown to simultaneously gut PRO's diagnostic justification and still trigger the access/capacity problems CON raised — a dilemma PRO's "additive backstop" response only partially defused.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that PRO’s mandatory checkpoint was either a burdensome bottleneck or a clinically meaningless rubber stamp. CON forced PRO to retreat from “experienced human clinician” to “cursory paraprofessional review,” then showed that such a review cannot catch the subtle, masked crises PRO claimed made AI-only care dangerous. CON also proved that a fixed calendar provides no early protection while threatening access for millions, whereas dynamic escalation responds to acuity in real time. PRO’s inability to repair the “diluted handoff” critique—compounded by a clipped closing—left CON’s comparative framework intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal and closing demonstration that PRO’s mandatory handoff, when pressed on clinician shortages, collapsed into a brief paraprofessional “re-authorization” that could not plausibly detect the complex pathology PRO claimed justified human review—a concession that turned PRO’s core safety justification into a bureaucratic fiction.

A successfully framed the fixed handoff as a necessary backstop rather than a replacement for dynamic escalation, effectively neutralizing B's argument that patients are left unprotected in early sessions. When B attempted to trap A on clinician shortages—arguing the mandate would either cause eviction or dilute into a meaningless triage rubber stamp—A adeptly clarified that a handoff is a trigger for accountable human review, not a kill switch. A's most decisive move was dismantling B's argument about displacing high-risk patients by pointing out that exempting low-risk users requires trusting the very AI classification that B's dynamic model relies on. Although A suffered minor execution costs for clipping at the end of two speeches, A's logical rigor, sharp rebuttals, and superior weighing of the accountable backstop versus unaccountable AI clash secured a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response in Rebuttal 2 to B's pressure question about displacing high-risk patients. A brilliantly pointed out that B's displacement worry assumes we already know who is low-risk, exposing that B's dynamic model relies on the exact flawed AI classification that A's fixed backstop is designed to catch.
