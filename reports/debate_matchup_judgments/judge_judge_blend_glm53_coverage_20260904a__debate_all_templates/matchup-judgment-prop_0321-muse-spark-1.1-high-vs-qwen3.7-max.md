### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the motion focused on consent for a limited commercial reuse, rather than ownership of style. It showed why output-stage copyright does not address lawful mass substitution or creators withdrawing work from public view. Side A raised a serious transaction-cost and incumbent-moat concern, but its upload-filter claim was speculative and its repeated computational-reading frame did not answer the difference in scale, purpose, and commercial impact. B overstated model retention technically, which kept the debate competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B most effectively answered the computational-reading analogy: public visibility does not itself establish consent to industrial commercial training, and a training-use veto does not automatically grant ownership over style. Side A corrected B's exaggerated copy language but never fully neutralized that narrower normative distinction.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): A built the clearer path by framing the decisive clash as who bears consent costs, using the human-vs-machine learning distinction and already-emerging collective opt-in pools to neutralize B's monopoly and open-web fracture claims; B's computational-reading and transaction-cost replies were coherent but never fully displaced creator autonomy or the live fact that free-for-all scraping already favors incumbents.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1 and R2 distinction that models create permanent, zero-cost infinite substitutes (not fallible student memory) plus the collective-pool answer to B's licensing questions.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by establishing that the current default exploits creators through platform terms of service and that a stronger default right places the burden of consent on platforms rather than creators. CON's "computational reading" analogy was materially damaged when PRO showed the scalable, permanent nature of model training, and CON's transaction-cost objection was answered by existing collective opt-in mechanisms like stock libraries and platform toggles. PRO's closing crystallization of "who pays for consent" provided the cleaner weighing mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal in Turn 3 showing that commercial models are permanent, queryable engines capable of producing infinite substitutes at zero marginal cost, which collapsed CON's "computational reading" analogy and reframed the harm as economic exploitation rather than innocent learning.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A won by making the cleaner comparative case: an input veto is overbroad when no protected expression appears in the output, and mandatory clearance would advantage large firms and licensing aggregators over open competitors and creators using affordable tools. Side B compellingly identified substitution and enforcement concerns, but its collective-licensing answer did not resolve the gatekeeper and entry-cost problem, while its claim that generative AI is strictly competitive was effectively rebutted with complementary uses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s closing response to B’s style-substitution claim was decisive: B acknowledged that harmful outputs may interpolate style rather than copy protected expression, and A explained why granting control over style or learning would create a new monopoly extending beyond ordinary copyright. This sharpened the choice between addressing actual copying and imposing a sweeping training veto.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): B built the clearer comparative path by showing an input veto is overbroad (bans non-substitutive learning), that style/vibe interpolation is not existing copyright harm for sound reasons, and that PRO’s collective-licensing fix still favors large aggregators while killing open tools smaller creators rely on. A’s power-asymmetry and output-enforceability concerns were live but never outweighed the commons and overbreadth costs B repeatedly crystallized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal + closing (B4.1–B4.2, B5.1) turning A’s black-box/style-substitution claims into proof that input control bans learning without copyrightable harm and invents a monopoly over public study.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON successfully undermined PRO's core case by showing that output-level copyright protection is already enforceable through substantial-similarity analysis and discovery, while an input veto would create an impossible permission thicket that entrenches tech giants and open-source gatekeepers. PRO's closing admission that the real harm is non-copyrightable style interpolation conceded that no existing IP right supports the requested default veto, leaving PRO asking for a new monopoly over public learning rather than enforcing existing commercial rights. CON's framing remained consistent and comparatively sharper across the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answers to PRO's pressure questions, demonstrating that output infringement is enforceable under standard copyright doctrine and that guarding training datasets reflects curation value rather than admission of theft—directly collapsing PRO's "unenforceable outputs" and "learning vs. stealing" pillars.
