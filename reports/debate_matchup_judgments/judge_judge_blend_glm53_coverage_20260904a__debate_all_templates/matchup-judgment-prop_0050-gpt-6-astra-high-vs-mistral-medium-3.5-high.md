- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): B kept a live route to victory by framing the real policy tradeoff: a uniform emissions charge either prices out occasional trips or under-restrains heavy repeat use, and progression plus retained distance/cabin charges breaks that link. B answered pressure more directly and crystallized the comparative choice more cleanly.

CON's opening was clipped, its rebuttals grew repetitive, and its insistence that "equal emissions demand equal tax" never adequately engaged PRO's access trade-off or justified ignoring the distributional problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's narrowing of CON's "global, real-time ledger" objection to a manageable domestic counter, and its burden-shift challenging CON to prove that substitution toward longer trips would outweigh emissions from flights avoided.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON built the cleaner decisive path: concentration of aviation emissions supports charging the damage each journey causes (so heavy flyers already pay repeatedly), but does not justify an extra personal-history escalator. PRO never closed the equal-emissions / necessity gap, mixed “excessive” with mere flight count, and left deterrence/incidence and exemption design unresolved while leaning on repeated concentration claims. PRO’s political framing was memorable, yet the live clash on metric, fairness, and targeting favored CON after pressure and rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s A1/A4 reply that an emissions tax already multiplies charges across identical trips, so calling “no escalation” a flaw assumes the conclusion; equal annual emissions should not get different taxes merely for more shorter legs, and takeoff effects should be priced directly if they matter.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by exposing that Side B (PRO) assumed frequency equals dispensability and excessive emissions without proving the link. CON showed that equal annual emissions should attract equal tax, and PRO never justified why a passenger’s flight sequence deserves an additional surcharge beyond per-journey emissions. PRO’s answers to pressure questions relied on unsupported generalizations (“typically more discretionary”) and vague appeals to existing frameworks rather than concrete exemption rules, leaving the core burden unmet. CON’s alternative—price each journey’s damage and redistribute—remained cleaner and unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1–A4.8, where CON directly answered B’s pressure questions and demonstrated that PRO had defended a bigger stick without better aim, crystallizing that equal emissions require equal tax and that frequency is neither carbon nor necessity.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A systematically dismantled Side B's core premise that flight frequency is a valid proxy for either carbon emissions or discretionary travel. Side A effectively demonstrated that a distance- and cabin-weighted emissions tax already ensures frequent flyers pay proportionally more, rendering the progressive escalator redundant and unfairly punitive to those with essential repeated travel needs (e.g., caregivers). Side B struggled to answer Side A's pressure questions, relying on vague appeals to "existing frameworks" for exemptions and flawed logic regarding takeoff emissions when total emissions were stipulated as equal. Side A's rebuttals were sharper, more logically rigorous, and decisively won the clash on targeting and fairness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's pressure questions and subsequent rebuttal, where A clarified that ten identical flights already incur ten full emissions charges under their model, exposing B's failure to justify why the tenth flight should carry an additional penalty beyond its actual carbon footprint.
