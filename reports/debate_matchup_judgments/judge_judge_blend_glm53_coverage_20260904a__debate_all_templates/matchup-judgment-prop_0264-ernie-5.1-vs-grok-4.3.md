### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins a clear but competitive debate by landing and sustaining the structurally decisive argument: when an officer carries personal liability insurance, the insurer pays the judgment, not the officer. The officer's out-of-pocket exposure is limited to a fixed annual premium, so the promised "personal stake" is largely illusory. PRO's answer—that premiums rise with claims history, creating career costs—is plausible but incomplete: CON immediately countered that departments absorb rising premiums through wages, rebuilding the exact taxpayer backstop PRO claimed to eliminate. That two-step chain (claims rise → premiums rise → departments subsidize → taxpayers pay anyway) was never broken by PRO. PRO's Rebuttal 2 ran notably short (207 of 340 words) and left this chain insufficiently addressed. CON's closing crystallized the single structural flaw cleanly and tied it back to the proposition's own standard—costs should not fall mainly on municipal taxpayers. PRO had genuine strengths: the medical-malpractice analogy was apt, the visibility argument was real, and the "moral hazard runs in both directions" reframe was sharp. But PRO never demonstrated that the mechanism actually shifts the marginal dollar away from municipalities rather than merely relabeling it. CON's brittle-precision problem in the opening (exact premium figures) slightly weakens its grounding score, but that imprecision did not infect the core structural argument that carried the later rounds. The debate was decided by which side had a live route to victory after the full exchange; CON's structural critique remained live while PRO's accountability mechanism was shown to depend on conditions it never secured.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was CON's introduction in A2.3/A4.1 of the "insurer pays, not officer" structural critique—that the officer's personal exposure is capped at a fixed premium regardless of settlement size—against PRO's response in B4.1 that rising premiums still create a career cost. CON immediately turned that response by pointing out that departments absorb those premiums through wages, recreating the taxpayer backstop. PRO never broke this chain, leaving CON's core structural argument fully live at the close.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON won by exposing a structural flaw in the insurance mechanism: because the insurer—not the individual officer—pays any settlement judgment, the officer’s financial exposure is capped at the premium level, which PRO repeatedly conceded departments could absorb through wages. CON sharpened this across rounds and crystallized it in the closing as the decisive barrier to shifting real cost away from taxpayers. PRO’s market analogies and deterrence theory remained plausible in the abstract, but they never fully answered CON’s pressure on baseline affordability for a $60k salary or discharged the subsidy spiral, and their “collective pressure on supervisors” turn in Rebuttal 2 was speculative. CON also handled PRO’s pressure questions more directly and precisely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 1 turn at [A2.3], arguing that because the insurer pays the judgment while the officer only pays a premium, the personal stake is actually reduced rather than increased, reversing PRO’s moral-hazard claim and forcing PRO into a defensive posture about “career costs” that never fully answered the capped-exposure problem.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON wins by successfully attacking the causal mechanism at the heart of the proposition. By demonstrating that insurance structurally caps an officer's out-of-pocket exposure at a fixed annual premium while the carrier pays the actual judgment, CON severs the link between individual misconduct and direct financial consequence. CON also effectively argues that volatile police risk and lack of actuarial data would make baseline premiums unaffordable on a typical officer's salary, forcing municipalities to subsidize coverage to prevent staffing collapses. This directly defeats the motion's explicit goal of shifting costs away from taxpayers. PRO's reliance on medical malpractice analogies and "career cost" price signals was coherent but never fully resolved the structural subsidy and moral-hazard clashes, leaving CON with the cleaner path to victory on the motion's core metric.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1) directly answers PRO's pressure question by explaining how insurance mechanics cap personal liability at the premium, meaning the marginal dollar of a large settlement never reaches the officer. This cleanly undercut PRO's deterrence claim and reinforced why departments would inevitably absorb the premium cost, returning the burden to taxpayers.
