### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins by a clear but competitive margin. PRO built and sustained the more internally coherent case: the anticipatory-avoidance mechanism was well-specified, the systemic vs. episodic framing was consistently applied across all rounds, and PRO's best line—"a flawed lesson invites rebuttal; pervasive caution teaches intellectual surrender"—crystallized the comparative stake better than anything CON offered. PRO's most effective move came in Rebuttal 2, where it turned CON's strongest factual point (state mandates requiring harmful framings are current and expanding) into partial support for its own thesis: vague enforcement climates cause omission to spread faster than the mandates themselves. That pivot was not merely evasive—it preserved PRO's mechanism while absorbing the factual concession. CON had two genuinely live arguments that PRO never fully answered: first, the marginalized-student dropout claim (if a student leaves school entirely, PRO's model of intellectual development is moot), and second, the developmental/age-appropriate sequencing point (a 10-year-old needs factual grounding before contested framings). PRO's answer to dropout—"they can still be reached by later correction"—was its weakest response in the debate and left that burden only partially discharged. CON also correctly won the "is harmful content a relic?" exchange; PRO's opening overclaim that such framings were "already largely excised" was factually outgunned. However, CON's case was more reactive than structurally dominant: it accumulated good specific rebuttals without ever displacing PRO's central framing about chronic, self-reinforcing curricular anemia across all students and all domains. PRO's systemic argument ultimately outweighed CON's targeted-harm argument for the purpose of the proposition as written—education quality broadly—even accounting for the clipping cost on PRO's first two speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 pivot on state mandates: rather than conceding that current harmful content rules refute its thesis, PRO argued those mandates primarily harm education through the vague enforcement climate they generate, causing self-censorship to spread faster and wider than the mandates themselves. This turned CON's strongest factual point into collateral support for PRO's mechanism, and CON never recovered a clean answer to it.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A built the more cohesive comparative case by showing how self-censorship operates as a chronic, systemic distortion of education's core mission, while successfully absorbing B's best examples of state content mandates into A's causal story as accelerants of fear-driven omission. A handled pressure questions more cleanly and crystallized the clash in closing as a trade between episodic error and chronic intellectual incapacity. B landed strong moral appeals about vulnerable students but never resolved the tension between calling self-censorship a localized outlier while claiming harmful content mandates are widespread, and B's age-appropriateness response to A's critical-capacity pressure did not fully address older students. The decisive edge goes to A's sustained mechanism and weighing, though the two clipped speeches were a real execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal reframing B's own evidence of state mandates as forces that accelerate rather than refute teacher self-censorship, turning B's strongest examples into support for the PRO case.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by establishing a cleaner comparative framework: self-censorship is a chronic, self-reinforcing mechanism that strips students of the critical tools needed to evaluate any content, including the harmful material CON fears. While Side A (CON) effectively highlighted immediate, targeted harms to marginalized students and the stickiness of misinformation, PRO successfully argued that anticipatory avoidance shrinks the curriculum at the planning stage, bypassing formal review processes and producing systemic intellectual timidity. PRO's weighing of chronic incapacity versus episodic error, alongside the intellectual immune system framing, resolved the clash more decisively. CON's case was strong but leaned heavily on extreme examples that PRO contextualized as outliers or already discredited, and CON never fully neutralized PRO's meta-claim that without exposure to contested premises, students cannot develop the skills to correct misinformation later. Both sides followed format rules and handled pressure well, but PRO's progression, sharper comparative weighing, and tighter crystallization gave it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over whether harmful content is a relic versus a current mandate, and how that interacts with self-censorship. PRO effectively turned CON's state-mandate examples by arguing that vague political climates accelerate anticipatory omission of even mainstream sources, making self-censorship the broader, systemic threat. CON's pushback that foundational facts must precede contested material was solid, but PRO's response that curated silence prevents students from ever learning to interrogate doctrine won the weighing on long-term education quality.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B wins because its core harm comparison—temporary, mitigable individual discomfort versus universal, permanent knowledge gaps—was clearly framed from the opening and never squarely neutralized. Side A's entire causal story depended on the premise that most self-censored content carries extreme activist overlays (privilege reflection mandates on TKAM, collective guilt exercises, etc.), and B correctly challenged this as unproven throughout. A's Rebuttal 2 answer to B's decisive Q1 ("can you show the vast majority of cut material includes these extreme framings?") responded by asserting TKAM now arrives with "mandatory privilege reflection exercises," an unverifiable claim presented with false certainty that B rightly exposed in the closing. B's pressure questions were sharper: Q1 targeted A's central unproven premise, and Q2 revealed that A's individual-harm threshold could never be met. A's own Q1 inadvertently helped B by asking why teachers drop classics without activist content—B answered at [B4.1] that vague political pressure cannot distinguish neutral from contested material, which is exactly B's position. A's "downstream of overreach" refrain became formulaic rather than responsive. B's harm comparison (narrow/avoidable vs. universal/permanent) remained live through every round, while A's mirror argument depended on a factual claim it could not ground.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to A's Pressure Question Q1 at [B4.1] is the most decisive exchange. A's own question—why teachers drop classics with no collective guilt content if self-censorship is only downstream of overreach—was intended to pressure B, but B turned it cleanly against A: vague political pressure campaigns don't distinguish extreme ideological content from standard decades-old curricula, so self-censorship sweeps up both. This directly exposed the fatal flaw in A's causal story and confirmed B's core thesis.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (CON)): PRO successfully established that vague political pressure causes teachers to preemptively cut standard, decades-old curriculum, creating universal and permanent knowledge gaps. CON failed to substantiate its core premise that the majority of self-censored material is wrapped in activist scaffolding; this claim remained an unsupported assertion against PRO's plausible examples. PRO maintained a cleaner comparative harm framework and handled pressure questions more directly, while CON's attempt to attribute the "downstream of overreach" position to PRO backfired. CON's closing merely repeated its unproven empirical premise rather than resolving the central clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal, which exposed that vague political pressure indiscriminately cuts standard curriculum alongside contested content, and turned CON's "downstream of overreach" framework against it.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by maintaining a cleaner comparative frame and exploiting Side A's (CON) failure to substantiate its core premise. PRO consistently weighed the universal, permanent harm of pre-emptive self-censorship against the narrow, mitigable harm of controversial content, while forcing CON to prove that most cut material actually contains the "activist scaffolding" CON describes. CON relied on repeated assertions about curricular overreach without bridging the gap to why vague political pressure wouldn't also sweep up standard, neutral lessons. The debate was significantly impacted by Side A's Pressure Questions round, which accidentally attacked CON's own case ("If self-censorship is downstream of overreach as you insist…"), creating a major execution cost and ceding momentum. Side B handled the misfire cleanly, answered directly, and crystallized the clash effectively in the closing. PRO's causal mechanism (risk-averse teachers cutting broadly to avoid unpredictable backlash) was more plausible and better grounded than CON's claim that self-censorship surgically targets only ideological overlays.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and 2 effectively dismantled CON's straw man by distinguishing between extreme ideological content and standard curricula, while sharply highlighting how vague political pressure forces teachers to pre-emptively cut neutral material. B's handling of A's misfired pressure questions—correctly noting the misattribution while still answering the substance—sealed the comparative advantage.
