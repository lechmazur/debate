### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that removing confinement can exchange continuous restriction for increased injury, disease, and mortality rather than straightforwardly reducing suffering. It also offered the cleaner response to antibiotic resistance: regulate prophylactic antibiotics directly. Side B compellingly emphasized guaranteed deprivation, but repeatedly asserted rather than demonstrated that confinement requires antibiotics and that alternative housing makes aggression merely manageable. Its reliance on import restrictions and safety nets also weakened its confident pennies-per-meal framing. Side A overstated some unsupported commercial and California specifics, keeping the margin limited.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was its answer that antibiotic resistance does not independently justify a confinement ban: direct antibiotic regulation targets the claimed harm, while confinement can reduce bites, fecal exposure, and contact with wild disease vectors. Side B largely repeated that confinement requires antibiotics without resolving those mechanisms.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON built the clearer path by making the proposition fail on its own terms: phase-outs do not reliably reduce net animal suffering (higher commercial mortality, lesions, and culling replace immobilization), produce non-modest price spikes once import walls and retrofits are added (California coupling), and are inferior to direct antibiotic regulation plus labeling. PRO's moral baseline and "pennies" framing never neutralized the regressive-now harm or the empirical welfare data; safety nets and "manageable aggression" stayed promissory. CON answered pressure more cleanly and kept the decisive comparison (bodies vs inches, immediate protein costs vs later programs) tighter across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON A2/A4 answers to mortality metric and confinement-without-antibiotics, plus California price-spike weigh against PRO's import-ban fix.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's two main pillars: the "modest" price increase and the net reduction in animal suffering. Side A convincingly argued that Side B's proposed remedies to prevent offshoring (import bans) would actually cause significant price spikes, citing real-world dynamics like those in California. Furthermore, Side A successfully challenged Side B's idealized view of cage-free systems by pointing to commercial data showing higher mortality and injury rates, framing the trade-off as exchanging a restricted life for a painful death. Side B's dismissal of these empirical outcomes as a "manageable husbandry challenge" was a weak weighing mechanism compared to Side A's grounded, outcome-focused rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the animal welfare clash, where it countered Side B's "manageable aggression" argument by pointing to commercial mortality data and reframing the metric of decency from "crate inches" to "bodies," effectively exposing the hidden suffering and higher death rates in cage-free systems.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by centering the motion's stipulated modest price rise and weighing that limited, distributed burden against severe and certain confinement. Side A offered plausible contract-farmer and processed-import mechanisms, but never grounded its escalation from modest costs to food insecurity or showed leakage large enough to defeat the welfare gain. Side B's claims about exact costs and prior phaseouts were somewhat overconfident, and Side A successfully distinguished fresh-market consolidation from processed-food imports, keeping the debate competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest response conceded that sufficiently large food-price increases could be unjust, then used the motion's modesty condition to expose Side A's missing threshold and directly compared the limited consumer burden with lifelong immobilization. Side A never established that the stipulated increase reaches its claimed crisis level.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B won by making the decisive clash clearer: extreme immobilization is a severe hidden harm, real phaseouts kept shelves full with only modest shared costs, and A's dual predictions (ruinous spikes plus import leakage) cancel each other. A raised legitimate regressivity and consolidation worries but never resolved the ownership contradiction or the live experience of states/EU, leaving B's path intact after sustained pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4/B5 exposing A's mutually canceling predictions on consolidation vs. leakage
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by decisively dismantling Side A's economic harms. B exposed a fatal logical contradiction in A's case: A simultaneously argued that domestic prices would spike (causing a regressive tax) and that cheap imports would flood the market (causing leakage). B correctly noted both cannot happen at the scale A claimed. Furthermore, B effectively neutralized A's consolidation argument by pointing out that the most restrictive systems are already high-capital corporate models, meaning phasing them out does not disproportionately burden small family farms. B's weighing of the modest cost against the severe harm of lifelong immobilization was clear and persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they explicitly laid out the contradiction in Side A's economic predictions, noting that A cannot simultaneously claim a regressive price apocalypse and an import takeover. This completely collapsed Side A's impact calculus.
