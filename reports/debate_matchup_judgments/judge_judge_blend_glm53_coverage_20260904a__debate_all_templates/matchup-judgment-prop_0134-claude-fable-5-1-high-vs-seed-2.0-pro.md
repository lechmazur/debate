### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by exposing the central accounting error in A's case: units already serving tourists are not removed from the long-term rental pool by a later restriction. B also distinguished returned units from future conversions prevented, which A incorrectly folded into its unsupported one-in-seven ratio. B then offered the clearer comparison between broad, recurring rent and displacement benefits and narrower, partly redirected income and visitor-spending losses. A's natural-exit counterfactual appeared only in closing and was asserted without grounding. B's clipped opening and later unsupported study references impose some cost, but do not overcome its decisive comparative advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was its answer that the one-in-seven delisting ratio measures what happens to existing tourist units but says nothing about tenant evictions and conversions that restrictions prevent in the future. A never supplied that missing ledger and instead repeatedly treated already-unavailable rentals as newly destroyed supply.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by showing that the six units CON labeled as "burned" supply had already left the rental pool when owners evicted tenants to convert them to STRs, so restrictions do not shrink supply further. A’s flow-versus-stock distinction and the eviction-pipeline argument turned CON’s own 1-in-7 example against them and established a live route to victory: diffuse rent relief and prevented displacement outweigh concentrated income losses from operators who retain the asset. CON never squarely quantified prevented conversions and introduced a new natural-exit mechanism only in the closing, which violated the rule against late new angles and left A’s comparative weighing as the cleaner, unresolved path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal and Pressure Question answers reframing the 1-in-7 statistic by distinguishing stock from flow, demonstrating that the six units were tourist units already removed by prior evictions and that the real benefit is stopping future conversions, thereby neutralizing CON’s central supply-destruction claim.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the supply counterfactual. CON's entire math counted STR units as currently in the rental pool, but CON conceded [A2.1] they left when evicted for tourists. PRO showed 1-in-7 is then pure gain plus a flow benefit of prevented evictions, while owner-occupier sales still house people. CON never estimated that flow, wrongly claimed it was inside the 1-in-7 ratio [A4.4], and added a new natural-reversion story only in closing. PRO's diffuse rent benefit vs reversible tourist premium better satisfies the motion's weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 1 and B4-B5 dismantling "burned" supply: showing no resident is evicted on restriction day, distinguishing stock vs flow, and exposing that CON's ledge of prevented conversions was never offered.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by exposing both the instability and poor grounding of B’s causal case. B moved among a 12% conversion rate, a 4–7% “sliver,” and repeated exact claims about rent growth and seven-to-one benefits without support. More importantly, once leakage into pieds-à-terre and other non-rental uses was raised, B substituted an incoherent claim that existing owners were continually “outbidding” tenants. A clearly explained why an owner who already holds the unit is not bidding in rental negotiations and why leakage weakens the supply mechanism. A’s own weighing was imperfect because a lower rent level can create recurring savings, and its clipped second rebuttal carries an execution cost, but it still provided the cleaner comparison between a limited, uncertain housing effect and recurring income and spending losses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to B’s marginal-bidding mechanism was decisive: an existing owner is not bidding against tenants for an apartment already owned, while a replacement pied-à-terre or condo buyer does not restore rental supply. This left B without a coherent answer to leakage and made its unsupported compounding claim much less credible.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B won because it squarely neutralized PRO's core mechanism: the claim that landlords "outbid" long-term tenants collapsed once CON showed owners already hold the units, and that leakage to pieds-à-terre/condos erases the supposed supply gain. B also exposed PRO's unsupported, shifting numbers as brittle fake precision. Without a coherent causal path or credible magnitude, PRO could not show the benefits exceed the certain, recurring costs to hosts and neighborhood businesses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that an owner already holds the apartment and therefore cannot be "outbidding" a nurse for it, combined with the point that a one-time supply shift changes rent levels but not the growth rate driving displacement—this gutted PRO's causal story and left its weighing unsupported.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON wins the tradeoff the motion sets. PRO shifted from 12% baseline to 4-7% sliver and relied on brittle, unsourced precision (seven dollars saved, growth slowed by half/two-thirds, 340 days) while asserting contradictory supply stories. CON shows the sliver minus leakage to pieds-à-terre, condos, and 30-day lets can only produce a one-time level nudge of 2-3%, not a compounding growth-rate brake on displacement, and that new marginal-bidding mechanism fails because an owner already holding a unit is not bidding against renters. Certain recurring losses to spare-room hosts and local businesses outweigh speculative diffuse gain, even after clipping penalty.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2/A5 exposing PRO’s empty-unit contradiction and dismantling the marginal-bid mechanism – if units are empty 340 days they add no supply, and an existing owner does not outbid tenants for his own apartment, so no street-level rent negotiations change.
