### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON built a cleaner structural attack and sustained it across rounds: the proxy-vs-use critique, the magnitude dilemma (a fee small enough to be tolerable can't change behavior; one large enough to change behavior is punitive and creates savage cliff effects), and the minivan/exemption contradiction all landed and were pressed hard in the questions round. PRO's answers were competent but conceded ground rather than closing it—most damagingly, PRO's A1 answer explicitly retreated from the opening's deterrence/pedestrian-death framing to "cost recovery alone justifies it," which CON immediately weaponized as evidence the safety case had quietly become a revenue case, and PRO's closing never repaired that fracture. CON's closing then exploited PRO's own "size is the cost, measured in feet and mass" framing by pointing out the actual motion charges a categorical surcharge for "oversized" vehicles, not a continuous per-foot/per-pound fee—a sharp, motion-text-grounded rebuttal that PRO had no room to answer. PRO's Rebuttal 2 was also clipped before its closing synthesis, a real execution cost. PRO's opening and externality framing were strong, and its distinction between crash frequency (AEB) and crash severity (mass) was a genuinely good point that was never fully neutralized, keeping this competitive rather than lopsided—but CON's dilemmas were more decisive and less answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's magnitude dilemma (small fee = pure tax with no safety effect; large fee = punitive cliff) forced PRO into "cost recovery alone justifies it," which CON then used in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing to reframe PRO's whole case as revenue-seeking dressed as safety—an attack PRO never squarely rebutted.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by converting PRO’s safety frame into a dilemma (small fee = pure tax; large fee = punitive/gamed), distinguishing endorsed use/weight tools from a static silhouette surcharge, and closing on the gap between continuous mass/space costs and the motion’s tiered cliffs. PRO kept a live curb-externality residual and landed real hits on imperfect proxies and AEB physics, but retreated to “cost recovery alone” and left calibration/threshold pressure unresolved; the clipped R2 also cost execution.

- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by systematically dismantling the mechanism of the proposition. CON effectively trapped PRO in a dilemma where the fee is either too small to change behavior (making it a pure tax) or too large (making it punitive and regressive). CON's closing was exceptionally sharp, noting that while PRO argued "size is the cost" (measured in feet and mass), the motion actually enacts tiered category fees with cliff effects, not continuous per-foot or per-pound pricing, and ignores velocity and usage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing argument pointing out that PRO's defense of continuous physical pricing (per foot/per pound) contradicts the motion's actual mechanism of tiered category fees with cliff effects, and that crash energy relies on velocity, which the static fee ignores.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Both sides fought a genuinely engaged debate, but B's case proved more resilient under pressure. B established an unrebutted physical mechanism (blunt, tall, heavy vehicles are more lethal to pedestrians), backed it with curb-scarcity and pavement-wear logic, and crucially exposed that A's chief alternative (federal design standards) is not a lever cities actually hold — a structural point A never overcame, instead pivoting to "spend on redesign instead," which B correctly noted needs the very revenue it disputes. A's strongest weapon was the textual "named category vs. measured cost" gotcha, sharpened effectively in cross-examination and the closing ("every time they defended the policy, they described a different policy"). This was rhetorically the most memorable moment of the debate, but B's answer — that the motion's own conditional clause ("when size imposes higher costs") licenses cost-tracking criteria like weight and hood height, with the named categories simply describing where those costs concentrate — is a fair, textually defensible parry that A never fully dismantled beyond repeating the charge. A also scored points on regressivity and on distinguishing exposure-based taxes (fuel tax) from a flat fee, but B's closing correctly noted parking/curb fees are inherently per-use, blunting that distinction. On balance, B's throughline survived the exchange more intact than A's did.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The cross-examination exchange over whether a three-ton EV sedan pays the same fee as a three-ton pickup: A's pointed Q1 forced B into a "yes" that then introduced hood-height/weight criteria not literally in the motion, which A leveraged hard in Rebuttal 2 and Closing — but B's framing that the motion's "when size imposes costs" clause licenses exactly that cost-based schedule prevented the trap from fully landing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): CON made the decisive clash clearer and stickier: every PRO defense of “true cost pricing” quietly substituted weight/footprint/hood-height schedules the motion never contains, while naming “oversized SUVs and pickups.” That reframe survived Pressure Answers and closing, neutralizing PRO’s physics and city-tool advantages. PRO kept the externality story alive and showed city design standards are weak, but never escaped the category-vs-cost bind or the point that length-based curb meters already handle footprint without the surcharge. CON’s comparative weighing (fee vs redesign/exposure pricing/general revenue) was tighter by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2/closing lock on B4.1: PRO’s “yes” on the three-ton sedan immediately expands into hood-height indexing the motion does not authorize, proving the defensible policy is not the one on the table.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully defending the economic mechanism of the motion and decisively dismantling Side A's alternatives. While Side A ran a highly polished semantic attack arguing the motion targets a "body style" rather than "measured cost," Side B neutralized this by showing the physical dimensions of these vehicles are the costs themselves, and the motion's conditional clause allows for geometry-based schedules. Crucially, Side B defeated Side A's "exposure" argument in the closing by pointing out that the motion includes parking and curb fees, which track exposure continuously, exposing Side A's selective reading of the text. Side B also proved that Side A's preferred alternatives (street design, federal regulation) are slower, out of local control, and lack funding without the fee's revenue. Side A's rhetoric was exceptional, but Side B's substantive command of the clash was superior.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing and Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side A's "exposure" argument by pointing out that parking and curb fees track usage per hour and per foot, and successfully navigated Side A's trap question about a 3-ton sedan by distinguishing mass from front-end geometry.
