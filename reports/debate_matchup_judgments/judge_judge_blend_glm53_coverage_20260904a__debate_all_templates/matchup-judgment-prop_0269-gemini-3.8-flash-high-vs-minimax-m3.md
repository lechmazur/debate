### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's economic critique (who actually funds expanded authentication/absorption, and the mirrored buyer-side moral hazard) landed harder and was never fully neutralized. A's answer to the funding pressure question ("fund it from existing commissions") was undercut by B's follow-up that those margins are already committed to hosting, payments, and dispute mediation—an unanswered point by the time of A's closing. Meanwhile, B's segmentation argument (that the $80–$400 "mid-tier" A relies on contains many low-risk categories, not just sneakers/streetwear) directly rebutted A's "concession surrenders the debate" claim, yet A's closing simply reasserted the concession without engaging that rebuttal, leaving a live point unaddressed. A scored well on the initial structural/incentive framing and on concrete anti-abuse mechanisms (tamper tags, reporting windows) responding to B's buyer-fraud concern, but B's cumulative cost-pass-through and scope-segmentation arguments were the more decisive, unresolved threads by the end. B's closing being clipped is a real execution cost, but the substantive content had already been delivered in the body of that speech.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1–B4.2), distinguishing "targeted SKU-specific authentication" from "categorical mid-tier expansion" and showing that A's own examples (sneakers/streetwear) are only a slice of the disputed band, defused A's "concession surrenders the debate" pressure-question trap and was never adequately answered in A's closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): A won by locking the decisive clash on residual risk and moral hazard: platforms take a large cut while externalizing counterfeit loss, despite superior detection tools and data. A successfully narrowed past B’s “every $20 tee” caricature to the unprotected mid-tier, then used pressure questions to force B’s concession on high-risk authentication, converting it into support for authenticating more items. B’s best live route—pass-through fees, scale friction, and buyer moral hazard—remained competitive and partially unanswered on pure funding mechanics, but B’s later targeted-vs-categorical split felt like damage control and never fully neutralized the incentive argument. B’s clipped closing was an execution cost. Overall A made the motion easier to affirm after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Pressure Q1 trapping B’s [B2.7] high-risk concession, then A4 crystallizing that sneakers/streetwear are the contested mid-tier so B had already granted “authenticate more” while still dodging residual risk absorption.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO clarified scope to unprotected mid-tier and kept cheapest-cost-avoider / incentive alignment live, while CON conceded targeted authentication for sneakers/streetwear which is precisely that mid-tier, undercutting its throughput case. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but CON built the more decisive structural case. CON correctly identified that the bulk of resale volume is low-margin, heterogeneous everyday apparel (not the luxury/sneaker goods PRO's core analogy relied on), and sustained a cost/margin critique that PRO never fully answered for that segment. CON's sharpest moment was catching PRO in a genuine contradiction: PRO argued apparel is too heterogeneous for buyers to judge but simultaneously claimed centralized inspection could handle that same heterogeneity "in minutes" at scale (B2.3), and later exposed that PRO's retreat to authenticating only branded/high-margin items (A4.1) is simply describing the status quo luxury-tier practice already conceded to exist (B4.3) — meaning PRO's "more" collapses into "the same," undermining the core ask of the motion. PRO's answers to pressure questions were reasonable but didn't resolve this retreat, and PRO's closing reasserted the tiered model without directly rebutting the "this is just status quo" charge. CON's closing assertion that "there is no free lunch" was somewhat conclusory rather than fully re-engaging PRO's funding-source counter (trust/volume/reduced chargebacks), but this gap is smaller than the unresolved contradiction PRO left standing. Overall CON's segmentation argument and contradiction-catching gave it the cleaner, more decisive path through the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.3/B4.3 combination — first exposing the internal contradiction between PRO's heterogeneity argument and its scalability claim, then showing that PRO's later retreat to "branded/high-margin only" authentication is indistinguishable from the current status quo — was the most damaging and unanswered exchange of the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): CON won a close debate by converting the clash into a live economic dilemma PRO never escaped: meaningful expansion of authentication/risk absorption either restates luxury-tier status quo or, down-market on heterogeneous P2P apparel, forces higher fees, swap-fraud exposure, and circular-economy damage. PRO’s “more, not everything” and tiered-risk frame was rhetorically clean and correctly punished maximalist strawmen, but after logistics corrections and answers on photo/local models it largely conceded ordinary low-margin flow stays unchanged while offering thin workable mechanisms beyond that. CON’s fee-pass-through and margin-density weighing stayed decisive through closings.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON Rebuttal 2 (B4.3–B4.4): PRO’s retreat to selective high-margin focus is status quo; further push is either toothless photo theater or crushing logistics/swap risk.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won the definitional clash. Motion says "more" not "all." CON's strongest costs - double shipping on $25 dresses, emissions, pricing out casual sellers - depend on universal central-depot inspection, which PRO disavowed for tiered risk-based authentication on branded/high-risk items. CON never solved A3.1's alternatives (seller vetting, local third-party, flag-based review) and its A1 reply in R2 asserted overhead without proof. CON's margin/verifiability point on swap fraud is strong but only blocks down-market universalism, not PRO's incremental expansion. That leaves PRO's comparative advantage and trust externality routes alive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.2-A2.4 / A4.1-A4.2 flipping heterogeneity and logistics: showing heterogeneous vintage is harder for buyers not easier, and that "more" can be selective review on flagged branded goods without a second shipping leg - directly undercutting B's core unit-economics and emissions case.
