- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but CON built the more resilient structure. CON's "direction of obligation" distinction (external legal remedies against firms vs. self-policing by the same custodian that caused the harm) and the "custody is not letterhead" closing reframe went materially unanswered by PRO, whose closing dismissed the point rather than refuting it. PRO's "capture by neglect" and obligation-vs-product framing were strong but repetitive across rounds and didn't fully neutralize CON's institutional-trust argument. CON's opening was clipped by the word limit, a real execution cost, but CON's rebuttal rounds and closing recovered the lost ground and landed the more decisive final framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's R2 point that PRO's safeguards (descendant boards, tribal vetoes, independent review) amount to plural custody with a state checkbook, paired with the closing's "custody is not letterhead" reframe, most damaged PRO's live route to victory by showing PRO had drifted from defending the motion's actual claim.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by successfully proving that the state cannot be trusted as the primary digitizer and curator of these sensitive records. Side B weaponized Side A's own proposed safeguards (independent boards, tribal archives) to demonstrate that Side A was functionally conceding the danger of state control and adopting B's "plural custody" model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, arguing that every protection PRO offered worked by taking the key out of the state's hand, thereby conceding that the state holding the key was the danger.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by systematically dismantling Side B's (PRO) model and exposing its internal contradictions. CON successfully reduced the clash to whether the state itself holds the digital key, arguing persuasively that state custody freezes official interpretations and relies on internal enforcement, which is weaker than external legal remedies against private firms.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it answered PRO's pressure questions by distinguishing between grants to third-party custodians (funding without state custody) and external legal vetoes against firms, effectively turning PRO's proposed safeguards into concessions of CON's plural custody model.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides built sophisticated, well-matched cases and engaged directly with each other's exact language, producing genuine clash. B's sharpest moment was forcing A to concede in the pressure round that statutory refusals would still block scanning against a named party's wishes, which B then used to argue A's "wholesale by category" framing collapses into something closer to B's own permissioned model. But A's counter-thrust was more damaging to the actual stakes of the motion: B's alternative — a fee-waived individual retrieval right — depends on the claimant already knowing a name, place, and date to request the right file, which fails precisely the populations the motion is meant to serve (renamed adoptees, unrecorded enslaved ancestors, Stolen Generations children stripped of identifying information). B's closing answer to this ("the erased still get a fee-waived search of the box that already holds them") never explains how someone locates an unindexed box without prior knowledge, leaving A's discoverability argument live and largely unanswered. A's Rebuttal 1 being clipped for length is a real execution cost, but the substantive content landed before the cutoff, limiting the damage. On balance A's case better survived the final exchange because its core practical claim (systematic, funded indexing serves the truly erased; ad hoc petition does not) was never squarely neutralized, while B's procedural/consent framing, though compelling, did not fully resolve how its narrower remedy reaches the people with the least documentation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Pressure Questions exchange (A3.1/B3.1) and its answers in Rebuttal 2: B's Q1 forced A to concede refusals limit wholesale scanning, which B weaponized effectively; but A's parallel pressure on "who indexes the box so it's findable" exposed that B's individual retrieval right cannot serve claimants who don't already know what to request, and B never closed that gap.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by systematically dismantling Side B's alternative 'locator service'. Side A convincingly argues that requiring a claimant to provide a name, place, and date inherently fails the most marginalized victims of historical erasure (those given numbers or stripped of surnames), proving that 'kinship is a network' requiring broader indexing. Side A also effectively neutralizes Side B's privacy offense by contrasting the legal safeguards of a public archive with the unaccountable scraping of private genealogy firms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal and closing brilliantly expose the flaw in Side B's targeted search model, pointing out that rationing retrieval by prior knowledge ('a name, place, and date') abandons the 'truly severed' whose names were changed or unrecorded.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B wins by decisively dismantling Side A's alternative model. While Side A mounted a strong philosophical defense of privacy and consent against state overreach, its practical alternative—a petition-based locator service—fatally requires the descendant to already know their name, place, and date. Side B brilliantly exploits this flaw, pointing out that the truly severed lack this exact information, rendering Side A's model useless for those most harmed. Side B's affirmative case, grounded in the state's duty to repair its own paperwork and the market's failure to serve marginalized groups, remained robust and practically superior.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which exposed that Side A's locator service requires prior knowledge of identifying details, thereby excluding the very people whose identities were systematically erased by the state.
