PRO is penalized modestly on grounding for citing nonexistent reference IDs [C1.3, C1.5] in Rebuttal 2, though the underlying claims those IDs pointed to were accurate. On balance, PRO built the cleaner comparative story about why structural change outperforms individual permission, and that story survived the exchange better than CON's autonomy-and-flexibility case.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Both sides were competent and the debate was genuinely close, but CON (Side A) emerged with more live, unanswered arguments by the close. The three most decisive unresolved clashes all tilt CON's way. First, CON's mathematical burnout mechanism—same workload in fewer hours, with staggered skeleton crews absorbing proportionally more work—was never adequately answered; PRO's "radical audit of inefficiencies" response was transparently idealistic and PRO never explained how external client demands shrink. Second, CON correctly exposed a contradiction at the heart of PRO's case: PRO simultaneously conceded that staggered schedules are necessary for client-facing work and claimed the "organizational rhythm slows down," but those two claims are incompatible. PRO's Rebuttal 2 response about "planned, reciprocal" coverage didn't resolve the arithmetic. Third, CON's bespoke flexibility argument (sick Tuesday, three-week medical emergency) was never squarely answered; PRO's only response was "employees are too afraid to use unlimited PTO anyway," which at best shows both policies have problems, not that the four-day pilot is better on this dimension. PRO's strongest asset—the well-grounded empirical claim that employees take fewer days under unlimited PTO—is real and was never fully dismissed, and the guilt-trap framing is sustained and memorable. CON's A1 response (fixable with minimum baselines; different metric) is only partially satisfying. But PRO's own closing concession ("even if the remaining four days are demanding") handed CON a clean crystallizing moment, and on net CON won more of the live mechanical clashes by the end of the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's exposure in Rebuttal 1 of the internal contradiction in PRO's staggering argument: PRO admitted client-facing companies must stagger schedules, yet simultaneously claimed the "organizational rhythm slows down," which is inconsistent. If half the team and all clients are working on your day off, the machine is running at full speed and you return to the same backlog PRO warned about. This directly undermined PRO's central mechanism for why the four-day week produces genuine rest.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): B won by making the core tradeoff clearer: A showed unlimited PTO can be underused and guilt-ridden, but B more effectively pressed that a four-day pilot may compress unchanged work, fail in client-facing settings, and offer rigid rest that does not meet real-life emergencies. A’s audit and capacity-planning answers were plausible but somewhat handwavy against B’s external-demand and coverage objections. B also closed more decisively by crystallizing daily burnout versus flexible autonomy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was the attack on A’s “radical audit” and staggered-coverage model: if external client demands and deadlines remain constant, removing a workday risks compression or shifting burdens, which directly undercut A’s structural-rest mechanism.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B wins: stronger structural argument on cultural failure of unlimited PTO, better answers to pressure questions on coverage and output, clearer comparative weighing that four-day pilots force real systemic change while unlimited PTO remains illusory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.4-B2.6 directly neutralizes A's compression and pilot-anxiety claims while sharpening the retention comparison.
