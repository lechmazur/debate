### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued with discipline and largely avoided fake precision, but B built a more coherent, load-bearing comparative frame: visibility/contestability versus invisibility/erasure of evidence, plus the asymmetric-incentive mechanism, and consistently returned to it across rounds. A's strongest weapon was the Dover example, correctly used to concede that political pressure can be justified—but B's A1 answer turned that concession into a distinction between outcome (correct) and mechanism (indiscriminate), which A never fully dismantled. A's best counter-move was the internal-consistency charge ("you need oversight reliable for falsehood but broken for omission"), and this was a genuinely sharp pressure point, but B's closing partially answers it by noting the asymmetry is about which harm leaves a *trail* even when oversight is imperfect on both sides — A's rebuttal reasserts the tension without fully showing why omission-detection via assessments should be trusted when B has shown fear can hollow those out too. The Pressure Questions exchange favored B: B's Q1/Q2 forced A into a costly concession (Dover counts as political pressure), while A's questions were answered but did not generate equivalent structural damage to B's case. Both closings crystallized existing clash without cheap new material, but B's closing sharpened the "which harm can be fought" framing more memorably and tied it back to A's own concession, while A's closing repeated the consistency charge without fully resolving why detection of omission should be trusted given B's rebuttal. Overall B maintained the more coherent throughline and answered the toughest question raised against it slightly more directly than A resolved the toughest question raised against it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's A1 in the Pressure Questions round, turning A's Dover concession into a distinction between justified outcome and indiscriminate mechanism, which reframed the whole debate and A never fully neutralized.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won by maintaining that political pressure on contested (not merely false) content produces a rational, asymmetric chilling effect that is invisible, unbounded, and self-reinforcing. Side B struggled to show that the content actually policed by such pressure—distinct from ordinary academic review—causes greater harm, and Side A effectively turned Side B's own examples to demonstrate that visible harms can be publicly contested while self-censorship erases its own evidence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal exposing that the same fear silencing teaching also hollows out the assessments meant to detect omissions, and its turning of the Dover example to prove visible content invites public correction while invisible omission does not.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built a more cohesive and persuasive case around the asymmetry of visibility and correctability. While Side A (CON) made strong points about the dangers of entrenched falsehoods and the difficulty of unlearning them, Side B effectively neutralized this by demonstrating that visible harms leave a trail that can be contested (as in the Dover example), whereas decentralized self-censorship destroys the evidence of its own occurrence. Side B's closing crystallized this clash decisively, showing that democracy can correct visible bad content but cannot fight an invisible absence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it brilliantly flipped Side A's Dover example to prove that visible controversial content can be fought and corrected in the open, contrasting it sharply with the uncontestable, unrecorded nature of silent self-censorship.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built the more decisive comparative case by consistently pressing the motion's actual burden: PRO must show self-censorship's harm is *more* serious, yet PRO repeatedly conceded its harm is unmeasured, its motive (fear vs. judgment) indistinguishable from outside, and its effects invisible to the very oversight it relies on as a remedy. CON's "gap vs. falsehood" asymmetry (a thin lesson is fillable; an installed false belief resists correction) was never squarely defeated, and CON's late "collapse" move—that any omission-harm serious enough to matter converts into an affirmative content defect CON's side already owns—forced PRO into a defensive posture it only partially answered in the closing via unelaborated assertion about implicature. PRO's opening and incentive-asymmetry framing were strong and original, and its rebuttals engaged seriously, but its concession that "neither side has established precise frequency" undercut its own comparative burden without recovering ground. CON's case was weakened somewhat by two clipped turns (an execution cost for exceeding word limits), but the substantive content that landed before each clip was sharp and consistently reinforced the winning throughline, giving CON the cleaner, more resolved path to the vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 move turning PRO's own voting-rights example into a dichotomy—either it's a mere gap (repairable, minor) or an affirmative false claim (a content defect, CON's category)—was the most decisive exchange; PRO's closing response (that implicature alone can mislead without an explicit claim) was plausible but arrived too late and undeveloped to fully neutralize it.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully imposing a strict comparative burden and showing that Side A’s harms were either unmeasurable or collapsed into B’s category. B persuasively argued that an invisible, unmeasured harm cannot be ranked above concrete, documented miseducation, and that A’s strongest examples of omission-harm either created mere gaps (easily repaired) or required positive false assertions (content defects review could catch). A’s mechanism of chilling effects was coherent but could not overcome B’s epistemic challenge and the “collapse” rebuttal, leaving A without a clean comparative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal and closing crystallized that the moment A’s omission-harm becomes educationally serious—students believing rights were universal, for example—it transforms into a content defect rather than a distinct self-censorship harm, leaving A with only an unmeasured incentive structure and no unique, demonstrable damage to rank above concrete content harms.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by establishing a more compelling structural mechanism for how self-censorship degrades education quality—specifically by destroying context and the classroom's corrective capacity. While Side A delivered rhetorically sharp attacks regarding the "invisibility" and unmeasurability of self-censorship, Side B successfully parried this by distinguishing between harms that are statistically uncounted versus those that are structurally unknowable, and by clarifying that "specifiable" omissions are not automatically caught by oversight. Side B also decisively won the late-stage clash over whether omissions create false beliefs, effectively separating the cause of the harm (political fear) from its form, preventing Side A from collapsing the PRO case into the CON category.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it dismantled Side A's attempt to collapse omission-harms into content-defects by explaining how students infer misleading pictures from omissions without explicit false claims, and clarified that "specifiable" does not mean "automatically detected by oversight."
