### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): A won a clear but competitive debate. B convincingly showed that content and social context matter, but A conceded that point and argued more successfully about priority: caps address universal displacement harms and remain an enforceable backstop when imperfect content judgments fail. B never fully resolved that asymmetry and repeatedly portrayed limiting toxic exposure as permitting or approving it. B’s distinction between a bedtime and a daily cap was valid, but it did not answer A’s broader dose, inactivity, and implementation arguments.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange answered B’s cyberbullying challenge: a cap is not permission for thirty minutes of harm but a fail-safe when attempts to identify and exclude harmful content inevitably miss. That directly weakened B’s central claim that caps merely budget toxicity, while preserving A’s position that quality guidance remains a supplement.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side B successfully established that simple time caps serve as an essential backstop for dose-dependent harms and parental judgment failures, while exposing that Side A's own proposals implicitly rely on time-based boundaries whenever stakes get real. Side B's "asymmetry" frame—that caps work when parents fail, whereas quality-first advice leaves children exposed when judgment misfires—survived as a live route to victory because Side A never fully answered why exhausted parents can reliably outmaneuver adversarial platform design. Side A's scarcity and false-security arguments were sharp, but Side B effectively turned them by noting that any boundary creates scarcity incentives and that harm reduction through dose-limiting is a feature, not a fatal concession. Side B made the decisive clash clearer across the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's book analogy (B2.1) and the sustained demonstration that Side A "smuggles a clock back in" and renames it "context" whenever their system actually needs to stop harm (B4.5, B4.8, B5.3) was the most damaging exchange, collapsing the supposed distinction between contextual boundaries and time-based rules.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by effectively exposing Side A's reliance on time-based rules (like bedtimes) and framing them as implicit concessions that time limits are necessary. Side B's "backstop" argument—that quality judgments will inevitably fail against adversarial algorithms, making a hard cap a necessary fail-safe for exhausted parents—was highly persuasive and largely unanswered by Side A, who merely repeated that any toxicity is unacceptable. Side B's rebuttals were sharper, more grounded in the reality of parental execution, and rhetorically superior.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's book analogy (noting books naturally end while screens are infinite scroll) and the sharp exposure that Side A's "screens off by 8pm" rule is just a time cap relabeled as "context."
