### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by consistently distinguishing anonymity from mere non-public ownership. That substantially neutralized Side B's lawful-SPV and commercial-privacy harms, while A supplied a plausible benefit through upstream verification, presumptive illegality, and higher friction for illicit capital. Side B's verification-capacity and authoritarian-registry objections remained meaningful, but it repeatedly treated regulator-known ownership as threatened despite A's explicit interpretation, weakening its comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal was decisive: privacy from the public can coexist with disclosure to competent regulators, so most legitimate uses do not require the anonymous structures covered by the ban. Side B's later claim that illegality does not create verification capacity limited A's advantage but did not restore those lawful-use harms.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by effectively establishing and defending the distinction between public anonymity and regulatory anonymity, and by exposing a fatal contradiction in Side B's case regarding international coordination.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's pressure questions and subsequent Rebuttal 2 effectively trapped Side B by pointing out that B's alternative (harder verification and sanctions) required the exact same international coordination that B claimed made a ban impossible.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO won by establishing a clean definitional bright line in its first rebuttal: the motion targets only entities whose beneficial ownership is hidden from regulators, not merely non-public structures. This reframing exposed that CON's opening examples of legitimate privacy and commercial secrecy were largely outside the scope of the ban. CON fought back effectively by arguing verification failures collapse that distinction and that coordination is not fungible, but PRO sustained the comparative advantage by showing its binary test creates upstream gatekeeping incentives and that CON's own alternative requires the same international coordination it declared impossible. PRO's closing crystallized this contradiction crisply, leaving a clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's first rebuttal (B2) distinguished anonymous shells invisible to regulators from merely non-public entities, demonstrating that CON's legitimate-use examples described disclosure-to-authority structures the motion does not touch. This reframing shifted the burden onto CON and created a live route to victory that persisted through the final exchange.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate. B established a genuine risk to dissidents and entrepreneurs, but never explained how its targeted regime could preserve regulator-blind anonymity while reliably distinguishing legitimate users from criminals. A showed that universal disclosure to controlled authorities raises secrecy costs even without perfect compliance and preserves ordinary commercial privacy. B also mischaracterized the Panama and Pandora Papers as breaches of government beneficial-ownership registries, weakening its central safety evidence. B’s clipped closing adds a modest execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest response was that imperfect adoption does not erase harm reduction: market exclusion can make remaining secrecy havens costlier, while B’s supposedly targeted alternative uses the same lever but intentionally preserves the opacity that prevents effective targeting.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully proving a logical contradiction in Side B's alternative. While Side B effectively used MLATs to show the risks of data sharing to dissidents, Side A demonstrated that Side B's proposed "targeted regime" cannot function if it preserves total opacity to regulators, as regulators cannot target what they cannot see. Side A's weighing of systemic, continuous harms against this structurally flawed alternative secured the victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly dismantled Side B's alternative by pointing out that preserving total opacity makes targeted enforcement impossible, leaving the criminal loophole wide open.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON successfully established that the universal ban adds no unique enforcement mechanism beyond correspondent-banking exclusion, which operates identically under a targeted AML regime. The ban therefore sacrifices vulnerable dissidents, whistleblowers, and entrepreneurs for zero additional gain against illicit finance. PRO never decisively rebutted this symmetry and in the closing effectively conceded the enforcement lever was identical while asserting—without sufficient proof—that preserving any anonymity loophole would fatally undermine enforcement. CON’s demonstration that safe-harbor data is reachable via MLATs and diplomatic pressure also undercut PRO’s defense of warrant-gated access more effectively than PRO undercut the zero-additional-enforcement charge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 argument that correspondent-banking exclusion works identically under either a blanket ban or a targeted regime, meaning the ban adds zero enforcement value while stripping vulnerable users of a survival tool they cannot replace ([A4.3]–[A5.2]).
