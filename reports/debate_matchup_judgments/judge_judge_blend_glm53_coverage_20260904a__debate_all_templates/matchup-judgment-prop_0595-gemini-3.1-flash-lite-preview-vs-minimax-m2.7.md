### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side B won by making the key comparison clearer: AI deployment gives employers unilateral power over surveillance, scheduling, evaluation, and termination, and existing after-the-fact law or top-down audits do not give affected workers a pre-deployment voice. Side A had a plausible concern about delay and technical expertise, but overextended by treating negotiation as bargaining over every patch and never fully answered why technical regulation and worker negotiation are mutually exclusive. B also effectively reframed expertise as lived workplace impact rather than code auditing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was answering the patch-gridlock and technical-audit objections: deployment negotiation need not cover routine maintenance, and councils need not audit code to identify harms in scheduling, surveillance, disability impact, or biased evaluation before they entrench.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): Side A wins by building a cleaner live route to victory around democratic legitimacy and proactive harm prevention, while successfully neutralizing Side B's strongest practical objections. A sharply distinguished system deployment from routine maintenance, undermining CON's "veto by friction" and gridlock narrative. A also reframed worker councils as sources of lived-impact expertise rather than technical auditors, which better fit the motion's focus on workplace conditions. B failed to answer A's pressure about what existing mechanism proactively stops harmful deployment before it occurs, essentially conceding the reactive nature of current law. B's closing repeated bargaining-chip and innovation-stifling claims without recovering from A's deployment/maintenance distinction or offering a compelling reason why affected workers should remain entirely outside the decision room.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answer A1 distinguishing substantive AI deployment from iterative security patches and maintenance, which directly dissolved B's gridlock concern and narrowed the clash to the actual scope of the proposition.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): PRO wins by successfully shifting the core clash from technical code accuracy to downstream workplace impact. CON effectively argued that mandated negotiation creates friction and lacks technical expertise, but PRO's rebuttals sharply clarified that the proposal targets substantive deployment—not routine patches—and that worker councils surface lived-experience harms (scheduling penalties, anxiety, deployment bias) that top-down audits and retroactive litigation inherently miss. PRO's pressure questions exposed the gap in CON's reliance on existing law and employer incentives, and CON's responses never fully bridged how technical compliance captures human impact without worker input. PRO's closing crystallized this comparative advantage cleanly, framing the proposition as a minimum democratic check on coercive technology rather than an innovation bottleneck.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) decisively handled CON's pressure by limiting the proposal's scope to substantive deployment changes (neutralizing the gridlock claim) and pivoting the bias clash from training data to deployment choices. By arguing that external audits cannot detect downstream human impacts like caregiver penalties or workplace anxiety, PRO cleanly separated its mechanism from CON's technical oversight alternative and established the decisive comparative advantage.
