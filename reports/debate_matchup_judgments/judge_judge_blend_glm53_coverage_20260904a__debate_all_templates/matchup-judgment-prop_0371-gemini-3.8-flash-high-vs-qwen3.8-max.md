### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but CON (A) built the more resilient path to victory. CON's central economic point—that mandating an included carry-on forces light packers (commuters, overnight travelers, personal-item-only flyers) to subsidize heavy packers via higher base fares—was raised early and never squarely rebutted; PRO repeatedly dismissed it as "economic fiction" without offering a mechanism for why inclusion wouldn't raise the advertised price. CON also offered a coherent alternative remedy (mandatory early, standardized, all-in disclosure plus size limits) that directly answers PRO's deception concern without the downside of a compulsory bundle, and CON handled the pressure-question exchange with reasonably solid, correctly formatted A1/A2 answers, distinguishing preventive fee deterrence at booking from last-minute gate-check triage. PRO's opening and rebuttals were rhetorically vivid ("algorithmic shell game," "captive customer," "phantom fares") and its gate-check question was a genuinely sharp piece of pressure, but PRO's own pressure-question answers (A1/A2 in B4) failed to engage CON's strongest question about fare increases for light packers, merely reasserting "fiction." CON's closing crystallized the decisive tradeoff (commuter vs. mandated roller-bag bundle) more cleanly than PRO's closing, which mostly recapped the humanitarian framing already on the table.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over B3.1/B3.2 (PRO's pressure questions on gate-checking and slippery-slope unbundling) versus A4.1/A4.3 (CON's answers distinguishing preventive booking-stage deterrence from last-minute handling, and defining a "core service" of seat+personal item vs. separable bin space) was the pivotal moment—CON's answers held up under real pressure, while PRO's parallel answer to CON's fare-increase question (B4.2) never supplied a comparable mechanism.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): B won by keeping the decisive clash on compulsory bundling versus choice-plus-disclosure: light packers would pay for unused bin space, base fares would rise, and PRO’s transparency harm is better fixed by clear all-in display than by forcing every ticket to include a roller. A’s essential-necessity and free-gate-check pressure stayed live but never fully neutralized B’s usable-minimum (seat + personal item) frame or the subsidy/regression objection. Later rounds narrowed cleanly toward that tradeoff and B crystallized it more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 A1/A2: free gate-checks show scarcity (not fee irrelevance) while rationing works earlier via disclosed fees; usable core is seat + personal item, so the remedy is display rules not forced overhead inclusion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side B won by exposing that the proposition forces an unnecessary bundle on travelers who do not need a full carry-on, and by showing that transparent all-in disclosure solves the phantom-pricing problem without eliminating cheaper functional tickets. A’s operational critique of gate fees was pointed but never overcame B’s central tradeoff: inclusion raises base prices and shrinks consumer choice, while enforced transparency preserves both honest comparison and the low-cost tier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal that PRO’s baseline is a disguised price increase creating a phantom discount for light packers—forcing backpack-only travelers to subsidize overhead space they never use—reframed the debate and preserved CON’s live path to victory.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly, but CON (A) built a tighter, more damaging line of attack. A's core economic point—that bundling finite bin space into every fare forces light packers to subsidize heavier ones—was never squarely rebutted; PRO's "unused allowance isn't a consumed subsidy" reply ignored that the cost of providing the capacity (not its use) drives the price increase. More decisively, when PRO's rebuttal 2 conceded that bins can still run short and proposed a "fee-free valet check" as the fix, A seized on this as a self-defeating admission: the mandate legally promises carry-on rights that airlines can still revoke at the gate, reproducing the exact chaos PRO set out to cure. PRO never resolved this tension, instead repeating that the baseline "gives one clear standard" without explaining how that avoids the confiscation problem A raised. A also answered pressure questions directly and used vivid, memorable framing ("tragedy of the commons," "legally promised... confiscated at the jet bridge") that sharpened the stakes. PRO's transparency/fragmentation argument remained live but was less decisive than the unresolved operational contradiction A exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's valet-check proposal ([A4.4]), showing that PRO's own fix for bin scarcity undermines its promise of guaranteed carry-on rights and reproduces the very gate chaos PRO claims to solve—an objection PRO never fully answered.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): B won the decisive clash on physical bin scarcity and genuine budget travel. A’s honest-pricing case was coherent, but once A conceded full flights cannot fit every bag and resorted to fee-free gate valet, B showed the mandate still produces boarding friction while pricing finite bin capacity into every base fare and erasing personal-item-only options. That left A defending higher prices without a reliable operational fix; B’s light-packer autonomy and rationing arguments remained live and clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4 turning A’s physical-capacity concession and fee-free valet fix into proof the mandate raises prices yet still yields gate chaos.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing that a reasonable carry-on baseline reflects the true cost of ordinary travel and that fragmentation undermines honest price comparison. Side B landed strong blows on physical scarcity and budget fares, but Side A blunted the cross-subsidy argument by distinguishing the underseat personal item from overhead space, and exposed the weakness in B's disclosure-only remedy. A's operational response—strict size limits and predictable gate-checking rather than surprise fees—preserved a live path to victory despite B's pressure on cabin capacity, and A's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly by framing the choice as real costs versus fragmentation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 together dismantled the subsidy claim by showing that a backpack-only traveler uses the included underseat allowance rather than consuming bin space, and crystallized the transparency advantage by asking why airlines continue to fragment baggage rules if search tools truly solved comparison.
