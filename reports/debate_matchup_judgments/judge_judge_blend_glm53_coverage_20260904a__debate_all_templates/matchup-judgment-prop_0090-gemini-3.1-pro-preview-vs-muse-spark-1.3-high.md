### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A built a tight, consistent case around emergency immediacy, tactile confirmation, and failure isolation, and it landed the debate's sharpest tactical blow: catching B's reliance on steering-wheel switches as an implicit concession that physical, tactile controls are needed for safety-critical functions. B's attempt to escape this ("the wheel point helps us, not them... the motion demands dash buttons") reads as an evasive narrowing of the motion's scope rather than a real rebuttal, since the motion never restricts "physical buttons" to the dash. B's automation-and-redundancy framing was genuinely creative and its "no cable, same body computer" counter to A's isolated-circuit claim was a fair technical push, but it never cleanly resolved the wheel-button concession or the ice/glove override scenario, and its Rebuttal 1 was clipped for exceeding the word limit, an explicit execution cost. A's pressure questions (manual override under ice, bumpy-road tapping) were more incisive than B's, and A's answers to B's questions were direct and on-point. Overall A gave the cleaner, more resolved path to the resolution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's move in Rebuttal 1 turning B's steering-wheel-switch solution into a concession that tactile controls are necessary for safety-critical functions was the decisive exchange; B's later attempt to reframe the motion as being only about "dash buttons" to escape this point read as evasive rather than resolving.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): B kept the decisive clash on eyes-free tactile access and emergency certainty clearer across rounds. A’s automation/redundancy/rental frame was original and competitive, but the clipped first rebuttal plus incomplete answers on repeated visual confirmation and non-automated overrides left B’s core safety route live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B turning A’s steering-wheel reliance into an admission that tactile inputs are required when eyes must stay on the road, then pressing the same gap in the flat-tire/ice and bumpy-road questions.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A built a much stronger comparative case by reframing the debate around system redundancy and automation rather than just the tactile feel of a single button. Side A decisively dismantled Side B's "mechanical fail-safe" argument by correctly pointing out that modern physical buttons are just electronic switches to the same body computer, making them single points of failure compared to the multi-path redundancy of screens, voice, and steering wheels. Side A also effectively neutralized the "muscle memory" claim with the highly relatable rental-car scenario, showing that physical buttons scatter across different models. Side B's reliance on isolated circuits and "glance once" learning felt outdated and poorly defended against A's sharp rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the "isolated circuit" and "mechanical fail-safe" argument, proving that modern physical buttons are merely electronic requests to the same central computer, stripping Side B of its core reliability claim and replacing it with a strong redundancy framework.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A built a consistent, intuitive mechanism (blind findability, deterministic tactile response, reliability precisely when systems are stressed) and sustained it across every round, using vivid concrete scenarios (fogged glass, black ice, screaming kids) to make the stakes concrete. Crucially, A's pressure question forced B to praise steering-wheel stalks and haptic "click" feedback as safer fallbacks — which are themselves physical buttons, undercutting B's central claim that touchscreens/voice alone suffice. B's attempt to parry this in Rebuttal 2 ("stalks aren't dashboard clutter") sidestepped rather than resolved the contradiction, and B's closing never returned to repair it. B's case, while well-organized and technically fluent (automotive-grade OS segregation, offline voice, docked haptic icons, fleet-optimized layouts), leaned on a checklist of "modern tech solves this" assertions that sounded plausible but didn't squarely answer A's cognitive-load-of-voice argument (noise, accents, confirmation loops) or the flat-glass-can't-be-found-blind point. A's closing crystallized the exchange cleanly by pointing back to B's own concession, giving the judge an easy, well-earned reason to side with PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question ("aren't wheel stalks and haptic thumb controls themselves physical buttons?") and B's incomplete answer in Rebuttal 2, which tried to redefine the resolution's scope rather than explain why tactile fallback is safe on the wheel but not the dashboard — a tension A exploited decisively in the closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B built the clearer path to victory by anchoring on worst-case redundancy for core safety functions (blind, instant, no conditions required) and keeping the clash on the proposition as written. B consistently showed automation/voice/docked icons as best-case tools that still need eyes or negotiation, while A's own praise of stalks/haptics conceded the tactile-fallback logic. A was competitive on reach distraction and adaptability but over-relied on dismissing B's scenarios as rare doomsday without neutralizing the live failure-mode comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 pairing: deterministic blind twist beats probabilistic voice loop, plus wheel stalks/haptics prove the tactile need B seeks.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B built a much stronger, more realistic case grounded in the actual conditions of driving (noise, stress, gloves, system lag). Side B successfully trapped Side A when Side A relied on steering wheel stalks and haptic feedback, pointing out that these are themselves physical and tactile controls. Side B's distinction between "best-case" systems (voice, automation) and "worst-case" reliability (physical buttons) decisively won the safety comparison. Side A's reliance on a "phantom doomsday" framing fell flat against Side B's evidence that voice misinterpretations and screen lag are everyday occurrences, not rare edge cases.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side A's reliance on steering wheel stalks and haptics by pointing out that these concessions actually prove the necessity of physical, tactile buttons, while also effectively contrasting the half-second blind reach for a knob with the multi-second cognitive hijacking of voice commands.
