### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) maintained a cleaner through-line across all five rounds: courts are public institutions; the motion's "most proceedings" qualifier already incorporates sensitive-case exceptions; and the live question is who bears the default burden. The "who decides" framing—requiring the scrutinized institution to grant or deny its own oversight—was introduced early and never answered satisfactorily by CON. CON's single strongest move was the spectator-vs-camera distinction: permanent, editable footage with facial identifiers enables doxxing and harassment at a scale no gallery visitor can match. That point landed and forced PRO to partially retreat to a "criminalize misuse" response, which is somewhat deflective. But PRO answered the audio alternative question directly (visual context—posture, composure, eye contact—provides credibility information audio strips away) and correctly noted that adding a visual channel is incremental rather than categorical given audio transcripts already exist. CON's counterclaim—that face identification uniquely enables weaponization—is genuine, but it supports a case-specific exception, not a blanket default against cameras, and PRO's framework already accommodates that exception. CON's empirical claim that "public confidence falls after televised trials" was asserted with no supporting mechanism and was never developed; PRO's competing claim about no measurable harm from three-decades of pilot programs was at least structurally plausible general knowledge. The burden-shifting argument (vulnerable people must prove their case exceptional enough to block cameras) was CON's most original late contribution, but PRO rightly noted the asymmetry cuts both ways under any default rule. On balance, PRO's structural framing and consistent adaptation gave it a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal distinguishing permanent, editable visual footage from transient spectator observation (A2.1) was the sharpest single exchange: it directly dismantled PRO's core spectator-equivalence analogy and introduced the facial-identifier doxxing risk. PRO's answer—any communicated account can be disseminated; criminalize misuse instead—was responsive but failed to close the scale gap. CON landed the point clearly, though it was not decisive enough alone to overcome PRO's broader structural framing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate on several converging grounds. First, PRO's empirical foundation was more concrete: the 40-year state trial court record, the plausible mechanism of judicial deterrence, and the specific Georgia judge example all gave PRO a grounded case that CON never fully dislodged. CON tried to disqualify the empirical record by arguing that mistrials are "the floor of catastrophic breakdown," not the ceiling of behavioral harm, but this created a self-sealing argument—unmeasurable harms are inherently unfalsifiable, which cuts against the side invoking them rather than for it. PRO correctly identified this as a burden problem: CON must show why we should expect the predicted harms, not merely assert that the data is the wrong metric. Second, PRO adequately answered CON's sharpest rebuttal—the "jurors forget cameras / cameras build trust" contradiction. PRO's response in B4.2 was coherent: jurors ignoring the camera and an accurate public record existing are two separate causal channels; the transparency benefit flows to the public watching, not to the jury performing. CON's closing called this a concession, but it was not—it was a genuine resolution of a false dichotomy. CON also argued the Georgia case only proves selective targeted recording works, which was a good point, but PRO could fairly respond that universal default access generalizes the deterrent broadly. Third, and significantly, CON had three speeches clipped for exceeding the word limit—the Opening, Rebuttal 2, and Closing. Each clipping cut off live arguments at critical moments, particularly the Closing, which was severed mid-sentence before the comparative weighing was complete. Per the judging protocol, these are real execution costs. PRO's case was delivered in full across every round, making it easier to evaluate and consistently more complete. CON's best moments—the Georgia reframe and the camera-contradiction gambit—were genuine and creative, but they were either answered or left incomplete, and the pattern of execution failures across three rounds tipped the balance to PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [A2.6] "jurors forget cameras / cameras build trust" contradiction was the most original and pointed rebuttal in the debate. However, PRO's B4.2 answer provided a coherent resolution: jurors ignoring a camera and a public record existing for accountability are different causal channels, so the dichotomy is false. This exchange was the central clash of the debate, and PRO's answer, while not airtight, was sufficient to neutralize the pressure without conceding the core case.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the cleaner comparative case for default transparency with judicial safeguards. Side A had a plausible mechanism that cameras can subtly alter witness, juror, and lawyer behavior, and effectively challenged mistrials as too crude a metric. But A leaned heavily on harms it described as hard to verify, suffered repeated clipping, and did not adequately answer the accessibility and public-accountability benefits. B’s empirical claims were somewhat overconfident, but B paired them with a stronger policy structure: most proceedings, exceptions for concrete harms, and public access as a core legitimacy value. B also defeated A’s “if jurors forget cameras, cameras are inert” dilemma by explaining that public value comes from an accurate record, not from participants performing for the camera.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer to the alleged contradiction between cameras being unobtrusive and cameras building trust was decisive: unobtrusive cameras can preserve fair proceedings while still giving the public a transparent record.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by offering concrete, documented benefits of courtroom cameras—judicial accountability and public trust—while showing that jurisdictions with decades of camera access have not experienced the doomsday outcomes Side A predicted. Side A’s case rested largely on subtle, unmeasurable behavioral harms that conveniently leave no trace in aggregate data, and its sharpest theoretical challenge—the camera-effect contradiction—was cleanly resolved by Side B’s distinction between an untainted jury and an authentic public record. Side A also incurred repeated execution costs from clipped speeches and word-limit violations, while Side B maintained disciplined format compliance and crystallized the clash effectively in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response to Side A’s “forget the camera” paradox in Rebuttal 2: by distinguishing between jurors remaining unperformative (preserving trial fairness) and the camera still producing an accurate, unedited public record (providing transparency value), Side B deflated what appeared to be a decisive contradiction and turned it into a feature of their policy.
