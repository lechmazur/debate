### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): CON (Side A) won on the proposition's own comparative metric: whether controls slow military capabilities more than they harm U.S. innovation. PRO's strongest move—training must precede deployment, so denying training chips chokes the pipeline—was logically sound but incompletely decisive, because CON consistently showed that practical battlefield systems (edge inference, swarm coordination, cyber tools) run on lighter compute already covered by stockpiles and algorithmic gains. PRO never fully neutralized this, and the "stockpiles deplete" counter in B4.1 was asserted rather than demonstrated as decisive against incremental military use cases. More critically, PRO never adequately answered the amortization/revenue flywheel argument: CON's point that China-market revenue is indispensable for amortizing next-node R&D fixed costs, and that India or allied markets cannot replicate its volume or pace, was the clearest live claim at debate's end and went only partially rebutted. PRO's redirection-plus-CHIPS-Act response was structurally weak: the CHIPS Act funds fabs, not the iterative commercial R&D that sets the innovation pace. CON also landed the "complexity cuts both ways" reframe in A4.2, turning PRO's own ecosystem-complexity argument into support for accelerated indigenization. On the relative comparison the proposition demands, CON maintained the stronger comparative case throughout, while PRO's best moments were about the magnitude of the bottleneck, not about the relative balance against U.S. competitiveness harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.2 "complexity cuts both ways" turn: PRO had argued that today's complex chip ecosystem makes catch-up harder, implying a more durable bottleneck. CON flipped this by showing the same complexity makes controls more economically damaging to U.S. firms (higher fixed costs to amortize, narrower revenue base) while simultaneously triggering China's most ambitious all-of-state indigenization effort, making the denial regime self-defeating on the exact strategic metric PRO invoked.

A's case remained coherent but never fully answered the revenue-flywheel harm or overcame B's framing that usable battlefield capability does not require perpetual frontier retraining.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal (B4), which turned A's complexity argument against PRO by showing it accelerates indigenous push while magnifying U.S. revenue loss, and reframed the training bottleneck as porous rather than decisive.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): CON wins by successfully fracturing PRO's core causal link. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (PRO)): PRO built and sustained the stronger path through the full exchange. Its core mechanism—frontier military AI demands tight synchronized compute clusters that embargoed chips deny, compounding delays across generations—was crisp, applied consistently, and tied to a concrete near-term military contingency (a Taiwan scenario measured in days) that grounded the comparative claim in the actual policy stakes. The "asymmetry of dependence" framing held up: China still smuggles embargoed hardware, which PRO rightly weaponized as evidence of current dependency rather than a concession of leakage. PRO's pressure question on smuggling was the sharpest exchange in the debate; CON's answer (smuggling proves porousness and fuels substitution) was creative but required accepting that controls are porous—which undermines CON's own causal story about restrictions driving substitute development. On the R&D harm question, PRO's empirical anchor—frontier releases keep arriving despite restrictions—directly challenged CON's "starvation" narrative, and CON's rebuttal ("spending yesterday's revenue") was interesting but speculative and time-lagged. CON's strongest material was the time-horizon argument: short-term lag for a permanent independent competitor is a bad trade. "Lose the long game to win a few quarters" was genuinely memorable. But PRO's response—physics still punishes non-frontier synchronization and military contingencies are near-term, not generational—was sufficient to keep the motion's comparative claim alive. CON also underused its word budget in the opening and first rebuttal, leaving ground unoccupied that PRO had already staked out. Both sides avoided brittle fake precision and grounded claims in plausible general knowledge. PRO's argument structure was more complete and more consistently directed at the motion's actual comparative question throughout the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question—if Beijing's substitution push is so advanced, why are they still smuggling embargoed H100s rather than deploying mature domestic alternatives?—was the most decisive single exchange. It exposed a real tension in CON's causal story and forced CON into an answer that acknowledged the porousness of controls, weakening CON's claim that restrictions reliably accelerate Chinese self-sufficiency while simultaneously undermining the proposition's benefit.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON)): Side A won because it maintained the decisive asymmetry of dependence: Beijing currently relies on smuggled frontier chips for militarily relevant AI that demands tight synchronization, while U.S. innovation continues advancing on secure domestic and allied demand. Side B never squarely answered why Chinese military AI still smuggles H100s if domestic substitutes are viable, nor did it prove that lost China revenue has actually starved U.S. R&D given continued frontier releases. A’s sharper comparative weighing, direct answers to pressure questions, and concrete military scenarios gave it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2, where it distinguished CEO profit motives from strategic necessity, tied the compute lag to specific Taiwan-contingency timelines, and crystallized that secure allied demand sustains the R&D flywheel without marginal embargoed sales.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON); B = Grok 4.20 0309 (Non-Reasoning) (PRO)): Side B wins by tightly linking the compute lag to decisive military timelines and effectively weighing this against Side A’s long-term industrial concerns. B sharply neutralizes A’s claims about R&D starvation by pointing to continued frontier releases and distinguishing corporate profit motives from strategic innovation health. B also successfully turns A’s smuggling point into proof of ongoing dependency, while A struggles to overcome B’s argument that financial subsidies cannot quickly overcome hard engineering constraints. Side A makes a strong case on long-term self-sufficiency and revenue scale, but B’s comparative weighing on the proposition’s specific trade-off is cleaner and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response to the smuggling and CEO pressure questions, distinguishing quarterly profit warnings from strategic R&D health and using smuggling as proof that domestic substitutes remain inadequate for frontier military workloads.
