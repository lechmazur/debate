  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second-rebuttal turn that Spotify defeated piracy with an affordable all-you-can-eat bundle—the precise model PRO wants to dismantle—while also exposing that PRO's "rights fee correction" is just industry-wide austerity by another name.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B wins because it made the revenue-risk clash more decisive. Side A persuasively argued that bundles overcharge single-team fans and that leagues can redistribute revenue independently of packaging, but Side B answered that redistribution cannot solve a smaller total rights pie. B’s strongest move was using A’s own premise that most fans care about one team to argue that many current bundle subscribers would downgrade, requiring an implausibly large wave of new paying fans to replace lost revenue. A’s choice framing was rhetorically strong, but it never fully answered the collective-action problem: a cheaper narrow pass may be attractive precisely because it avoids subsidizing the broader league ecosystem. B therefore gave the cleaner reason why mandatory bundling can be structurally justified despite consumer frustration.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s rebuttal that existing subscribers would downgrade en masse, shrinking the total broadcast-rights pie before revenue sharing can help small clubs, was the decisive exchange. It directly undercut A’s “just redistribute revenue” and “new non-payers will offset losses” routes.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): CON built the more robust economic framework, showing that team-specific passes would trigger a revenue death spiral: existing single-team subscribers would downgrade en masse, and the hypothetical wave of new low-price customers could not offset the lost aggregate revenue required to meet fixed, multi-billion-dollar league-wide rights commitments. PRO’s strongest conceptual move—separating consumer packaging from league revenue sharing—was answered by CON’s demonstration that broadcasters’ rights bids depend on predictable, pooled subscription pools, so consumer fragmentation necessarily shrinks the total pie. PRO’s music-industry analogy backfired when CON turned it in Rebuttal 2 to show unbundling cratered mid-tier creator revenue for a decade, and PRO never recovered from CON’s physical-scarcity distinction that dismantled the season-ticket comparison. While PRO’s coercion framing remained rhetorically sharp, CON successfully showed that the “choice” was an economically illusional gamble with existential downsides for league parity and small-market survival.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s turning of PRO’s music-industry analogy in Rebuttal 2, showing that unbundling devastated industry revenue and hollowed out the mid-tier, directly analogizing to the destruction of small-market clubs in a fragmented sports ecosystem.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO wins by cleanly separating consumer packaging from league revenue distribution, which neutralizes CON's core parity claim. PRO effectively frames the bundle as coercive and grounds the case in the live reality of cord-cutting and piracy, arguing that tiered passes monetize lost demand rather than simply cannibalizing existing subscribers. CON's economic warnings and sharp rebuttals on digital scarcity and the music analogy were strong, but PRO's closing crystallized the clash more decisively, turning CON's death-spiral prediction into an admission that the bundle cannot withstand open competition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's distinction between how broadcasters package content and how leagues distribute revenue [B2.1], which forced CON onto the defensive shrinking-pie argument and allowed PRO to control the weighing around untapped non-paying demand.
