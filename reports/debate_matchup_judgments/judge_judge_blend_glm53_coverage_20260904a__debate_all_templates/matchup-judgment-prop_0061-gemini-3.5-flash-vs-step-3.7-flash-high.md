PRO's pressure questions were tighter and exposed a genuinely live fiscal burden. The closing crystallization was cleaner on PRO's side. Neither side made a flawless case; CON's specificity on Japan's existing robotics deployment and senior labor participation was stronger than PRO's, but the fiscal core of PRO's argument remained the decisive live route to victory after the full exchange.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins because its core case on immediate, concrete harms—200,000+ unfilled care vacancies and 8,000 rural communities facing abandonment—was never credibly answered by CON. CON consistently responded to these present-tense crises with future-tense automation promises ("robots multiply human capacity," "exoskeletons allow staff to focus on direct care"), which PRO correctly identified as vague and insufficient given the urgency. PRO also effectively reframed "mass immigration" as a strawman by anchoring its proposal to targeted, gap-aligned flows, and PRO's pressure Q1 exposed a genuine gap in CON's model: if a shrinking working-age base must still fund ever-rising pension and care costs for Japan's existing elderly, CON's "stable plateau" is not obviously less vulnerable to the dependency trap than PRO's plan. CON never provided Japan-specific evidence for its social cohesion collapse prediction, relying instead on generic Western analogies that PRO correctly labeled as inapplicable to a targeted program. CON's best single move was catching PRO's concession in B4.1—that temporary visas would be used for high-turnover sectors—and framing this as an admission of a "disposable guest-worker underclass." This is the most damaging rebuttal in the debate, and PRO's closing failed to adequately defend against it. However, CON overstated the contradiction: dual-track immigration with temporary and permanent pathways is a standard policy architecture, not evidence of exploitative stratification, and CON provided no Japan-specific evidence that it generates the social fracturing described. The two execution costs (Side B clipped in both rebuttals) and the unanswered temporary-visa charge keep the margin competitive rather than decisive, but PRO's immediate-harm case and stronger framing of the policy design question carry the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's exploitation in A4.3 of PRO's B4.1 concession on temporary visas—framing it as a retreat from community-revitalizing integration toward a "revolving door of disposable guest workers"—was the sharpest single argumentative move in the debate. It turned PRO's own answer against PRO's stated vision. PRO's closing failed to meet this charge directly, which cost PRO some ground even though CON overstated the significance of dual-track visa policy as evidence of exploitative social stratification.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully established immediate, documented harms—200,000+ unfilled care vacancies and rural abandonment—that CON’s automation-heavy future could not adequately answer, grounding the debate in present-day urgency. While CON landed a sharp rhetorical turn by framing PRO’s temporary-visa language as a retreat to a disposable guest-worker model, PRO’s closing clarified that its approach remained targeted and policy-supported, drawing on existing Japanese successes in Osaka and Tokushima. CON’s social-cohesion and Ponzi-scheme objections were plausible but were sufficiently met by PRO’s stabilization-at-replacement argument and Japan-specific counter-examples. CON’s reliance on speculative automation and per-capita abstractions ultimately felt less tangible than PRO’s concrete harm mitigation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal and closing seized on PRO’s mention of "temporary visas for high-turnover sectors" [B4.1] to reframe PRO as abandoning community integration for a revolving-door guest-worker underclass, directly undercutting PRO’s rural-revitalization narrative.

When pressed on the aging-immigrant burden, Side A conceded reliance on temporary visas and restricted residency to stabilize intake. Side B sharply reframed this as a retreat from genuine integration, arguing it creates a disenfranchised guest-worker underclass that directly undermines Side A's opening vision of community revitalization. Side B also directly answered Side A's pressure for Japan-specific scaling evidence by citing the documented failures of the Technical Intern Training Program, effectively neutralizing Side A's localized examples.
