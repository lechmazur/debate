### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO built the stronger cumulative case through three advantages that survived the full exchange. First, the EU 2022/2024 ISA mandate and commercial fleet precedent in A4.1 directly answered CON's most potent pressure question (Q1: has this ever been deployed at scale?) with real-world evidence, undermining the empirical foundation of CON's crash-risk claim. Second, PRO's key physical distinction — that the override action is identical to standard rapid acceleration, just with mild resistance — was never genuinely refuted. CON's clever pivot in B4.1 to "cognitive overhead" (the quarter-second doubt of "will it let me go?") was the strongest new move in the debate, but PRO had already preemptively addressed it by showing the EU deployment has not produced measurable evasive-crash spikes, which CON never answered. Third, PRO's weighing comparison held: documented speeding fatalities on one side versus a speculative psychological cascade (routine overrides → eroded trust → missed AEB warning → death) on the other. CON's alert-fatigue argument was genuinely original and interesting, and the "trust is not siloed" framing was memorable, but it remained ungrounded in any deployable evidence and was at least partially answered by PRO's distinction between continuous haptic throttle feedback and sudden auditory collision alerts. CON's case was rhetorically sharp and its originality score is high, but originality without empirical anchoring could not close the gap once PRO cited the EU mandate. PRO's closing crystallized the key weighing cleanly; CON's closing was forceful but relied on an unsubstantiated "equally large class of deaths" claim that was never established in the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.1 response to CON's pressure question Q1 was the most decisive exchange. CON's most durable challenge was the empirical gap — has ISA actually been deployed at scale as a default? PRO answered with the EU mandate (plausible and real background knowledge) and commercial fleet precedent, directly removing the epistemic vacuum on which CON's evasive-crash-risk claim depended. CON never responded to this evidence, leaving its core empirical objection materially damaged for the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A won by keeping the debate tied to the proposition: default ISA is overridable, addresses a large baseline speeding harm, and corrects predictable underuse of optional safety systems. B had a strong and vivid objection about misreads, hesitation, and trust erosion, but it relied heavily on asserted psychology and unsupported claims of documented evasive-risk harms. A more directly answered the core friction argument and gave the cleaner comparative weighing between a concrete speed-related safety problem and B's more speculative system-trust risks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure and second rebuttal on the override mechanism were decisive: B conceded there was no distinct physical action, which weakened the claim that ISA creates a dangerous mandatory maneuver in emergencies.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): PRO successfully established that defaulting to ISA overcomes documented behavioral barriers to adoption, while the override preserves autonomy without introducing a novel physical burden.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s pivot in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 that the override imposes a cognitive tax—a quiet split second of hesitation—rather than merely a physical one, and the rebuttal that trust is not siloed to answer PRO’s distinction between haptic and auditory alerts.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won primarily by building and defending a coherent comparative case that Side B never dismantled. The three pillars—hardware already exists, defaults powerfully shape behavior, opt-out preserves choice—were introduced cleanly and survived the exchange. Most decisively, Side A's pressure Q1 ("what number of ISA failure deaths justifies accepting all those confirmed speeding deaths?") was never honestly answered; Side B said "we're not choosing speeding fatalities" but offered no alternative mechanism to reduce them. Side A's throttle-override clarification neutralized the critical-maneuver objection at the rebuttal stage, forcing Side B to retreat to haptic friction intensity—a narrower, less decisive claim. Side B's genuine strengths were the seatbelt-analogy distinction (ISA failures shift risk to innocent third parties) and the Hawthorne-effect / advisory-vs-haptic distinction challenging trial data. Both landed real blows. But Side B's closing still could not answer the fundamental cost-benefit framing: it offered only "leave it optional" without explaining how optional ISA reduces the one-third-of-deaths baseline. Side A's "choose proven lives over theoretical edge-case risk" crystallization was cleaner and the live route to victory remained intact. Side A loses some grounding credit for bold specificity (ten-thousand-hour failure rate, double-digit drops across every rollout) in a closed-book format; Side B was somewhat more epistemically cautious. But Side A's comparative weighing and structural clarity outweighed that cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 on the throttle override [A2.5–A2.6]: by pointing out that every production ISA already allows full-throttle override without menu-diving, Side A materiially damaged Side B's critical-maneuver argument. Side B's response (haptic resistance makes it non-trivial) was plausible but narrowed the claim significantly, and Side A's subsequent "subtle pushback, not a physical block" answer kept the comparative advantage intact across the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won because it kept the debate on the default question: speed is a large recurring cause of harm, ISA is already available, opt out and accelerator override preserve driver control, and defaults plausibly change behavior at low cost. Side B raised real concerns about misreads, haptic friction, and predictability, but relied on worst case phantom braking scenarios and never showed those risks outweigh routine speeding harms or why optional uptake solves them. A's late claims about trial results and precise failure rates were overconfident and discounted, keeping the margin modest.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal was decisive: it reframed B's emergency maneuver argument as assuming a nonoverridable limiter, emphasized throttle override and opt out, and forced a comparison between rare ISA edge cases and recurring speed deaths.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO won by framing the debate as a choice between a massive, known cause of death (excess speed) and a technology already installed in vehicles whose alleged failure modes have not produced the predicted systemic harms in real-world rollouts. CON landed a plausible conceptual blow on systemic risk—phantom braking endangers third parties—but never answered PRO's empirical claim that jurisdictions implementing default ISA saw double-digit drops in fatal crashes without measurable increases in rear-end collisions. CON's response (Hawthorne effect, advisory vs. haptic distinctions) remained speculative and could not override PRO's positive evidence. CON also struggled to sustain its automation complacency and permanent-disablement arguments after PRO's sharp pressure questions, and repeated the haptic-friction point without advancing it. PRO's closing cleanly crystallized the aggregate lives-saved versus theoretical edge-case tradeoff, while CON's closing remained stuck on individual anecdotes. PRO's grounding was weakened by brittle precision on failure rates and trial outcomes without sourcing, but this did not cost them the comparative win because CON could not exploit it with counter-evidence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal neutralizing CON's systemic-risk scenario by asserting that real-world default-ISA rollouts produced double-digit drops in fatal crashes and no measurable rise in rear-end collisions, turning CON's hypothetical phantom-braking pileups into a disproven fear.
