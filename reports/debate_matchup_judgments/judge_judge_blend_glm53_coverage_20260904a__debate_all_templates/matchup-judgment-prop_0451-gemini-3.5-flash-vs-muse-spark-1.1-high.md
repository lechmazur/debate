### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON built a cleaner, more resilient causal story: the stock-vs-flow distinction (installed solar/EVs keep producing regardless of Beijing, unlike gas flows) was a genuinely original framing that PRO never fully dismantled, only asserting "systemic vulnerability" without rebutting the coercion-timing logic. CON's "self-defeating monopoly" point (a price-gouging China would fund its own competitive displacement) and the devastating pressure-question exchange over upstream refining—where CON showed tariffs on finished EVs don't touch lithium refining, and a Polish-made battery still needs the same inputs—went essentially unanswered; PRO's localization-via-tariffs answer didn't address that the refining chokepoint sits upstream of assembly. PRO's opening was strong (state subsidy dumping, dependency risk, political-consent argument), but rebuttals increasingly repeated the deindustrialization thesis rhetorically ("infinite balance sheet," "hollowed-out economy") without engaging CON's mechanism-level counters. CON's closing sharpened the irreversibility asymmetry (CO2 vs. retrainable industry) while PRO's closing mostly recapped without resolving the refining/tariff mismatch or the self-defeating-monopoly point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's answer to Q1 in Rebuttal 2 (A4.1-A4.2), showing that tariffs on finished EVs don't refine lithium and that a monopoly forced to quadruple prices funds its own displacement, directly gutted PRO's core "managed trade solves upstream dependency" claim and was never adequately re-answered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A won by distinguishing manageable supply-chain risk from immediate fossil-fuel dependence and by weighing irreversible near-term emissions against potentially recoverable industrial capacity. Side B offered a credible jobs, sovereignty, and political-consent case, but repeatedly assumed that cheap imports necessarily produce permanent monopoly and deindustrialization. It never squarely supplied the cost, timeline, or emissions tradeoff of its managed-trade alternative. Side A also answered the pressure questions directly, whereas Side B largely reframed the first rather than resolving it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange showed that tariffs on finished EVs and panels do not themselves remove the upstream refining chokepoint, while higher monopoly prices would encourage competing entrants. This materially weakened Side B’s claim that treating finished imports primarily as a threat was the necessary route to resilience.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A won by establishing sharper logical distinctions that Side B could not overcome. A's stock versus flow framing effectively neutralized B's geopolitical dependence argument, and A's dismantling of the predatory pricing myth using the learning curve and global re-entry dynamics was highly persuasive. Crucially, A exposed a fatal flaw in B's policy logic during the pressure questions: treating finished goods as the primary threat via tariffs does not solve the actual upstream mineral refining chokepoint, a point B failed to adequately resolve.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, specifically the direct answers to the pressure questions, where A dismantled B's claim that finished-good tariffs would solve upstream refining monopolies. A sharply noted that a tariff on a finished EV does not refine lithium, and that high-wage transition jobs in installation and grid software are unfairly dismissed by B as low-margin work.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B constructed a tighter, more concrete comparative story: the solar-industry precedent, the 13M-jobs stake, and the claim that Europe's real bottleneck is grids/permitting/refining rather than sticker price. A's best moment was the durable-asset-vs-consumable-fuel distinction undercutting the Russian gas analogy, but A never rebutted B's specific and repeated claim that Chinese EVs are landed in Europe above China-domestic prices (so tariffs hit exporter margin, not consumers), which is the load-bearing premise of A's entire affordability/backlash argument. B also used the Pressure Questions round more effectively, forcing A into a vague answer about "diversifying raw material partnerships" on refining dependency, while B's own answers (A1/A2) directly engaged with tariff-revenue recycling and the US 100%-tariff comparison. B's closing crystallized the clash ("cheap today, dependent tomorrow") more sharply than A's closing, which mostly restated earlier framing without closing the pricing-margin gap. The result is a clear, competitive win for B on rebuttal quality and unaddressed pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's repeated claim that landed Chinese EV prices are marked up above China-domestic prices (so the affordability premise is weaker than A claims) went unanswered through both of A's later speeches, materially damaging A's core political-backlash argument.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making threat-first compatible with decarbonization rather than conceding Side A’s protection-versus-climate binary. Its localization, industrial capacity, supply-chain leverage, and political-backlash case created a stronger long-term comparison. Side A compellingly distinguished durable clean-tech from consumable Russian gas and explained how affordability can induce infrastructure, but it repeatedly characterized calibrated trade defenses as closed-loop scarcity without fully answering B’s concern that import dependence erodes Europe’s capacity and climate coalition. B’s unsupported precise figures and claims about tariff incidence narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over whether threat-framing necessarily makes the transition unaffordable. B answered that calibrated anti-subsidy measures can force localization while preserving jobs and funding affordability measures; A largely returned to the broader claim that all protection produces scarcity. Although B overstated how painlessly tariffs would be absorbed, it more directly reconciled industrial resilience with climate deployment.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B built a more robust and contextually grounded case. B effectively neutralized A's "durable asset" and "speed" arguments by identifying the true bottlenecks of the green transition (grids, infrastructure) and highlighting the ongoing supply chain leverage China holds over scaling, refining, and replacement. B's pressure question regarding the timing of Chinese FDI in Europe (surging after threat-framing) was particularly decisive, exposing a flaw in A's claim that opportunity-framing naturally attracts localization. B also successfully flipped A's political backlash argument by emphasizing the electoral danger of deindustrializing a 13-million-job sector, making the comparative stakes much clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it answered A's pressure questions by explaining that countervailing duties hit exporter margins rather than consumers, and that threat-framing is necessary to prevent the EU from becoming the sole dumping ground as the US closes its market, thereby forcing the very localization A claimed opportunity-framing would achieve.
