### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON built a tighter, more consistently pressed case around two linked ideas: the "birth lottery" regressivity objection (vividly illustrated by the two-engineers-same-income example) and the strategic move that, since both sides concede public care is guaranteed immediately, the levy accomplishes nothing but unjust reimbursement allocation ("the concession decides the debate"). PRO's rebuttals to these points—arguing that identical incomes needn't imply identical responsibilities because of a "particular relationship"—were coherent but largely reasserted the reciprocity premise rather than defeating the unfairness intuition or explaining why a duplicate, birth-contingent levy is preferable to ability-based taxation alone. PRO's own strongest example (the free-riding sibling) was only weakly engaged by CON in the opening and effectively neutralized by CON's later framing that fairness among siblings isn't a matter courts should adjudicate. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format correctly with A1/A2 labels, but CON's answers reinforced its central frame more effectively, while PRO's answers, though thorough on abuse safeguards, remained abstract on the core redundancy/regressivity charge. CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("it only decides who reimburses the treasury afterward"), while PRO's closing restated its position without fully closing the gap CON had opened.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's "concession decides the debate" move in Rebuttal 2 (A4.3), leveraging PRO's own admission that public care is guaranteed either way to reduce the whole dispute to an unjust birth-based reimbursement scheme, was the most damaging and clarifying exchange; PRO's closing response conceded the guarantee point without fully rebutting the reframing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B successfully leveraged Side A's concession—that the state would guarantee immediate care regardless—to reduce Side A's impacts to mere treasury reimbursement. Side B then outweighed this by persuasively arguing that the reimbursement mechanism unfairly taxes the children of the poor (the "two engineers" example) and forces abuse victims to recount their trauma to bureaucrats.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 response distinguishing child-to-parent obligations from other family duties by pointing out that spousal and child support stem from voluntary undertakings, whereas a parent's care was discharging an assumed duty, not creating a debt.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully narrowing the clash to cost-shifting rather than care provision. By establishing early and reinforcing late that both sides guarantee immediate care, Side A stripped Side B of its strongest moral urgency, reducing the proposition to an intrusive, state-enforced reimbursement scheme. Side A's "two engineers" example effectively highlighted the "birth lottery" regressivity, and Side A's distinction between voluntary child support and non-consensual parental care neutralized Side B's reciprocity argument. Side B offered strong procedural safeguards and a philosophically consistent defense of particularized obligations, but could not overcome Side A's superior weighing of the practical and emotional costs of auditing family trauma merely to save the public purse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they seized on Side B's concession that care is guaranteed in both models to reframe the entire debate as pure cost-shifting, making the intrusive audits and "birth lottery" taxation seem wholly unjustified.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON built a tighter, more sustained logical case around the "arbitrariness" problem: two equally wealthy, equally-benefited adult children could face wildly different legal liabilities based solely on whether their parent happened to save money, a distinction PRO never squarely resolved (its closest answer, "responsibility is selective," restates the conclusion rather than defeating the counterexample). CON's pressure questions also did real damage: forcing PRO to concede that the state must guarantee uninterrupted care regardless of family liability defused PRO's central eviction narrative, and forcing PRO to concede that unresolved abuse allegations default to public funding revealed that the "family pays first" mechanism carves out significant exceptions in practice. PRO's rebuttal to the consent objection (no one consents to taxes, spousal duties, or child support either) was genuinely strong and blunted one CON line, and PRO's "dutiful daughter pays twice" and "subsidized abandonment" framing were rhetorically vivid. But by the closing, CON had reduced the dispute to a single clean question—does receiving childhood care create a collectible debt triggered by parental poverty—and PRO's closing reasserted moral force without closing that specific logical gap. CON's closing more cleanly crystallized the remaining clash and left the decisive comparative question in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure questions (A3.1/A3.2) and PRO's forced answers (B4.1/B4.2) were decisive: PRO's concession that the state must guarantee care during disputes and that unresolved abuse claims default to public funding undercut the urgency and practical bite of PRO's core "family pays first" mechanism, which CON then exploited effectively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by cleanly neutralizing Side A's emotional impacts and narrowing the debate to a precise clash over taxation vs. debt collection. By forcing Side A to concede in the PQ round that care must be guaranteed during liability disputes, Side B successfully removed the threat of "eviction" from Side A's side of the ledger, exposing the proposition as merely an extra reimbursement mechanism that penalizes adult children for their parents' misfortune.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 flawlessly leveraged Side A's PQ answers: "PRO must justify that allocation, not borrow urgency from an eviction its own guarantee prevents."
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by systematically dismantling Side B's (PRO) urgency argument. By forcing PRO to concede in the pressure questions that the state must guarantee uninterrupted care during liability disputes and for edge cases (like unprovable abuse), CON successfully narrowed the debate to whether the state should act as a retroactive debt collector for a moral obligation. CON's "two wealthy adults" thought experiment effectively highlighted the arbitrariness of billing adult children based on their parents' savings rather than their own overall capacity. Furthermore, CON's reframing of progressive taxation as a fairer alternative to a kinship-based surcharge neutralized PRO's "free rider" and "dutiful daughter" concerns. PRO argued the moral case for reciprocity well, but failed to overcome CON's logical proof that parenting is a non-consensual duty rather than a loan, leaving PRO's collection mechanism looking arbitrary, intrusive, and redundant.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they capitalize on Side B's concession to guarantee interim care, stripping away the "eviction" urgency and exposing PRO's case as a mere retroactive collection mechanism that arbitrarily penalizes adult children for their parents' financial misfortune.
