### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON's rebuttals did more damage to the motion as written. CON's sharpest move was catching PRO's drift from "much more" investment (the motion's own language) to "three or four deferred fighters" — a concession CON exploited relentlessly, arguing that a sum small enough to be a "rounding error" cannot satisfy the proposition's demand for major reallocation, while a sum large enough to matter would gut the deterrence PRO itself relies on. CON also resolved PRO's "either serious or priced-in" pressure question with a coherent threat-differentiation framework (market prices commercial/accidental risk; military capability answers state sabotage), which PRO never fully dismantled. PRO's mechanism for why hardening "enables" naval interception (raising attacker cost by requiring more vessels) was met with an unanswered rebuttal ("two covert ships are as silent as one"), and PRO's closing—where a rebuttal to this point should have landed—was clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost that let CON's last word stand. CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly and landed the more memorable final framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1/B4 sequence, which turned PRO's own "complements, not substitutes" framing against it and forced PRO into naming a concrete, tiny reallocation ($300M/3-4 fighters) that undercut the motion's demand for "much more" investment — a concession PRO never recovered from, compounded by PRO's clipped closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): A narrowly won by showing that resilience complements deterrence: diverse routes and hardened nodes force an attacker into a slower, larger, more detectable operation. B never established why shifting a modest sum from fighters necessarily weakens the naval capabilities it emphasized, and its market argument inadequately addressed national-security externalities. B landed a strong scale challenge, while A's clipped closing and uncertain package costing narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's direct answer to B's survival challenge was decisive: protection need not be invulnerable if it raises the number of simultaneous cuts, time, coordination, and exposure required. Naming several deferred fighters also answered the tradeoff demand, while B mostly asserted that this protection was decorative rather than disproving its layered value.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by effectively exposing Side A's retreat from "much more" investment to a $300 million rounding error, which fatally undermined the proposition's core demand. Side B also decisively won the tactical clash by demonstrating that physical hardening does not defeat covert state actors (who can simply use more silent ROVs), whereas the naval presence and sensors Side A proposed defunding are what actually provide detection and deterrence. Side A's clipped closing further cemented Side B's advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side A's $300 million concession, showing it was either a rounding error that proved the status quo was fine, or insufficient to stop a peer state's covert campaign, thereby collapsing Side A's cost-asymmetry argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): A built a more granular causal case (asymmetric cost, correlated vs. uncorrelated failure, market redundancy clustering on profitable routes, deniability defeating attribution-based deterrence) and then executed a decisive move in Rebuttal 2: forcing B's own threat taxonomy (harmless gray-zone probing vs. alliance-deterred total war) to show the frigate does no unique work in either bucket. B never fully rebuilt a mechanism for how visible presence deters specifically deniable sabotage, instead asserting "presence deters escalation" without engaging the attribution problem A raised. B's strongest surviving point—that hardened cable infrastructure still needs safe seas for repair ships to operate—was raised early, met with a direct (if thin) reply from A (A4.6), and reasserted in B's closing, but by then it was one surviving thread against several fronts A had already won. A's opening being clipped for length is a real but minor execution cost, and A's own cost-scaling answer to B's pressure question was a bit hand-wavy ("that's a debate about scale"), but overall A's argument architecture, adaptation across rounds, and framing of the decisive question were sharper and more damaging to B's live case than B's counters were to A's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 move (A4.5) turning B's own gray-zone/total-war dichotomy against B — showing the frigate does no unique work in either category — was the most decisive exchange; B's response in Rebuttal 2/Closing reasserted presence-deterrence and the "undefended water" point but never fully repaired the framework damage.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A won by showing that commercial redundancy is often optimized for profit rather than strategic dispersion and that deniable sabotage weakens the deterrent value of visible forces. Side B raised legitimate cost and escalation objections, but never established how marginal surface procurement reliably prevents covert cable attacks. A's clipped opening and loose cost comparisons narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange was the deniability turn: redundancy limits damage without attribution, while Side B's frigate-based deterrence depends on adversaries expecting detection or escalation. Side B's answer that presence keeps probing harmless remained more asserted than demonstrated.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A wins by decisively dismantling Side B's deterrence logic. A forces B to concede that visible naval power cannot deter deniable, gray-zone cable sabotage, and that all-out war is already covered by broader alliance commitments. This leaves B's "frigate" doing no unique work against the most likely threat. B's retreat to claiming gray-zone attacks are merely "headlines without damage" allows A to brilliantly frame the stakes as a "cumulative erosion of confidence" that redundancy solves but surface ships cannot. A's rhetoric ("paying fleet prices for a job his fleet cannot perform") crystallizes the clash perfectly. B's "undefended water" point is strong but fails to overcome A's practical point that peacetime repair ships do not need destroyer escorts. A's minor clipping at the end of the opening did not materially harm the core case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where A answers the pressure questions and exposes that B's threat model leaves the frigate doing no unique work against either deniable gray-zone probing or all-out war, effectively neutralizing B's core deterrence argument.
