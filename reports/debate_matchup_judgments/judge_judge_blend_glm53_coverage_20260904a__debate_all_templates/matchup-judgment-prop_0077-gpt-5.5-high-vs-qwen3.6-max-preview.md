### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Its most decisive achievement was maintaining the civil-versus-criminal-warrant distinction throughout, a distinction CON never genuinely neutralized. CON's core framing—"dangerous offenders" shielded by sanctuary—depended on treating civil detainers as equivalent to criminal warrants, but PRO consistently pressed that when charges are dropped or sentences complete, local criminal authority is lawfully exhausted, and federal officers retain their own independent tools. CON never found a satisfying answer. PRO's trust mechanism was also more durable than CON's rebuttals suggested: the causal story that potential victims decide whether to call 911 based on perceived local police behavior is coherent and well-grounded in how domestic violence, trafficking, and gang intimidation cases actually generate evidence. CON's A2 counter—that U-visas and T-visas replace victim cooperation—was effectively turned in PRO's closing: those instruments only help after a victim has already entered the system; they do nothing to make someone pick up the phone in the first place. CON never answered that point. CON had real moments—the "manufactures friction" line was sharp, and the recidivism gap is a genuine concern—but its case relied on asserting that removability equals dangerousness and that a detainer is functionally equivalent to a warrant, both of which PRO kept contesting successfully. PRO's word-limit violations (multiple clips) are a real execution cost and prevent a larger margin, since we cannot fully assess what was cut. Still, the visible portions of PRO's arguments consistently outperformed CON's responses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing rebuttal of CON's U-visa/T-visa mechanism was the most decisive exchange. CON introduced these tools in A4 as a concrete replacement for victim cooperation in vulnerable communities, which was CON's best answer to PRO's trust argument. PRO's closing immediately identified the flaw: these visas only assist victims who have already entered the system; they do not determine whether a battered spouse or trafficking victim picks up the phone in the first place. CON had no remaining speech to answer this, and it left PRO's foundational trust claim standing while CON's best counter was exposed as addressing a downstream stage rather than the threshold decision that sanctuary policy is actually about.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully decoupling 'dangerousness' from 'removability.' Side A builds a strong mechanical case around the necessity of detainers to bridge custody gaps, but Side B effectively flips this by pointing out that holding individuals who have completed sentences or had charges dropped—without a judicial warrant—is an administrative shortcut, not a local safety imperative. Side B also provides a compelling defense of the trust mechanism by demonstrating that U-visas cannot overcome the fear of making the 'first call' if local police are viewed as immigration intake.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal (B4.4) brilliantly flips Side A's focus on dropped charges and completed sentences, pointing out that if the local criminal system no longer has a lawful basis to hold someone, inventing local detention power for civil immigration purposes is exactly what poisons community trust.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A won by establishing that sanctuary policies preserve the foundational trust required for local policing, particularly for crimes like domestic violence and trafficking where victim reporting is essential. While Side B presented a coherent case about detainers and interagency coordination, A successfully distinguished between criminal public safety and federal immigration enforcement, showing that B's "measurable prevention" largely amounted to administrative convenience for federal removal rather than local crime reduction. A's closing effectively undermined B's answer to the trust question by noting that U-visas and confidentiality protocols only operate after a victim enters the system, leaving B with no substitute for the "first call." A's institutional competence framing—that city police should solve crime rather than serve as a backup detention arm for civil immigration—provided the cleaner comparative weighing. Side A incurred execution costs from three clipped speeches, but the visible material was sufficiently complete to sustain its route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing rebuttal of B's U-visa answer, demonstrating that post-entry legal protections cannot replace the initial willingness of victims and witnesses to contact police, thereby exposing a gap in CON's alternative mechanism.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): PRO (Side B) edges this competitive debate primarily through the "empty pipeline" argument: you cannot weigh the efficiency of a jailhouse handoff against crimes that were never reported because fear silenced the victim. This strikes at the root of CON's case rather than just disputing its scope, and CON never fully neutralized it. PRO's "ecosystem vs. discrete transactions" framing in Rebuttal 2 is the clearest comparative weighing in the debate, and the A1/A2 responses were direct and substantive. CON's strongest contributions—the complainant/suspect distinction, the "certainty also protects offenders" point, and the domestic-violence-survivor scenario—are all legitimate and landed with some force. The targeted trust-building alternative is genuinely compelling, and CON correctly identifies that PRO's demanded certainty also benefits criminal suspects. However, PRO's cross-arrest reality response to CON's Q1 showed the proposed middle ground is harder to operationalize than CON claimed, and PRO's core causal logic (broken trust drains the pipeline that generates the arrests CON wants to facilitate) remained standing at the close. CON's arguments were hampered by multiple clippings, a real execution cost. The margin is clear but modest because CON's alternative framing was coherent and pressured PRO throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 in Rebuttal 2: "You cannot weigh a custody handoff against a crime that was never reported. Your model optimizes the back end of an empty pipeline." This directly collapsed CON's enforcement-efficiency argument by showing it presupposes the reporting infrastructure that sanctuary policies are designed to preserve, and CON's response—that the middle ground maintains both—required the practical coherence of the complainant/suspect split, which PRO had already challenged with the cross-arrest reality.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side A won by successfully carving out a middle-ground framework that absorbed Side B's best offense (protecting victims and witnesses from status checks) while isolating the specific overreach of sanctuary policies (shielding lawfully booked offenders from federal notice). Side B struggled to adequately defend why providing basic notice of release for serious offenders outweighed the concrete dangers of releasing them back into the community.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in A4 that cross-arrests in domestic violence cases are a reason to improve local policing practices, not a justification to refuse all federal cooperation for legitimately booked violent offenders.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins because it fractures Side A's case with a narrower alternative: targeted protections for victims and witnesses capture the trust benefits without shielding arrestees and creating enforcement gaps. Side A never proves that trust requires noncooperation for arrestees rather than merely non-interrogation during initial contact; Side B's distinction between complainants and suspects holds up under pressure and exposes Side A's overreach. Side B also lands the sharper weighing on released-offender risks, while Side A's ecosystem argument, though evocative, assumes without proving that the narrower policy would collapse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.3), where it demonstrates that the victim/arrestee distinction is credible, that cross-arrest risks should be fixed through better policing rather than blanket noncooperation, and that Side A's 'certainty' standard protects offenders rather than witnesses.
