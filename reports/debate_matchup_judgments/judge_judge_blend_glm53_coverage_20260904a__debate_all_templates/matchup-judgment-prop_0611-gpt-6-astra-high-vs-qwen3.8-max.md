- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides ran a coherent, plausible strategic case without fake sourcing, and the pressure questions genuinely targeted the crux (whether permanence adds anything beyond dispersal/readiness, and what stops an incident from forcing an escalate-or-retreat choice). CON's line was tighter across rounds: it turned PRO's own "closing window" logic back on PRO ("a standing forward posture is even more destabilizing because it places NATO's tripwire directly on the line before any political decision has been made"), and its rebuttal that layered denial measures both prevent seizure and that permanent bases "can be overrun or struck first" directly undercut PRO's central "reversal dilemma" motivator. PRO's answers to the pressure questions were serviceable (the availability-vs-survivability distinction was a real point) but the threshold answer was vague and largely rhetorical, and PRO's closing repeated the "complements, not substitutes" line without directly rebutting CON's sharper "case collapses" framing. CON's closing ("front-loaded danger," "decision space with worse decisions" turned back at PRO) crystallized the clash more cleanly and left fewer open threads.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Turn 4 point that "if visible movement is destabilizing, then a standing forward posture is even more destabilizing because it places NATO's tripwire directly on the line before any political decision has been made" [A2.5], reinforced by CON's answer in Turn 8 that permanent bases don't resolve PRO's own reversal-dilemma scenario since they too can be struck or overrun first, was the most damaging unanswered turn in the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by decisively distinguishing between "availability" (which requires permanence) and "survivability" (which requires dispersal), effectively nullifying Side A's claim that permanence is nonessential. Side B's weighing of the "reversal dilemma" (retaking territory under nuclear threat) against immediate escalation friction was clear and largely unmitigated by Side A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 clearly explained that permanent basing and dispersal are complements solving different problems, shutting down Side A's attempt to frame them as mutually exclusive.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the decisive comparison live: permanent forward combat power reduces the occupation-then-reversal-under-nuclear-threats dilemma, while CON’s reinforcement alternative retains a dangerous window. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON's decisive move was isolating the actual crux early: PRO's evidence (command nodes, depots, air defense, rapid response) proves the value of readiness and supporting infrastructure, not specifically the value of permanently garrisoning additional troops versus ready rotational forces using the same infrastructure. CON pressed this distinction relentlessly through rebuttals and pressure questions, forcing PRO onto the defensive about marginal benefit. PRO's best material—denial logic against a fait accompli and the "use-them-or-lose-them" reversal on reciprocal alerts—was strong in Rebuttal 1, but its Rebuttal 2 answers (continuity claims, and the assertion that defensive/hardened postures simply stay below the escalation threshold) were largely conclusory and never squarely refuted CON's repeated point that conventional first-strike pressure can arise even from defensive concentrations. The clearest moment of the debate was CON's closing catching PRO's "CON offers resilience later" framing as a strawman, since CON had explicitly built in ready, in-theater forces from the opening—this crystallized that PRO had not actually established the marginal case the motion requires (permanence specifically, not readiness generally, justifying the extra escalation risk). PRO never cleanly recovered from that isolation of the crux, so CON's comparative framing controlled the debate by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 move distinguishing "permanent basing" bundle from the actual disputed increment (extra permanently garrisoned troops vs. supporting infrastructure/ready rotational forces), reinforced by CON's closing catch of PRO's "resilience later" strawman.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A won by successfully unbundling the concepts of 'forward presence/infrastructure' and 'permanent garrisoning.' While Side B argued powerfully against an empty frontier, Side A effectively demonstrated that their alternative (ready rotational forces already in theater with hardened infrastructure) captured most of Side B's defensive benefits without the same marginal escalation risks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and Closing effectively neutralized Side B's core offense by pointing out that Side B was improperly crediting permanent expansion with the entire benefit of immediate defense, which rotational forces already in-theater also provide.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): A won by holding the motion’s actual burden: prioritize permanent basing even given escalation costs, not merely prove NATO needs immediate local denial. A repeatedly unbundled permanence from enabling infrastructure and ready in-theater forces, forcing a true marginal comparison PRO never fully met. B’s deterrence story and continuity advantages were real and well argued, but too often credited permanence with the whole benefit of fighting on day one and still framed A’s alternative as delayed rescue—an attack A’s closing cleanly neutralized. After full exchange, A left the cleaner live route: fund survivable fighting power first; permanent expansion must earn priority rather than inherit it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s sustained unbundling (especially A2.1/A4 and the closing): PRO keeps awarding permanent garrisons every readiness benefit available under resilient denial with ready rotations, so the priority claim fails once that inflated comparison is removed.
