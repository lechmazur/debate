### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but CON built a cleaner, more sustained structural weapon: the dilemma that if the family veto genuinely remains free, gains must be marginal (undercutting "measurable increase"), and if refusals fall because saying no now defies a state-set default, that drop is evidence of pressure (undercutting "no added distress/violations"). This dilemma was introduced in the opening, sharpened after PRO's attempted dissolution in Rebuttal 1, driven home via pointed pressure questions, and never fully escaped by PRO, whose answer ("framing corrects a systematic misreading of a pro-donation majority's silence") is plausible but largely asserted rather than defended against CON's specific point about unreached minorities, access barriers, and mistrust. CON's infrastructure argument (Spain's outcomes reflecting coordinator systems more than the legal default) also tracks real-world policy nuance better than PRO's causal attribution to the law itself, giving CON a slight edge in grounding. PRO's rebuttal that opt-in itself misreads majority preference is a genuinely strong counter-frame and its closing image (forcing a grieving mother to authorize cutting) is rhetorically effective, but it doesn't fully resolve the conjunctive burden the motion demands — measurable gain AND no added harm — leaving CON's bind as the last standing, unresolved tension.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's "fewer refusals under an easy veto" claim — reframing it as "proof of pressure, not proof of better consent" — crystallized the central dilemma that PRO never fully dissolved, even in its closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by establishing a devastating dilemma: either the opt-out default leaves families with a truly effortless veto (yielding only marginal gains) or it relies on state-backed pressure at the bedside (increasing ethical violations and distress). Side B's attempt to resolve this by claiming the change simply normalizes the conversation is masterfully trapped by Side A as being a form of ethically significant bedside pressure. Side B functionally concedes this in closing by admitting exhausted families 'go along' with the expectation, violating the prompt's strict burden of no added ethical harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1 response in Rebuttal 2, which flawlessly explains how framing donation as a state default turns a protected choice into an act of opposition, converting B's 'better conversation' into proof of pressure.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): B won by keeping the dual burden of the proposition live: measurable transplant gains for most patients and no added ethical harm or distress. The recurring soft-veto vs. pressure dilemma forced A into a choice A never fully escaped—either family objection remains easy (so gains look like modest conversational nudges against a hard medical bottleneck) or refusals fall because saying no means opposing a state default at the bedside (ethically loaded). A’s referral-and-framing story was coherent and its guilt/authorization rhetoric was sharp, but it did not cleanly bridge converted conversations to transplant rates for most patients without relying on that contested social pressure. B’s answers and closings repeatedly crystallized that unmet burden more clearly than A dissolved it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s sustained soft-veto dilemma (esp. B2.3/B4.1–B4.2): if families retain an easy veto, measurable transplant gains stay weak; if refusals fall under a legal presumption, the gain is ethically tainted pressure rather than free consent.
