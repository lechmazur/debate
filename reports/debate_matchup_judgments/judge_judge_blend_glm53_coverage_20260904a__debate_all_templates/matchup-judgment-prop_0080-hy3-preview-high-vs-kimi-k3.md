### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): CON built a sharper, more structurally grounded case by reframing the motion around the necessary legal predicate of collective bargaining: employee status, and the automatic costs (termination exposure, taxation, Title IX, caps) that follow regardless of what a CBA "can" negotiate. PRO's rebuttals largely reasserted that a CBA "can" enshrine protections without ever specifying what athletes concede in return, and CON's pointed question on concessions (Q2) was answered only with vague appeals to "stability," which CON correctly exposed as evasive in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing. CON's "both roads run through Congress" reframe—conceded by PRO's own "just as easily" line—cleanly isolated the actual point of difference (employee reclassification costs) and PRO never neutralized it. PRO's answer that bargaining units can be "subdivided" to preserve revenue-athlete control also inadvertently conceded the very fragmentation PRO's opening condemned, which CON immediately flagged. Both sides followed the Q&A format correctly, but CON's substantive answers were more responsive and load-bearing, while PRO's were more evasive on the decisive concession question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 point that PRO's benefits are all contingent ("can" enshrine) while CON's costs are structural consequences of employee status, sharpened further when CON's Pressure Question forced PRO to name a concession and PRO's answer (mutual "stability") failed to identify any real trade-off—an evasion CON exploited through the rest of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won decisively by controlling the comparative framework. Once PRO conceded that both systems require an act of Congress for national uniformity, CON successfully proved that statutory baselines with binding arbitration provide the same enforcement without the structural harms of employee classification (taxes, termination risk, caps) that PRO's collective bargaining model requires. PRO's late rebuttals relied heavily on mischaracterizing CON's arbitration as "non-binding" and "school-run," which CON easily dismantled.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 expertly dismantled PRO's pressure questions by pointing out the false premise of "non-binding" arbitration, explaining judicial enforceability, and exposing that PRO's answers regarding sub-units and concessions functionally conceded CON's points on fragmentation and caps.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): CON won by turning PRO's "can enshrine" benefits into contingent promises while costs of employee status are structural and pre-bargaining. B pressed the unit-voter problem, the inevitable cap concession, and taxable/termination/Title IX costs that A dismissed as "speculative" or never answered, and offered a clean comparison: both paths need Congress, but a federal baseline with binding independent arbitration gives court-enforceable rights without reclassification. A kept re-describing that as "non-binding school-run compliance" after B corrected it and never named a concession for floors, leaving its live route weakened.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1 asymmetry attack — every PRO protection is a "can" versus CON's structural costs from employee status, plus B1.5/B2.6 point that national uniformity needs Congress either way so baseline with binding arbitration achieves enforcement without firing, taxing, and capping athletes.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO built a tighter, more internally consistent case: it narrowed the resolution to schools already sharing revenue, used the "floor not ceiling" framing to neutralize CON's recurring objections (non-revenue athlete harm, NIL siphoning, gymnast market value), and repeatedly forced CON to answer a single decisive question—name one protection an athlete can personally enforce against the school. CON never answered that cleanly; its A1 in Rebuttal 2 asserted "state-mandated" and "binding university regulations" almost interchangeably without naming a concrete, athlete-enforceable mechanism, which is the kind of unsupported specificity the debate rules flag as weak grounding. CON's arguments (union majority dominance, dues, management-rights clauses, budget rigidity) were reasonable but largely repeated across rounds rather than advancing, and its opening was flagged as clipped for exceeding the word limit—a real execution cost that also meant its strongest framing (dual-mission model, one-size-fits-all) was never fully developed. PRO's closing crystallized the clash memorably ("a courtesy with a budget line") while CON's closing mostly reasserted earlier claims without resolving the enforceability gap PRO kept exposing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 point that CBAs set floors, not ceilings, combined with the pressure question demanding a single athlete-enforceable protection—CON's Rebuttal 2 answer conflated "state-mandated" with "university regulations" without naming anything concrete, leaving the core clash unresolved in PRO's favor.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by cleanly winning the core clash on enforceability. Side B effectively used pressure questions to expose that Side A's "safeguards" (compliance liaisons, grievance channels) are all ultimately controlled by the university, successfully framing Side A's "flexibility" as unilateral management control.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing crystallization that "a promise enforced by the promisor is not a right; it is a courtesy with a budget line" decisively neutralized Side A's reliance on university grievance channels.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO won the enforceability and scope clash. A narrowed the unit to revenue-share athletes only, defused the gymnast/NIL and rower examples as floor-not-ceiling and separate classes, and pressed the unanswered question: name one athlete-enforceable protection not controlled by the school. CON's answer (binding university regulations, compliance liaisons, grievance channels) was still school-authored/staffed, and its dues-siphons-NIL claim was rebutted by A4.3. Opening clip and repetition further cost CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1 [A2.2-A2.3] turning the non-revenue and NIL attacks into floor-vs-ceiling and scope-widening, amplified by pressure Q A3.1 on enforceable injury protection which B never cleanly answered.
