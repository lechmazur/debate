### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's sovereignty/control argument proved more durable under pressure. B's core structural point—that any funding mechanism a treasury will actually approve inevitably regrows audits, milestones, and consultants, restoring former-power control—was never squarely answered by A beyond asserting "the recipient defines the endpoint," which dodges the practical accountability question B raised in Pressure Questions and reiterated in Rebuttal 2. B also exploited a real tension in A's case: if conservation money must keep flowing even when communities choose shrines or reburial over Western climate standards, then it isn't conservation funding tied to any outcome at all, undermining A's "bounded obligation" claim. A did land a genuine hit by forcing B to concede that provenance archives must be paid for by the former holder, but B's postage-vs-institution-building distinction was a plausible, if imperfect, parry that limited the damage. Execution mattered too: A's opening and closing were both clipped for exceeding word limits, costing A the chance to fully answer B's sharpest closing points (the "regrows conditions" and "general aid rebranded" arguments), while B stayed within format and delivered a clean, focused closing that crystallized the clash. That combination of unanswered structural pressure and clipped execution tips the debate to B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point that "no treasury writes a check its auditors cannot inspect," forcing control back through the finance ministry, combined with the shrine/reburial squeeze on A's "unconditional funding" claim, was the most damaging exchange, and A's closing (itself clipped) never fully neutralized it.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): B kept a cleaner route to victory: ownership ends the possession wrong, papers can go with the object as completed return, and post-return funding of labs/capacity is not a property duty. A argued compound causation and practical care well, and landed a sharp concession capture on archive costs, but repeatedly lost the live comparison once B forced the endpoint, audit, and shrine/reburial traps. Unconditional money either becomes supervised trusteeship or unbound general aid; that left the motion looking broader and less disciplined than clean return. A's clipped turns also cost some follow-through.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3–B4.5: A's "recipient-defined unconditional money" plus funds even for shrine/reburial turn conservation into blank-check aid that finance ministries will recondition.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by decisively trapping Side B on the issue of provenance research. While Side B argued that returning records is just "postage" and part of a clean break, Side A effectively demonstrated that locating, digitizing, and translating a century of colonial archives is inherently a funded provenance program. This forced Side B to concede the core mechanism of the proposition while trying to rename it. Side A also provided a robust conceptual defense of unconditional funding transfers (using a decommissioning analogy) against Side B's sovereignty concerns. Side B's practical point about treasury auditors imposing strings was strong, but could not overcome the conceptual collapse on the archives issue.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing exchange regarding the cost and complexity of digitizing colonial archives, which exposed Side B's "clean break" model as functionally identical to the proposition's demand for funded provenance research.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B identified and sustained a genuine structural dilemma that A never fully escaped: funding must either be conditioned by the former colonial power (reinstating control) or be fully recipient-directed (in which case it is indistinguishable from ordinary revocable aid, not a binding "obligation"). B pressed this in Rebuttal 1, sharpened it via pressure questions, and closed with the memorable framing that "an obligation Lagos cannot refuse and a gift London cannot direct" is incoherent. A's attempted resolution—an irrevocable duty to fund with recipient-controlled spending—was clever but left unanswered B's sharpest hit: under A's own scheme, the funder still implicitly certifies when "capacity" is adequate, reviving the old colonial paternalism A claims to reject. B also successfully disanalogized A's car-theft and polluter analogies (restitution-related decay is not ongoing harm caused by the former power, unlike pollution), which undercut A's causal story for perpetual funding. A had strong material (the "stolen more than objects" framing, the underfunded-not-incompetent line) and answered pressure competently, but did not neutralize B's core contradiction by the close. B's clipped opening is a real execution cost, but the substantive content survived largely intact and B's argument only grew sharper across rounds, giving it the cleaner, more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1/2 exchange exposing the "obligation vs. aid" dilemma—if London sets terms it's boardroom colonialism, if it sets none it's just aid Lagos could decline—forced A into a distinction (irrevocable duty, recipient-controlled spending) that B then showed still lets the funder define when "capacity" is adequate, reviving the very paternalism A sought to avoid.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B won the decisive clash: restitution as a closed justice act versus an open funding duty. B repeatedly forced A into an unstable dual claim—money that is both nonnegotiable obligation and fully recipient-directed with no London control—and argued there is no coherent third option, only aid or boardroom leverage. A’s shell-car, stripped-capacity, and endowment answers were serious and improved across rounds, but B better neutralized them by cabining true damages to the object, rejecting perpetual sector subsidy as object-debt, and showing A’s own finish line (“when the home can keep it”) still lets the former taker define adequacy. Clipped B opening is a real cost, yet B still made the choice clearer and more voteable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 on the polluter/damages expansion and donor-defined finish line (B4.1–B4.6), crystallizing the obligation/control/aid contradiction.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by exposing the mechanical and conceptual contradictions in Side A's proposed funding mechanism. B successfully forces a dilemma: the funding either comes with donor control (recreating boardroom colonialism) or it doesn't (making it standard, declinable foreign aid rather than a directed restitution obligation). B also lands a decisive rhetorical blow by framing A's "capacity" finish line as repackaged colonial paternalism, effectively neutralizing A's pragmatic arguments about conservation and leaving A unable to resolve the paradox of a "duty without control" in the final speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they systematically dismantle Side A's polluter analogy by distinguishing between ongoing harm and completed theft, and sharply crystallize the impossibility of an unconditional, irrevocable transfer of funds that the donor cannot direct but the recipient cannot refuse.
