### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins by a clear but competitive margin. Three arguments stayed live through the exchange in PRO's favor: (1) the information asymmetry argument—regulators lack proprietary data to challenge deals while platforms hold it, forcing burden-shifting onto the informed party—was introduced in the opening and never squarely answered by CON, whose rebuttals focused almost entirely on VC exit economics; (2) the speed-and-irreversibility point, that digital markets tip before ex-post litigation can intervene, was touched by CON only weakly ("use injunctive speed and resources"), which does not rebut the structural gap; and (3) the pivotal reframe in Rebuttal 2 that CON's own defense of outsized acquisition premiums inadvertently concedes they represent payment for neutralizing competitive threats—this damaged CON's most live route to victory without a fully adequate counter. CON mounted genuine pressure on two fronts: the VC funding math (pre-revenue teams cannot simply IPO or attract mid-tier buyers at comparable valuations) and the ex ante proof problem (acqui-hires and emergent integrations cannot demonstrate substantial consumer benefit before integration). These arguments have real force and PRO's answers—"valuations reset," "concrete integration roadmap"—were somewhat abstract and did not fully address the acqui-hire / experimental-backend category. However, CON's opening was clipped (execution cost), and CON never engaged the information asymmetry core or offered a convincing fix for enforcement speed, leaving PRO's structural case intact. PRO's closing crystallized the stakes ("who gets the benefit of the doubt") more cleanly than CON's closing, which was rhetorically sharper but narrower in scope. The debate is competitive; CON's practical VC argument and emergent-technology objection kept it honest, but PRO's surviving arguments are more fundamental to the proposition's justification.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 pivot [B4.3–B4.4]: turning CON's own defense of large acquisition premiums against it by arguing those outsized premiums are precisely the price dominant platforms pay to extinguish nascent competitive threats. This reframe converted CON's strongest empirical claim (VC math depends on strategic premiums) into evidence for PRO's core thesis, and CON's response—that premiums reflect synergy and scarcity, not just anti-competitive intent—acknowledged the ambiguity but did not resolve it, leaving PRO's reframe with lasting damage to CON's live route.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won by more convincingly framing the presumption as correcting irreversible info asymmetry and false negatives in tipping markets, directly answering pressure questions to reframe VC premiums as monopoly dependence rather than neutral synergy, and weighing that against CON's inability to separate killer acquisitions from integrations without chilling all deals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal 2 directly answered A's pressure questions (A1: resetting premiums redirects to genuine rivals; A2: integration roadmap is the threshold), exposing CON's over-reliance on dominant-buyer rents.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B wins by successfully pressuring the practical unworkability of PRO's mechanism. While PRO built a strong case on information asymmetry and the catastrophic cost of false negatives in tipping markets, CON consistently demonstrated that an ex-ante proof standard is inherently incompatible with early-stage tech integration, acqui-hires, and portfolio-risk VC math. PRO's rebuttals that capital would simply "redirect" to mid-tier buyers or that a "concrete roadmap" would suffice relied on optimistic assumptions that CON effectively dismantled by highlighting how early-stage valuations and emergent synergies actually function. CON's closing crystallized this decisive clash: the presumption is an indiscriminate sledgehammer that cannot separate defensive kills from productive innovation, chilling the startup pipeline it claims to protect. Both sides were rhetorically sharp and followed format, but CON's grounding in venture dynamics and mechanism feasibility provided the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 directly answered PRO's pressure questions by distinguishing acquisition premiums as scarcity/synergy value rather than "ransom," and explaining why shareholder portfolio logic cannot be translated into pre-integration regulatory proof. This exposed the fatal bluntness of PRO's standard and anchored CON's winning weighing on mechanism feasibility.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Its core arguments—information asymmetry, the "kill zone" framing, and the distinction between concrete verifiable evidence and guaranteed future outcomes—held up under pressure and were never squarely neutralized. CON's central claim (proving future consumer benefits is legally impossible) was consistently undermined by PRO's observation that dominant platforms already produce detailed board projections to justify these same deals internally; PRO correctly noted the standard it proposes is not a crystal ball but a translation of existing internal justifications into public evidence, and CON never answered that cleanly. PRO's rebuttal 2 responses were particularly sharp: on Q2, PRO turned CON's own "massive exit premiums" argument against it, reframing the VC-to-platform pipeline as monetizing suppression rather than funding innovation—the "kill zone breeds acquisition targets, not rivals" framing was original and not answered adequately. CON's strongest live argument—that eliminating dominant buyers collapses seed funding—was real and deserves credit, but PRO's counter (that those premiums reflect competition-killing value, and that redirected incentives can fund truly independent startups) was at least equally plausible and arguably stronger in context. CON repeated the VC collapse argument across nearly every round with minimal development, while PRO adapted each round to narrow the clash. PRO's closing crystallized the decisive question memorably: why should the public bear the burden when the dominant platform holds all relevant information? CON never produced a compelling answer beyond appealing to general principles about state burden of proof, which PRO had already shown is structurally inadequate in markets defined by information asymmetry and irreversible harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3) response to CON's A2 pressure question: PRO reframed CON's "massive exit premiums only dominant platforms can afford" as evidence that those premiums reflect the value of eliminating competition rather than creating it, then turned CON's own pipeline argument into a structural indictment—the current ecosystem breeds acquisition targets for gatekeepers, not independent rivals. CON never recovered from this inversion, continuing to argue in the closing that the funding desert would harm innovation without addressing why a pipeline that terminates at incumbent capture counts as healthy innovation in the first place.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): A wins because CON consistently showed the presumption creates an impossible evidentiary bar for speculative benefits, collapsing VC exits and startup formation, while PRO's asymmetry and kill-zone arguments were not decisive enough to neutralize the upstream funding effects.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4) most decisively answered B's pressure questions on projections vs. legal proof and exit alternatives, sharpening the funding desert comparison that remained live.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by successfully framing the debate around information asymmetry and the "kill zone," effectively turning Side B's venture capital collapse argument into an admission that the current ecosystem is captured by gatekeepers. Side A also provided a cleaner distinction between guaranteeing future outcomes and substantiating claims with internal projections, neutralizing Side B's core contention that the evidentiary standard is impossible. Side B argued competently on startup exit mechanics and regulatory risk-aversion, but could not overcome Side A's sharper comparative weighing and decisive closing crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the VC funding pressure, reframing massive exit premiums as the monetary value of killing competition and arguing that removing them redirects capital toward independent challengers rather than destroying innovation. This directly turned Side B's primary impact into a reason to vote PRO.
