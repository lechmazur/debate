### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides fought hard, but CON (A) ultimately built the cleaner, more decisive path: its closing sharpened a disconnect PRO never fully resolved—the motion funnels revenue to producers, not households, so "compensation" (affordability conditions, subsidized basics) is unguaranteed cash-flow relief for a parent facing today's checkout price. A also exposed a genuine internal tension in PRO's case: if the tax succeeds in shrinking fast fashion, its own funding base for the "replacement industry" erodes, and PRO's rebuttal ("that's the point, it's transitional") didn't actually neutralize the contradiction. PRO's strongest tool—turning CON's carbon/discharge-tax alternative into an equally regressive, equally traceability-plagued policy—was clever and did real damage, but CON's distinction (its alternative rebates cash directly to households, while the motion earmarks funds to manufacturers) survived that attack. PRO's repeated "poor pay either way" line elided the difference between diffuse externality costs and certain, immediate price increases, and CON pressed exactly that gap in both rebuttals and the closing. Execution-wise, both sides suffered a clipped speech (CON's Rebuttal 1, PRO's Closing), but PRO's clip landed right at its final weighing punch, costing it more in the round that mattered most for crystallization. Overall CON's case is slightly cleaner and easier to vote on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 tu quoque—turning CON's own carbon/discharge-tax alternative into an equally regressive, equally traceability-dependent policy—was the sharpest single move, but CON's follow-up distinguishing "revenue to households via rebate" from "revenue to producers via subsidy" (Rebuttal 2 and Closing) neutralized it and became the decisive exchange of the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by keeping the motion’s actual structure decisive: a heavy category tax whose proceeds go to manufacturers, not households, while conceding higher prices for lower-income buyers. PRO’s best moves—incidence equivalence, “build the replacement,” and cost-per-wear—were rhetorically sharp, but CON repeatedly broke the collapse by distinguishing what is taxed, how heavily, who is compensated, and when. Definition/classification of “fast fashion,” liquidity timing, the non-identity of affordability conditions with household rebates, and the shrinking-base paradox all stayed live and favored CON. PRO narrowed well to revenue use, but never fully answered the payer-beneficiary disconnect or the certain immediate burden versus speculative industrial chain. Clipped turns on both sides cost polish, yet CON left the cleaner vote path: reject an ill-matched, regressive industrial policy in favor of harm-targeted charges with real household relief.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 answer that rebates “do not fail in principle” under PRO’s tax but are simply not what the motion funds—exposing affordability conditions as producer subsidy, not cash relief.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing a persistent structural disconnect in PRO's model: the motion sends revenue to subsidize manufacturers rather than directly compensating low-income households hit by price rises. PRO's "affordability conditions" never overcame CON's argument that cash rebates are a more certain shield for the poor than subsidized industrial capacity that may not yield accessible goods when families need them. CON also landed the "shrinking tax base" objection unrebutted: if the tax succeeds in killing fast fashion, it starves the very revenue stream meant to scale the replacement industry. PRO's closing mischaracterized CON's position on rebates, and while PRO's "build the alternative" framing had force, CON showed the proposition guarantees immediate regressive burdens for a speculative payoff. The clash narrowed effectively to compensation and fiscal viability, and CON defended their ground more cleanly across the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question and subsequent rebuttal on the payer-beneficiary disconnect, showing that subsidizing manufacturers does not guarantee low-income buyers either cash or suitable clothing when needed, and that shrinking fast-fashion tax revenue would starve the replacement industry before it scales.

CON's closing crystallized the clash sharply and memorably, while PRO's closing largely restated earlier points without neutralizing the concession-driven damage.
