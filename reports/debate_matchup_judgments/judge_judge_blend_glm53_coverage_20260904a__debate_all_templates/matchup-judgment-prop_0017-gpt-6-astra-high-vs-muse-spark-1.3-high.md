### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B's practical objection landed harder and was never fully neutralized: the "genuinely available" test, once operationalized, invites endless case-by-case litigation over curfews, sobriety rules, dorm sleeping, and separation, effectively becoming a permanent veto that leaves neighbors, wheelchair users, and children waiting indefinitely. A's answer to the pressure question on this point (documented staff assessment with "prompt independent review") actually validated rather than resolved this concern, and B seized on that concession directly in Rebuttal 2 by quoting A's own language back at it — the single most decisive exchange in the debate. A's core philosophical claim (compliance is impossible without a real alternative) remained intact in the abstract but never fully answered B's parking/library analogies or the "sleep is necessary, this specific park is not" reframing, nor did A press B on whether a citywide ban leaves literally no legal place to sleep — a real weakness in B's position that went underexploited. B's closing crystallized the clash more memorably ("restoration theater," "fatal gap") while A's closing mostly reasserted its standard without closing the operational loop B had opened.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 bracket-quoting A's own concessions ([A4.1]-[A4.3]) to show that A's "documented assessment" process concedes exactly the litigation-gatekeeping problem B had warned about, turning A's attempted middle ground into evidence for B's slippery-slope critique.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's proposed "middle ground" of surgical hazard removal. Side A demonstrated that leaving the tents while only clearing immediate hazards leads to "restoration theater," and established that breaking up camp density provides an independent safety benefit that justifies enforcement even without available shelter.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal exposing PRO's "surgical enforcement" as unworkable "restoration theater" (clearing three feet today only for tarps to close back tomorrow) decisively won the pragmatic clash over maintaining public spaces.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A successfully defended the proposition by showing that without genuinely available alternatives, anti-encampment laws punish unavoidable biological necessity rather than chosen conduct. Side B’s public-trust and concentrated-hazard arguments justified targeted interventions that Side A already accepted, but never fully overcame the moral problem of penalizing sleep when no workable alternative exists. Side A gave the cleaner, more direct answers to pressure questions, establishing that an actual-ability standard is administrable and exposing Side B’s false choice between untouched encampments and unrestricted removal authority. Side B’s "restoration theater" critique was sharp but did not establish that dispersal without alternatives produced net safety gains or resolved the core compliance paradox.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal (A4), which directly answered Side B’s pressure questions by distinguishing actual barriers from mere preferences and showing that discrete hazards could be addressed immediately without broad criminalization, thereby neutralizing CON’s "permanent veto" objection and exposing the false dichotomy underlying CON’s case.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A opened powerfully with vivid, concrete framing of shelter inadequacy and the banishment logic, but its central rebuttal move—trying to exclude B's clinic-access counterexample from the category "anti-encampment law" by calling it a "safety order"—was cleanly exposed by B as definitional evasion ("simply defines away the question"). B's necessity/proportionality framework, anchored in the clinic-access and wheelchair-route examples, survived pressure by consistently distinguishing "somewhere lawful to remain" from the motion's specific triad of shelter, storage, and services. In the final rounds, A tried to collapse B's concessions into PRO's own standard by smuggling "storage and outreach" into a hypothetical that never specified them, and B's Rebuttal 2 directly and correctly called this out, showing the concession only required *some* lawful alternative, not the full package the motion demands. B's closing crystallized this decisive distinction ("Somewhere lawful to remain is not the same as city-provided shelter, storage, and services") while A's closing retreated to "the label does not matter," which does not answer the textual gap. B's tighter fidelity to the motion's actual wording and sharper logical pressure gave it the more convincing path to victory, despite A's stronger rhetorical color throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4-B4.5), where it demonstrates that A's "collapse into PRO's principle" argument illegitimately imports storage and outreach into a hypothetical that specified neither, directly undercutting A's central closing claim that any lawful alternative satisfies the motion's shelter+storage+services requirement.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won decisively by holding Side B to the exact text of the resolution. Side A used a narrow example (a wheelchair ramp) to prove an anti-encampment law could be justified without providing shelter, storage, and services, as long as a lawful outdoor alternative existed. Side B's attempt to redefine such a law as mere "traffic control" rather than an anti-encampment law was unconvincing, leading Side B to functionally concede Side A's premise.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 expertly capitalized on Side B's answers to the pressure questions, pointing out that Side B smuggled "storage and outreach" into a scenario that lacked them, and solidifying the concession that a standing prohibition on encampments can be justified without the full service package.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): CON successfully demonstrated that the proposition’s categorical requirement—anti-encampment laws justified ONLY when cities provide shelter, storage, and services—sets a universal prerequisite that fails for narrow, noncriminal, location-specific restrictions. By showing that a standing rule clearing a clinic access route (enforced through notice and relocation, not fines or jail) can be justified without a full service infrastructure, CON exposed PRO’s attempt to define such measures as “traffic control” rather than anti-encampment laws as a semantic dodge. PRO’s more emotive case about sweeps and banishment was powerful but targeted a broader criminalization regime than the motion’s text required; CON kept the debate focused on the overbreadth of “only when” and maintained a clean distinction between lawful outdoor alternatives and the specific shelter/storage/services package the proposition mandates.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing crystallized that PRO’s conceded “narrow standing rule” was, by definition, an anti-encampment law—undermining PRO’s categorical “only when” framework by showing a justified member of that category that required neither city-provided shelter, storage, nor services.
