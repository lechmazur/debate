### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A built a narrower, more defensible standard (a proven, contributing operator failure — e.g., knowingly re-renting after a required suspension trigger) and used it to expose a real tension in CON's case: CON accepted "clear triggers" for regulatory sanctions against operators but insisted the same triggers were too vague to ground compensation. A's pressure questions forced this contradiction into the open, and A's answers to CON's own questions (A1/A2) directly named a knowable-in-advance failure without requiring perfect GPS, which blunted CON's central "hindsight/vagueness" attack. CON's strongest material—floodgates concerns, rental-car analogy, cost-shifting to careful riders, deterrence dilution—remained forceful throughout, but its closing response to the compensation-gap problem (a city fine doesn't pay the pedestrian's bills) was mostly assertion ("fleet fine, not lifetime medical bills") rather than a resolving answer. That left A's narrowed liability trigger as the more precisely defended, harder-to-dislodge position by the end, giving it a clear but not overwhelming edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure questions (A3.1/A3.2) plus A4's direct A1/A2 answers, which exploited CON's own acceptance of "clear triggers" for account bans to show the same standard could ground tort liability — CON's B4 reply relabeled this a "retreat" but never explained why permit fines alone compensate the injured pedestrian when the rider cannot pay.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully leveraging Side A's own concessions regarding permit regulations to prove that companies have actionable control over safety. Side B's argument that the purpose of an account ban is precisely to prevent reckless riding effectively dismantled Side A's defense that the rider's choices were entirely independent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing point that "Calling the rider's later choice 'independent' cannot settle causation when preventing that choice was the safeguard's purpose" decisively neutralized Side A's "hands on the handlebars" framework.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B built a more convincing and nuanced case, successfully trapping Side A by using A's own proposed solutions (account bans) to establish corporate control and responsibility. Side B also decisively won the clash on regulatory fines versus tort liability by pointing out that city fines do not compensate injured pedestrians, which is the core issue of the proposition. Side A's economic arguments about higher fees were strong, but Side B's moral weighing regarding pedestrians subsidizing cheap rides with their physical safety was more persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they directly answered Side A's pressure questions, exposed Side A's inconsistency in accepting "clear triggers" for regulatory sanctions but calling them too vague for tort compensation, and effectively dismantled the "independent choice" argument by linking it back to the purpose of the safeguard.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): The decisive clash was whether "more legal responsibility" adds anything beyond ordinary negligence liability once reasonable safeguards are used. B pressed this consistently: if operators take reasonable precautions (geofencing, verification) they should not be liable when a rider deliberately violates rules; if they skip reasonable precautions, that's just ordinary negligence, not an expanded duty. A's rebuttals conceded exactly this line ("if the company genuinely used reasonable safeguards... liability should fade," and in closing "Use real safeguards and liability fades. Skip them and pay"), which B correctly identified as collapsing A's own case into B's fault-based framework rather than defending genuinely heightened/strict responsibility. A's strongest material (foreseeability, profit, control, cheapest-preventer) was answered by B's distinction between foreseeable misuse and company fault, and B's pressure question (Q1) forced A into an answer that essentially adopted B's negligence standard with different vocabulary. A never cleanly rebutted that the "higher duty" reduces to relabeling ordinary negligence with stricter enumerated checklist items, which B flagged directly in Rebuttal 2 and Closing. B's answers to A's pressure questions (A1/A2 equivalent, properly labeled) were direct and consistent with its throughout-debate position, while A's own Q2 about the vanished rider was answered by B in a way that exposed A's compensation-gap argument as not establishing a new duty, only a sympathetic bill needing a payer. Both sides argued competently and rhetoric was strong on both ends, but B controlled the central definitional battleground (what "more responsibility" adds) and closed the debate having narrowed the dispute to A's advantage-conceding line, making the choice easier to resolve in B's favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.3), showing that A's "liability should fade" concession collapses A's proposed heightened duty into ordinary fault-based negligence, directly following from B's Pressure Question 1 and exposing the central weakness in A's case.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a decisive strategic victory by successfully arguing that Side B's demands are either already covered by ordinary negligence or are unreasonable due to technological limitations. By forcing Side B to concede that 'liability should fade' when a company uses reasonable safeguards, Side A neutralized Side B's core moral appeal about uncompensated victims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal brilliantly capitalized on Side B's answer to PQ1, pointing out that under Side B's own rule, pedestrians hurt by vanished riders who bypassed reasonable safeguards are still left uncompensated.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by systematically dismantling Side B's definition of "more legal responsibility." Side A forces Side B to clarify whether the proposition demands strict liability or a specific articulation of ordinary negligence. When Side B concedes in Rebuttal 2 that liability should fade if a company uses reasonable safeguards, Side A effectively argues that Side B is merely advocating for the enforcement of existing fault-based duties, not a substantive expansion of liability. Side A's analytical precision exposes that Side B's proposed standard either unjustly punishes careful operators or simply restates current negligence principles. While Side B's rhetoric is highly engaging and its practical concerns about judgment-proof riders are compelling, Side A's logical discipline on the core legal mechanism secures the victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it uses Side B's own concession that "liability should fade" when reasonable safeguards are used to prove that Side B's "higher duty" collapses into the ordinary negligence standard Side A already defends, neutralizing the core distinction PRO needs to win the motion.
