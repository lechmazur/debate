### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): CON won on the strength of two arguments PRO never fully neutralized. First, the structural deterrence problem: PRO's "proportionality" limiting principle (long-term status earned through sustained contribution) does not distinguish the current cohort from any future wave making identical lived-reality claims. PRO conceded no criterion that would ever say no to the next wave, and CON exploited this in both rebuttal 2 and the closing. Second, CON's most effective maneuver was weaponizing PRO's own concession—PRO acknowledged regularization raises worker bargaining power and costs (A2.5, A4.1), and CON turned this directly into "if regularized workers cost what domestic labor costs, the employer 'desperate need' PRO invokes was need for cheap, captive labor, not labor per se." PRO's response (employers need workers for physically difficult roles that domestic workers refuse even at higher wages) was plausible but only partially answered the challenge and left tension in PRO's case. PRO's two most durable arguments—labor-market disruption from enforcement and regularization fixing wage suppression—remained competitive but never resolved the structural logic CON built. PRO also took two clip penalties (opening and first rebuttal exceeded word limits), creating execution costs: late-argument threads were cut and CON could not fully respond to material that was never shown. CON's pressure questions were sharper, directly exposing the employer-contradiction and the principled limits question, while PRO's questions, though pointed, were answered more convincingly by CON (temp visas + return enforcement < permanent regularization as pull factor; attrition + Japan/South Korea temp schemes as labor-market response). The closing exchange crystallized CON's lead: PRO leaned on "who fills the jobs" without resolving the deterrence loop; CON used "immigration systems survive on credible rules, not endless forgiveness" as a clean, earned summary of its structural case. The margin is real but competitive because PRO's economic disruption argument is genuine and CON's attrition-and-mechanization alternative is harder to deliver in practice than in theory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.4 response in Rebuttal 2, using PRO's own concession against it: PRO argued regularization gives workers bargaining power and makes them more expensive (A2.5, A4.1), so CON concluded that "desperate employer need" was need for cheap, captive labor rather than labor per se—and that once regularized, those workers cost what domestic labor costs, leaving positions unfilled anyway. PRO's counter (employers need workers for difficult roles domestic workers refuse regardless of wages) was adequate but left the internal tension visible, and CON built its closing around this crack. Combined with CON's Q2 squatter analogy exposing the absent limiting principle, this exchange did the most damage to PRO's live route to victory.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): A wins a clear but competitive debate. B made the stronger immediate humanitarian and labor-market case: integrated workers fill real roles, regularization can reduce exploitation, and enforcement risks disruption. But A more effectively controlled the decisive long-term clash: regularization creates repeat incentives, weakens legal channels, and B's limiting principle of contribution and ties does not explain why future arrivals would not make the same claim after waiting. A also used temporary legal migration plus enforcement as a plausible middle path against B's deportation-versus-status framing. B's clipped turns and incomplete answers on deterrence and fairness to legal migrants leave A's rule-of-law route live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal to B's pull-factor and job-gap questions was the key exchange: it conceded temporary visa risks but distinguished them from permanent regularization, then offered attrition, wage adjustment, mechanization, and regulated temporary schemes as alternatives to B's service-collapse framing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): PRO wins by keeping the debate focused on the concrete harms of removing already-integrated workers and the failure of enforcement to solve labor gaps or underground exploitation. CON's deterrence argument remained theoretically strong but could not overcome PRO's sector-specific evidence that domestic labor would not fill essential roles in care, agriculture, and food processing even at higher wages, and CON never squarely answered how attrition avoids driving workers into deeper precarity. PRO's limiting-principle answer to the squatter analogy also provided a more workable framework for regularization without blanket amnesty.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.3 turn highlighting the contradiction between PRO's claims about employer desperation and PRO's argument that regularization raises worker bargaining power, forcing a sharp clash over whether demand is for scarce labor or merely for exploitable cheap labor.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A maintained a cleaner, more unified through-line across all rounds. Its core claim—that integrated workers represent an economic and social reality that enforcement cannot simply undo—survived the full exchange better than CON's opposing arguments. On exploitation, A's "firewalls say live in fear, status says equal footing" distinction was sharper and more memorable than B's response that durable firewalls could be designed; that response was thin and arrived late. On the sequencing debate, A's reframe that "you can't repair the gate by tearing apart the household inside" was more concrete and harder to answer than B's abstract gate metaphor. On moral hazard, B had a genuinely strong argument—one-time regularizations historically repeat—but A partially neutralized it by showing how regularization plus employer sanctions breaks the dependency cycle. B never fully refuted the complementarity point. Critically, B suffered three clipped speeches, losing substantive content each time; this is a real execution cost that left key arguments underdeveloped, particularly the firewall alternative, which was introduced in Rebuttal 1 but never fully built into a compelling structural response. A's closing, while also slightly clipped, had already made its essential points. B's closing was rhetorically effective ("writes the script for the next crisis") but relied heavily on repetition of earlier points rather than resolving the key clashes. Overall, A answered the live pressure more directly, advanced on the exploitation and economic-reality arguments, and built the more coherent comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's firewall alternative (A2): "Firewalls say: You can report this violation, but you'll still live in fear. Status says: You have equal footing." This compressed the structural distinction clearly and put CON on the defensive for the rest of the debate. B's response—that firewalls can be made "durable"—was vague and never showed how a temporary status during investigation resolves the ongoing power asymmetry between undocumented workers and employers. A's framing of the exploitation clash carried into the closing and remained essentially unanswered at the level of structural inequality.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): B won a close debate by keeping the focus on already-integrated workers and showing why status better addresses current economic disruption and exploitation. A’s moral hazard and “hostage situation” framing was strong, but B gave a plausible answer that regularization can shrink the shadow economy, empower workers to report abuses, and make future employer enforcement more credible. A’s firewall alternative blunted the exploitation claim but did not fully solve the ongoing vulnerability of workers without long-term status.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to the firewall alternative was the key exchange: it showed that temporary or case-bound protections may permit reporting but do not give workers equal footing to refuse abuse, change jobs, or live without deportation pressure.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by building a cleaner path from the actual proposition to the ballot. A grounded the debate in the concrete reality of already-integrated workers—demonstrating that stricter return enforcement tears holes in essential labor markets and that temporary firewall protections cannot fix the structural power imbalance that enables exploitation. B’s moral-hazard and sequencing arguments were forceful, but they leaned heavily on theoretical future enforcement and a hostage metaphor that never overcame the immediate economic and humanitarian costs A identified. B also incurred real execution costs from three clipped speeches, while A more directly answered pressure questions and crystallized the comparative choice in the closing. After the full exchange, A’s live route—formalize proven contribution to enable credible enforcement and equal labor standing—remained more intact than B’s promise that enforcement-first would somehow avoid economic disruption.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was over firewalls versus long-term status. A showed that temporary investigative protections leave workers vulnerable once an inquiry closes, preserving the very fear that prevents workers from refusing abuse or leaving bad employers. B responded that firewalls could be durable shields, but never explained how temporary protections equalize bargaining power or solve structural inequality, leaving A’s exploitation argument largely intact while B’s alternative appeared to treat symptoms rather than the disease.
