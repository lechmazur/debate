### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by separating genuine reuse from waste and showing that testing identifies the material while the ban supplies a clear destination rule and legal consequence. Side A raised a serious enforcement challenge, but its claim that prohibition erases manifests was unconvincing: smugglers can still falsify ordinary shipping documents, while licensed importers may profit from accepting bad loads. Side A also repeatedly alleged harm to working-device repair economies despite Side B explicitly exempting functional reuse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest answer was that functional testing and a ban are complementary, not competing: testing sorts reusable devices from residue, while the ban prevents failed material from being exported under a nominally certified channel. This turned Side A’s preferred testing regime into substantial agreement with the motion’s destination rule.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): A built the cleaner decisive path: toxic-subsidy/accountability frame plus a workable waste-vs-reuse split that survives pressure. B’s strongest attack (ban deletes paper trail and licensed buyer incentive; testing already works inside certified trade) was sharp and sustained, but never showed how certification alone blocks mislabeled residue without effectively installing the same destination rule the motion requires. A’s replies made that fork explicit—paperwork is forgeable; testing only has teeth if failed waste cannot lawfully go to lower-income destinations—and B’s retreat to “own regional waste” left the Ghana/India import-repair defense under-defended. Narrow but clear A win on comparative enforceability of the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 fork (A4.1–A4.5): certified trade either trusts paperwork (smugglers win) or testing (then untested residue must stay out of lower-income destinations)—so the export loophole closes either way and the ban supplies the needed rule, not a demotion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): PRO defended the core environmental justice claim that rich countries should not externalize toxic e-waste to lower-income nations, and showed that pre-export functional testing preserves legitimate reuse while isolating hazardous residue for domestic or certified regional processing. CON landed sharp attacks on enforcement and paper trails, but never fully overcame PRO's accountability argument: certified trade to weaker regulatory jurisdictions remains vulnerable to mislabeling, whereas keeping waste inside jurisdictions with stronger courts, inspectors, and voters offers a more credible enforcement path. The edge is razor-thin because CON's alternative is well-framed, but PRO's bright-line ban plus testing created the cleaner comparative advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal exposing that PRO's admission about under-resourced customs undermines the feasibility of drawing a bright line between waste and working goods, and that a ban eliminates the very paper trails needed for oversight rather than sharpening enforcement.
