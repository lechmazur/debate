### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but PRO (B) constructed a more resilient path to victory. B grounded its case in concrete, plausible examples (Falepili Union, Argentina's humanitarian visas) and a clear causal mechanism (practice settles law), then used the Pressure Questions round to expose CON's central weakness: CON never offered a credible mechanism or timeline for "settling" climate-displacement law, only a repeat of the same negotiating process that has stalled for decades. When CON answered by proposing "a mandated negotiating sequence," B correctly identified this as circular—describing the very process that has already failed. Most decisively, CON's own Rebuttal 2 conceded that "emergency admission, temporary protection, disaster visas" should apply now, which B seized on in closing as an admission that pre-settlement humanitarian pathways are already necessary and operative—undercutting CON's "wait for settlement" framing. CON's attempt in closing to distinguish "rescue" from "expansion" came late and was not fully developed, leaving the concession live and damaging. CON's strongest points—discretion risks, moral-alibi concerns, backlash dynamics—were real but B directly answered them (ballot-based allocation as a design fix, backlash driven by chaotic border arrivals rather than quiet visas, no mechanism shown by which visas siphon adaptation funding) without CON landing a comparably clean counter. B's closing crystallized the clash more sharply and exploited CON's own concessions, giving it the cleaner path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) and Closing, which turned CON's own admission that "emergency admission, temporary protection, disaster visas" apply now into proof that CON's plan already concedes the core of the motion—shifting the debate from "whether to act" to "what pre-settlement action should look like," a frame CON never fully recovered from.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won through the stronger comparative: imperfect pathways can protect people now and later be corrected, while waiting leaves immediate displacement unanswered without any credible route to faster legal settlement. Side A identified serious risks of discretion, selectivity, and temporary status, but never established that delaying expansion would actually produce its preferred binding regime or that pathways would obstruct one. Its proposed negotiating sequence was aspirational, and its reliance on emergency admission weakened its categorical sequencing argument. Side B also answered the pressure questions directly and honestly, although its mistaken C-reference and some overconfidence about temporary status becoming permanent were minor costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its response that no binding guarantee exists in either world, but Side A adds closed pathways to that uncertainty. The contrast between correctable temporary protection and irreversible exposure sharply undercut Side A’s demand for settlement-first guarantees.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A won by making the live comparison clearer and easier to decide: delay already produces a refusal default, settlement has no credible termination date, and imperfect expandable pathways beat zero pathways plus endless process. A's honest concessions (no binding upgrade duty; no binding anti-selection rule yet) were then weighed cleanly against CON's worse status quo, while the closing turn that B's own emergency/disaster tools already enact pre-settlement climate-linked protection forced B onto a thinner distinction. B had real force on discretion crystallizing and moral-alibi risk, but its "mandated negotiating sequence" never became a concrete alternative to decades of failure, so its route stayed more abstract and less decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing conversion of B4.2's emergency admission/temporary protection/disaster visas into de facto acceptance of the motion, reducing the clash to trajectory of pre-settlement protection rather than whether to act at all.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON (Side A) built a durable strategic fork early—expansion either stays small/capped (a pilot in disguise) or scales, in which case it inherits every unresolved eligibility problem—and pressed it with increasing precision across every round. PRO (Side B) never escaped this trap: under pressure, PRO's own language repeatedly slid toward "capped," "modest," "time-bound," and "enough structure to learn," which CON weaponized directly, including in a devastating closing that quoted PRO's own hedges back at them. CON's pressure questions also exposed a real weakness—PRO's eligibility criteria (imminence, severity, vulnerability) function as ranking factors, not exclusion rules, and CON correctly identified this as "discretion with a queue" rather than genuine triage, a point PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer did not adequately resolve. PRO's strongest material—irregular migration becoming more dangerous without lawful routes, and burden-sharing benefits to nearby host states—remained live and forceful throughout, but CON's answer (existing channels can deliver these benefits without new undefined admissions tracks) blunted its impact enough, combined with CON's sharper internal consistency, to give CON the more convincing overall path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 line "That is my pilot with better marketing" [A2.1], responding to PRO's reframe of expansion as "capped, time-bound, regionally shared, and evaluated" [B2.3], set up a fork PRO never escaped, and CON's subsequent rebuttal showing PRO's ranking criteria don't actually exclude anyone sealed the point.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): B won a clear but competitive debate. A powerfully highlighted eligibility, durability, and backlash risks, but its case depended too heavily on defining every capped, time-limited expansion as merely a pilot. B offered the more natural reading of the motion: pathways can meaningfully expand while remaining bounded and reviewable. Its account of transparent thresholds, priority ranking, safer planned movement, and practice generating future law answered the central uncertainty objection while preserving the urgent humanitarian benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.4 most effectively answered A's claim that climate eligibility could not exclude or prioritize applicants. B supplied sequential gates and transparent humanitarian ranking, then explained that expansion need not create an absolute right to entry. A's response exposed residual discretion but did not prove that such discretion makes bounded expansion impossible.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B won by locking the motion to its verb and timing and forcing A into a live fork: either defend real expansion and inherit unsolved eligibility/fragility costs, or defend capped time-bound learning programs and concede B’s sequencing frame. A’s urgency and practice-builds-law story stayed coherent but kept shrinking under pressure into pilot-like programs, weaker rationing criteria, and benefits (remittances, host support, ordinary labor channels) that did not uniquely require expanding an unsettled climate track first. B answered pressure directly, kept the comparison clean across rounds, and made the decisive choice easier: settle via narrow practice, then scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s expand-vs-pilot fork (esp. B2.1/B4.1/B5.1): showing A’s “capped, modest, correctable” defense answered pressure only by abandoning standing expansion.
