- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, competing frameworks—A on sovereignty, speed, and unenforceability; B on reciprocal security and disciplined, reviewable restraint—but A's rebuttals did more damage to B's live mechanism. A's sharpest hit was that "act now, justify later" is a legal trap: retrospective necessity review, decided by distant adjudicators, will systematically favor resourced states with data and lawyers, chilling exactly the fast action crises require. B never squarely answered this power-asymmetry point, instead retreating to abstractions like "interim stays" and "genuine need falls within the exception." A's pressure question about a state defying a narrowing order got an evasive answer (B4.3 essentially assumes the conflict away rather than confronting it), a real execution cost under the judging criteria. B did force a meaningful concession that bans don't guarantee the poor are fed, and its contagion/reciprocity framing remained live, but it never neutralized the enforcement-feasibility attack, which was the debate's central fault line.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point that retrospective necessity review favors better-resourced states ("Discretion does not disappear; it migrates to adjudicators far from the hunger") combined with B's evasive A2 answer to the defiance pressure question in Rebuttal 2 was the decisive exchange—B never squarely closed that gap.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) identified the decisive structural dilemma early in Rebuttal 1: a rule loose enough to respect emergency uncertainty (notify, justify, time-limit) cannot stop multiple countries from simultaneously imposing "compliant" bans, so the contagion problem PRO promises to solve persists; but a rule strong enough to overrule a "documented shortfall" becomes outsiders compelling exports from a state's own hungry population without compensation. PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to the pressure question actually confirmed this dilemma rather than resolving it—defining compliance as "documented shortfall + least trade-restrictive means," which CON immediately exploited in Rebuttal 2 by pointing out three neighbors could all meet that test simultaneously, leaving the landlocked importer exactly where PRO said the rule would prevent. PRO's closing reasserted that the rule stops "the most destructive form" (open-ended blanket bans) but never explained why simultaneous justified, time-limited bans aren't equally destructive to the importer, leaving the central clash unresolved in CON's favor. CON's closing crystallized this cleanly ("relabeled it compliant, not stopped"), while also maintaining pressure on PRO's unfunded compensation mechanism (export levy requires continued exports and targeting capacity PRO concedes may not exist). PRO's opening and early framing (contagion spiral, farmer disincentives) were strong and original, but the debate turned on the toothless-vs-binding dilemma, which CON pressed harder, exploited more precisely via the pressure-question answers, and never let go of through to the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 dilemma ("weak enough to fail importers or strong enough to endanger exporters") combined with Rebuttal 2's direct use of PRO's own stated threshold to show three neighbors could simultaneously satisfy it, defeating PRO's central contagion-prevention claim on PRO's own terms.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by cleanly establishing and executing a fatal dilemma regarding enforceability. A proved that PRO's "bounded flexibility" either permits justified short-term bans (failing to solve the importer's shortage) or forces a state to export despite a documented shortfall (endangering the exporter's poorest citizens without compensation).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 8 (Side A's Rebuttal 2) decisively won the debate by showing that B's standard of "documented shortfall" and "least trade-restrictive means" allows three neighbors to legally ban exports simultaneously, meaning B's rule fails to prevent the very queue it claims to solve.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON built a decisive analytical dilemma: PRO’s “bounded flexibility” either permits simultaneous, documented, time-limited bans by multiple neighbors (failing importers) or compels exports against genuine shortages (harming exporting-state poor without guaranteed compensation). PRO never resolved this trap across rebuttals and closing, repeating the middle-ground mantra while CON sharpened the weighing and crystallized the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s exposure of the unstable middle ground in PRO’s “bounded flexibility” — showing that simultaneous, justified, time-limited bans remain permissible under PRO’s own threshold, leaving importers unprotected, while stricter enforcement would coerce exporters without compensation.
