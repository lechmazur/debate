### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently with plausible mechanisms and no fake citations, but A built a more decisive strategic through-line: by pointing out that B's own proposed remedy (subsidized 24/7 stabilization clinics, helicopters, telehealth in unprofitable rural areas) is itself government intervention against pure market logic, A forced B into defending a distinction between "local access" and "local hospitals" that never fully escaped the charge of conceding the resolution's core normative claim. B's clinical volume-quality argument (low-volume maternity/surgical units are unsafe) was strong and consistently pressed, and B's "filter" rebuttal to A's regional-congestion point was somewhat unaddressed by A, but B's closing still leaned on the same semantic distinction without fully dissolving the concession framing. A's pressure questions targeted this tension directly and A's answers (A1/A2) engaged them head-on, while B's answers, though properly formatted, relied on reasserting the same distinction rather than closing the gap. The debate was close and well-executed on both sides, but A controlled the framing of what counted as "the concession" more effectively across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's repeated "concession" move (first in Rebuttal 1 [A2.3], sharpened in Pressure Q1, and driven home in Rebuttal 2/Closing) forced B to spend real capital distinguishing "bridge to care" from "duplicative hospital," a distinction B never fully cashed out against the literal wording of the motion ("subsidize local medical access").
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by maintaining a clear and consistent distinction between subsidizing local access (stabilization clinics, transport, telehealth) and subsidizing duplicative inpatient hospitals, successfully resisting Side A's "concession" frame. B sharply rebutted A's golden-hour and maternal-hemorrhage scenarios by showing that low-volume local hospitals lack the volume to keep specialists and surgical teams ready around the clock, rendering them dangerous illusions rather than lifelines. B's "bridge vs. tower" framing and focus on scarce clinician allocation provided a cleaner comparative case for why consolidation plus funded access beats propping up underused full-service hospitals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal of the maternal hemorrhage scenario, demonstrating that a low-volume rural maternity ward cannot maintain 24/7 obstetric surgery, anesthesia, and blood-bank readiness, thereby turning PRO's own example into evidence for CON's "illusion of care" thesis.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by drawing a sharp, highly persuasive distinction between subsidizing full-service rural hospitals (which suffer from dangerous low-volume skill decay) and subsidizing lean stabilization clinics and transport (which safely bridge the gap to regional hubs). Side A effectively dismantled Side B's "concession" trap by clarifying that funding basic access embraces consolidation, while funding duplicative inpatient towers defies it. Side A's medical reasoning regarding volume, specialization, and the illusion of readiness was grounded, logically consistent, and decisively answered Side B's most aggressive pressure points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the maternal hemorrhage pressure question, pointing out that a low-volume rural maternity ward cannot keep an OB surgeon, anesthesia, and a blood bank ready at 3 AM anyway. This made stabilize-and-transfer the unavoidable reality under either system, effectively destroying Side B's romanticized view of local surgical readiness.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases without fabricated evidence, but A won the decisive structural exchange. A's key move—forcing B to admit that its "market" alternative (mobile ALS, freestanding EDs, regional hubs) still requires the same government subsidy and scarce rural staff—collapsed B's central framing that consolidation reflects "market logic" rather than a different subsidy allocation. B never adequately answered this reframe in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, instead retreating to repeating the volume-outcome/golden-hour argument. A also successfully distinguished stabilization from definitive specialized care, blunting B's strongest medical argument, and used the "someone public must pay either way" point to reduce the debate to a question of what subsidy buys rather than whether to subsidize—a framing more favorable to the PRO burden. B's case remained clinically plausible and rhetorically sharp (golden hour, definitive care) but left A's funding-equivalence pressure and the systemic ambulance-coverage-gap point largely unrebutted, costing it the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's [A4.3]-[A4.4] point that B's alternative infrastructure also needs public funding and scarce staff ("Someone public must pay either way... the clash is no longer subsidy versus market") was the most damaging exchange, since it undercut B's core "market logic" framing and was never squarely answered in B's subsequent speeches.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by demonstrating that local stabilization is often the only bridge between a rural patient and distant regional excellence, and by exposing that Side B's alternative model still requires government funding and scarce rural staff—shifting the clash from market versus subsidy to which subsidized system actually keeps people alive. Side B never adequately answers the obstetric and bad-weather transport failures, mischaracterizes A's empty-wards concession as a wholesale surrender, and fails to explain why recruitment becomes easier under a regionalized model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4.1–A4.5), where they directly answered pressure questions, narrowed the proposition to right-sized local access rather than obsolete inpatient wards, and turned the funding clash by showing Side B's mobile EMS and regional hubs also depend on public subsidy—collapsing the market-logic frame.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by decisively neutralizing Side A's main alternative. Side A argued that market logic should replace traditional rural hospitals with freestanding ERs and robust mobile EMS. Side B brilliantly pointed out that the exact same market failures (low volume, poor payer mix) that cause rural hospital closures will also cause rural freestanding ERs to fail, meaning they too inherently require government subsidies. This collapsed Side A's "market logic" framing, shifting the debate to *what* to subsidize, where Side B's case for a right-sized local stabilization hub proved far more practical. Side B also effectively defended the clinical value of basic stabilization and highlighted the "uncovered county" problem when rural ambulances are tied up on 90-minute transports, leaving Side A's reliance on mobile EMS clinically and logistically vulnerable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of the "freestanding ER" alternative in Rebuttal 2, pointing out that the same thin volume and poor payer mix that sinks a rural hospital will also sink a private freestanding ER, thus proving that government subsidy is unavoidable and exposing a fatal flaw in Side A's market-based alternative.
