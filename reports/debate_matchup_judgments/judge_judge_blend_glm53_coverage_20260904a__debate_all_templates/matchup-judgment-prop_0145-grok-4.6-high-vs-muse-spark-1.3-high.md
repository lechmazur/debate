- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases with sharp pressure questions and direct A1/A2 compliance. The pivotal exchange came when B's pressure question forced A to justify reading all private messages by default despite A's own earlier claim that parents should supervise "where lethal third parties enter, not where intimacy lives." A's answer—that friend threads are where danger enters, so you "cannot sort intimacy from infiltration without looking"—effectively collapsed A's own distinction and handed B the closing line that ordinary teenage speech becomes "the hazard by definition." B also scored with the internal contradiction that if grooming "looks normal" (A's own justification for anticipatory monitoring), then routine scrolling of ordinary chats would fail to catch it too, undercutting the tool's claimed efficacy; A's rejoinder (parent baseline vs. stranger view) was plausible but not fully dispositive. A retained a live point about gradual buildup indicators (a moved address, a new midnight contact) not tripping curfew-style triggers, which B's closing addressed only partially. On balance, B did more damage to A's core framework and finished with the cleaner, more memorable reason to prefer its side, giving it a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2/Closing sequence (B4.4, B5.1) showing that A's own defense of default message-reading—"friend threads are where third parties enter, you cannot sort intimacy from infiltration without looking"—directly collapses A's earlier claimed distinction and effectively concedes that the default treats all ordinary teen communication as presumptively dangerous.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): PRO showed that CON's safety case either duplicated the 'specific concern' standard or failed to justify default surveillance, while exposing the collapsing intimacy/infiltration distinction and the standing cost to trust and development. CON's seatbelt analogy and deterrence claims were left materially damaged after the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal and closing, which turned CON's 'grooming is invisible' claim against the utility of default monitoring and collapsed CON's distinction between supervising lethal entry points and reading ordinary friend messages.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully trapping Side B in a logical contradiction regarding the monitoring of intimacy versus third-party threats, and by establishing a strong epistemic bind: if dangerous messages are subtle, default scrolling misses them; if they are obvious, they already constitute a specific safety concern. Side B made strong emotional appeals regarding irreversible tragedy, but Side A dismantled the practical mechanism of default monitoring, proving that the proposition allows for targeted intervention without the collateral damage of constant surveillance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, where they capitalized on Side B's admission that parents cannot sort intimacy from infiltration without looking. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides fought a tight, well-structured debate, but B established the stronger throughline: default surveillance corrodes the exact trust that makes a frightened or pressured teen call a parent for help, and B repeatedly turned A's own concessions (evasion via paused locations, second accounts) into proof that tracking buys little real safety while paying a real trust cost. A's best weapon—that B's "specific concern" triggers (smell of alcohol, missed curfew) are lagging indicators—was sharp and forced B onto the back foot, but A never squarely answered B's core mechanism that surveillance itself converts would-be-confiding teens into hidden ones; A's rebuttal ("evasion already happens anyway") answers a different claim than the one B was making. B's opening frame ("safe when nobody is watching") anchored the debate and was echoed even in A's own language, and B's closing image of the 16-year-old who needs a parent she'll "text now" rather than a dot she can "replay later" landed as the more resonant final comparison, even though A got the last speech. The debate is close, but B's case survives the exchange slightly more intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's [B2.1]-[B2.4] turn, which converted A's "I'm at Sarah's is a claim" line and A's concession that teens "already use coded language and second phones" into a demonstration that always-on tracking pays full trust costs for an easily-evaded, largely illusory benefit, was the most damaging exchange; A's counter-pressure on lagging indicators ([A4.3]-[A4.4]) was the strongest response but never fully neutralized the trust-erosion mechanism.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won because it demonstrated that default surveillance destroys the trust necessary for a teenager to seek help in moments of risk, creates counterproductive secrecy, and is undermined by trivial workarounds that produce noise rather than safety. While Side B effectively argued that waiting for a specific concern can be too late, Side A blunted this by offering concrete pre-harm warning signs and targeted preventive protocols (e.g., sober-driver conditions, open check-ins) that protect without permanent monitoring. Side B never fully rebutted the central developmental and relational costs of treating every teen as a default suspect.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 answer to Side B’s pressure questions, where A listed concrete pre-harm indicators (missed curfews, slipping grades, teacher flags) and paired them with targeted high-risk protocols (sober-driver plans, no-questions-asked pickup) that directly showed how parents can prevent first harm without always-on tracking, defusing B’s core “too late” attack.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A built a much stronger, psychologically realistic case about how default surveillance destroys the trust necessary for teenagers to actually seek help in a crisis. Side A also successfully defended the "specific safety concern" threshold by providing concrete, pre-catastrophe examples of what triggers it, neutralizing Side B's main argument that the proposition requires waiting for a disaster. Side B relied heavily on repetitive talking points and failed to adequately answer how default message monitoring avoids invading intimate privacy. Side A's "lifeline" framing was the decisive impact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's "too late" argument by listing ordinary early warning signs (slipping grades, new older circle, sudden secrecy) that trigger targeted monitoring, and pointed out that default tracking merely records the first mistake rather than preventing it.
