### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B consistently exploited the motion's precise wording—"even if it increases protectionist measures and fiscal burdens"—forcing A to defend protectionism specifically rather than industrial policy broadly. B's sharpest move was catching A's rebrand of free zones, ports, and universities as "industrial policy" [B2.1], noting that if everything counts, the motion's actual burden goes undefended. B also exposed a genuine internal contradiction: A argued nearshoring capital is sticky (geopolitical de-risking, not cost arbitrage) to counter the "capital will flee protectionism" worry, yet separately warned that without protectionism capital would "exit whenever cheaper labor emerges" [A4.4]—B named this directly ("They cannot have both" [B4.2]) and A never resolved it. On the decisive pressure question of who administers subsidy withdrawal, A's answer ("global value chain polices the inefficiency" [A4.3]) was aspirational rather than mechanistic, and B correctly labeled it "an outcome, not a mechanism" while pointing to Tierra del Fuego as the empirical counter-example of patronage capture. A's case had reasonable structure (East Asian analogy, CHIPS/IRA framing) but its closing simply reasserted earlier claims without engaging B's sharpest attacks, while B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly around the unresolved contradiction and the "vitamins vs. poison" framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 [B4.1-B4.2], catching A in the contradiction between "capital is sticky due to de-risking" and "capital will flee to cheap labor without protectionism"—a clean, unresolved logical trap that A's closing never addressed.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B won by enforcing the motion's actual burden—the "even if" concession on protectionism and heavier fiscal burdens—rather than letting industrial policy dissolve into uncontroversial ports, schools, and promotion. A’s export-oriented/GVC-discipline distinction from ISI was plausible but never supplied a credible sunset or dispute-proof instrument once B pressed state capacity and USMCA risk. B’s vitamins-vs-poison frame, Costa Rica separation, and demolition of A’s A1/A2 answers (content mandates redundant or reckless; “export discipline” does not administer patronage subsidies) made the decisive comparison cleaner and left A defending mechanisms that already operate under openness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 on A’s content mandates and sunset mechanism: either redundant with negotiated USMCA rules or dispute-provoking, and “export discipline” is aspiration because the patronage state still administers the subsidy.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON controls the "even if" burden. PRO rebrands ports, universities, and free zones as industrial policy but never justifies added protectionism and fiscal cost. A exposes this as vitamins vs poison, uses ISI history and Tierra del Fuego to show capture, and turns PRO's own export-discipline/de-risking logic into an openness mechanism. B's USMCA and sunset answers rely on aspiration and invalid IDs [C1.2, P1.1], never solving patronage cut-off.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2/A4 — vitamins-vs-poison distinction plus turning global value-chain discipline against PRO, showing content mandates are redundant/reckless and subsidy sunset lacks a mechanism, leaving PRO's shallow-assembly fear without a protectionist necessity.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): A built a more compelling and original core narrative (a closing geopolitical window, comparative advantage as constructed not inherited, geography as a "moat" for Mexico/Central America, and the export-test distinction from ISI) and sustained it through rebuttal, notably landing a genuinely sharp pressure question about USMCA's own content mandates. B's rebuttals were disciplined and logically clean — especially the reciprocal/unilateral and horizontal/selective distinctions used to defuse A's pressure questions — but B's own alternative (state-funded ports, grids, and macro stability) quietly imports similar fiscal exposure without fully reconciling that tension, and A's "who funds it" pressure point was never squarely resolved, only reframed. A's case also had stronger forward momentum and rhetorical memorability across rounds, though this is offset by real execution costs: both of A's rebuttals were clipped for exceeding word limits, costing some completeness and closing punch. On balance, A's superior opening construction and unanswered urgency/geography framing edge out B's tighter but more repetitive defensive case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question tying USMCA's regional-content rules to the "protectionism kills value chains" claim was the sharpest moment; B's answer (reciprocal multilateral rules vs. unilateral mandates) was a reasonable save, but A's closing dismissal of it as "bookkeeping" left the tension only partially resolved, making this the debate's most consequential unfinished exchange.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by holding the decisive mechanism clash: unilateral protection and selective fiscal bets raise input costs and sovereign risk in debt-constrained Latin America, undermining the export competitiveness and stability nearshoring requires. A had a strong opening frame (export-tested IP vs old ISI; closing window; status-quo stagnation) and landed real pressure on USMCA content rules and the fiscal character of ports/grids, but never fully neutralized B’s reciprocal-vs-unilateral and horizontal-vs-selective distinctions. Two clipped A rebuttals and a partly unanswered geography/scope problem left B with the cleaner residual path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2: USMCA thrives as reciprocal continental free-trade integration, not unilateral local-content protection; baseline grids/ports are horizontal public goods, not selective corporate industrial policy.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B PRO won the decisive weighing. It turned A's strongest claims into a symmetry problem: USMCA itself imposes regional-content rules yet hosts booming nearshoring, undercutting the claim that content inherently kills value chains, and A's own alternative of ports/grids is also state spending requiring sequencing. B's answer — proximity/time-zone/tariff-free access swamps marginal input cost, content ratchets as clusters form, export market disciplines firms, and performance-linked incentives widen the formal tax base — directly met A's higher-cost and rent-seeking pressures. B crystallized reversible subsidies vs irreversible supply-chain hardening as the ballot, while A left the fiscal-equivalence question and Washington-created wave origin unanswered, despite B's clipping penalty.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 plus Pressure Q1/Q2: showing USMCA rules of origin are content mandates underpinning Mexican boom, and that proximity advantages and ratcheted, export-tested incentives outweigh marginal cost and make fiscal costs self-liquidating vs status quo narrow-base fragility.
