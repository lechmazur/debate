### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent frameworks, but B maintained a cleaner, more consistent operational test throughout: autonomy names the decider who inhabits the consequences, and dignity cannot override a competent, informed choice once coercion is removed. B's key move—distinguishing origin (why we protect bodies) from rank (who decides once inside the gate)—went largely unanswered on its own terms. Under pressure, B gave a clean, direct answer (A1/A2) that a supported, competent refusal must be honored, while A's answer to the parallel question ("autonomy decides only when dignity can see the refusal as an assertion of the patient's standing") reintroduced exactly the unnamed second interpreter B had been hunting for. A never resolved this: A4.1 claims dignity can "require continued care" after a competent refusal, which functionally overrides the patient's veto, while A4.3 insists dignity gives no one license to seize the body—an unreconciled tension that B's closing exploited effectively ("Requiring treatment after a competent refusal is not commentary"). A's gate/house metaphor and examples (stigma-shaped refusals, exhausted elders) were philosophically serious, but the debate's decisive procedural question—who finishes the decision about the body—was answered more directly and consistently by B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2), which directly answered A's pressure question with a bright-line rule and then turned A's own dignity-as-endorsement move against it by noting A still needed to name who ranks a competent refusal as "authentic," a demand A's closing never cleanly met.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully exposing the structural tension in Side A's case. B forced A to clarify whether dignity actually overrides a competent refusal; when A conceded that the patient retains the veto against unwanted invasion but that dignity "grades" the choice or refuses endorsement, B decisively argued that a value functioning only as a spectator's grade cannot outweigh the authority that actually finalizes the decision over the body.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal dismantling the 'origin equals rank' argument with the locksmith analogy, followed by B's sharp Rebuttal 2 pointing out that grading a competent choice is merely content control.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO made the decisively clearer comparative claim: moral weight = who finishes the decision for the body that bears consequences. CON's admission in R2 that dignity can deny moral finality to a competent, supported refusal because motive sounds like burden exposed dignity as content-based veto, exactly the paternalism PRO warned about. PRO's reframe that restoring options is autonomy's coercion check, not dignity override, undercut CON's strongest social-failure argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1/R2 line that CON collapses "whether someone is speaking" into "whether speech is worthy" and that declining a competent no is a veto, culminating in Q1 forcing CON to concede continued blocking.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, closely matched cases, but PRO (B) executed the exchange more cleanly. PRO's core move—distinguishing autonomy's "competence/voluntariness" filters (testing whether a choice is authentically the patient's) from dignity's filter (an outsider's moral approval of the choice's meaning)—directly answered CON's parity claim that both principles rely on interpreters, and it was delivered with proper A1/A2 structure after the pressure round. PRO also kept the debate anchored to the "central run" of personal medical choices (refusal of treatment, contraception, end-of-life decisions) and offered a plausible alternative explanation (commercial exchange, professional integrity) for CON's kidney-sale and healthy-limb-amputation examples, weakening their force as clean dignity-over-autonomy wins. CON (A) had a genuinely strong theoretical point—that weight should be measured at points of conflict rather than agreement—but never fully rebutted PRO's reframing of those conflict cases as boundary/threshold exceptions rather than the operative default, and repeated the "gerrymandering" charge without much fresh development in later rounds. CON's opening was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost that cut off part of its foundational case. On balance, PRO gave the cleaner, more resolved path to the motion as a weighing question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2 answers) distinguishing "competence" as testing authenticity of choice versus "dignity" as testing an outsider's moral approval of the choice's meaning, directly defusing CON's claim that both principles suffer equally from the interpreter problem.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B won by successfully exposing that Side A's framework inherently transfers authority over the patient's body to an outsider. Side B effectively neutralized A's strongest examples (kidney sales, healthy limb amputations) by framing them as issues of professional conscription or commercial regulation, while proving that in core medical conflicts, dignity is too easily weaponized against the patient.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Turn 7 distinction between safeguarding (competence filters checking if a choice is authentic) and substitution (dignity tests judging the choice's moral meaning) cleanly defeated Side A's attempt to equate both as standard outsider judgments.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO wins the weighing clash. CON correctly notes that agreement cases (refusing chemo, contraception) do not rank the principles, but its ranking cases—kidney sale, healthy-limb amputation—were reframed by PRO as market/professional-integrity thresholds, not ordinary personal medical choices where the patient bears the burden. PRO's distinction between heavier-than vs absolute survived: a default can be heavier without being unlimited. On the decisive "who decides dignity?" pressure, CON answered that "the practice" decides, which conceded PRO's paternalism worry, while PRO cleanly distinguished competence filters (is it really the patient's choice?) from dignity veto (does an outsider approve?). CON's clipped opening also cost it a full foundation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 2 A1/A2: reframes losses as rare thresholds not central run, and distinguishes authenticity tests from moral-approval substitution—directly neutralizing CON's interpreter and gerrymandering attacks and leaving CON without a patient-side tiebreaker.
