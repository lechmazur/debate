### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON built a more resilient case by reframing "speed-driven harm" as confounded intensity/self-selection rather than a distinct mechanism, using concrete analogies (accumulator chasing, vodka/espresso, the slow-bleed household) that made the comparison vivid and hard to dismiss. Crucially, CON's evasion critique (designers padding animations, renaming micro-markets) and the offshore-migration point were never fully neutralized — PRO's "structural regulation" and "enforcement/international cooperation" answers were plausible but thin, and CON's rebuttal that mandatory intervals still require a number, and that VPN/crypto casinos undercut enforcement, remained live through the final speeches. PRO repeated the same "neurological hijacking/compulsive loop" assertion across rounds without much evolution, and its clarification that fast-product regulation is "additive not replacing" slow-product protection, while a fair clarification, arrived late and didn't fully dissolve CON's equity framing that the motion's comparative "more aggressively" language still implies relative neglect of the larger, poorer pool of slow-harm victims. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions competently with proper A1/A2 structure, but CON's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly around a single alternative (loss-based, speed-neutral regulation) that addresses both harm types, giving the reader a cleaner reason to vote CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.2 vodka/espresso analogy directly undercut PRO's own drug-regulation analogy, showing that "acting faster" doesn't justify differential rules when total harm (volume, vulnerability) is what matters — a rebuttal PRO never fully recovered from, especially since its later structural-regulation defense still couldn't answer the offshore-migration and definitional-evasion follow-ups.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): CON won by keeping the decisive clash on comparative fairness and workable regulation. PRO never escaped the force of “more aggressively than”: admitting real slow-bleed harm while demanding a harsher tier for velocity still leaves the larger, quieter pool less protected. CON’s outcome-based alternative (real-time deposit/loss caps, cross-product exclusion) was shown to hit rapid spikes first inside one equal rule, while PRO’s structural fixes still needed numbers that designers can pad and still risked offshore leakage. PRO’s distinct-mechanism claim stayed asserted more than demonstrated against CON’s same-loop-stretched-over-time answers and repeated definitional pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 / closing chain: universal real-time caps already prioritize fast spikes without a weaker ceiling for slow ruin, collapsing PRO’s “additional not weaker” defense and the structural-evasion reply.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): B won because A never overcame the equity and arbitrariness problems with speed-tiered regulation. B showed that slower forms like lotteries harm a larger, poorer population, so giving them weaker ceilings is unjust, and that "structural" speed rules still require arbitrary thresholds designers can game. A repeatedly asserted that fast gambling produces a qualitatively distinct harm but did not adequately explain why that distinctness requires a separate harsher tier rather than being absorbed by universal loss-based safeguards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to A's structural-regulation defense and the equity reframe in closing, especially the "triage by thrill" line showing speed tiers abandon more victims to protect fewer.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built a more developed and adaptive case: it distinguished mechanism from outcome ("same destination, different road"), met the arbitrary-threshold challenge with a legal-analogy response (speed limits, drink strength), and introduced a "ceiling vs brake" distinction that explained why universal deposit caps alone are insufficient even if speed rules can be partly circumvented. CON (A) landed a genuinely strong point—that speed-specific rules are porous because players can simply click faster, which undercuts PRO's proposed remedies—but never developed this into a full answer to PRO's ceiling/brake and multiplicative-exposure reasoning in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, both of which were notably shorter and largely repeated earlier framing rather than engaging PRO's newest distinctions. PRO's answers to the Pressure Questions (A1/A2) were substantive and directly responsive; CON's A1 in Rebuttal 2 largely dodged the specific "identical rules for lottery vs. engineered slot" hypothetical instead of confronting it. Across rounds, PRO progressively sharpened and complicated the comparison while CON's case, though clear and consistently argued, stalled on repetition of its two core claims without fully neutralizing PRO's later refinements.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer distinguishing deposit caps as a "ceiling, not a brake" (B4.2), directly answering CON's circumvention critique while explaining why speed-specific friction still matters, was the most decisive exchange—CON never squarely rebutted this distinction in its own Rebuttal 2 or Closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the live clash on distinct mechanism and prevention, not identical ruin: speed collapses reflection inside the session, so equal rules under-protect. B repeatedly equated outcomes with mechanisms and leaned on circumvention plus “no perfect threshold,” but never neutralized A’s continuum/road-speed/alcohol analogies or the both-tools weighing (ceilings plus in-binge friction). A answered pressure cleanly and crystallised the vote path; B’s route stayed narrower and more repetitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 / closing mechanism split (ruined life ≠ identical path; fire vs drowning; caps are ceilings not brakes; friction inside the binge) against B’s rate-only and circumvention line.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a credible, distinct harm mechanism—speed and immersion dissolve the pauses that let reflection and regret intervene—rather than merely asserting different outcomes. Side B repeatedly conflated faster loss rates with an identical harm profile and never squarely rebutted why continuous, frictionless play does not create a distinct in-session psychology of chase and trance. Side A effectively answered Side B's line-drawing objection with accessible regulatory analogies (speed limits, alcohol strength), turned the circumvention argument by showing universal deposit limits are also evadable, and demonstrated that stakes and speed multiply harm rather than substitute for each other. Side B's closing overstated Side A's concession on autoplay and ignored the friction argument, while Side A crystallized the clash cleanly around equal treatment being unequal protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal answering Side B's pressure questions: the school-zone analogy showing regulation does not require perfect thresholds to be justified, and the turn that deposit limits are ceilings hit in twenty minutes rather than brakes applied inside the binge, exposing why universal tools alone fail to address the distinct harm profile.
