### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): CON (Side A) built a disciplined, adaptive case: rather than deny the cruelty of extreme confinement, it reframed the debate around burden of proof — should the mandate be justified by demonstrated welfare improvement, or excused only if every alternative is worse? This reframe survived the whole debate and was never fully dislodged. Crucially, CON turned PRO's own concession (that exceptions could exist where replacement worsens welfare) into an admission that welfare outcomes, not equipment categories, should govern policy — the core of CON's thesis. CON also neutralized PRO's "both together" and enforcement arguments by showing PRO's monitoring tools work equally well under CON's framework, undercutting the claim that a categorical ban is uniquely enforceable. PRO's opening was strong (mainstream moral intuition, EU precedent, antibiotic resistance), but the antibiotic mechanism was never adequately defended after CON's rebuttal, and PRO's central late-round claim — that no jurisdiction has succeeded without a categorical ban — was asserted more forcefully than it was proven, especially since CON explicitly noted absence of a named counterexample isn't proof of impossibility. PRO's case also suffered a real execution cost: four separate speeches were clipped for exceeding word limits, including the closing, which was cut off exactly at the moment it was resolving the burden-of-proof crux CON had identified — leaving PRO's strongest closing argument unfinished. CON's closing, by contrast, cleanly crystallized the standing disagreement without new material. Overall CON provided the clearer, more resolved path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4), where it seized on PRO's Pressure Question answer conceding that exceptions could apply if replacement worsens welfare, and used that concession to argue PRO had implicitly accepted CON's governing principle — that actual welfare outcomes, not the mere elimination of a housing category, should determine policy. PRO's Closing began to respond to this but was clipped before completing the point, leaving the exchange unresolved in PRO's favor.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner path on the motion as written: extreme immobilization is a severe continuous floor harm, workable large-scale alternatives already exist, phaseout-plus-monitoring is both/and rather than either/or, and CON could not show a standalone welfare-only regime that actually ended the worst confinement. CON’s instrument and burden-of-proof critique was sharper and forced a real concession, but it remained mostly theoretical against PRO’s comparative real-world case and the motion’s modest framing. Clipping costs mildly hurt PRO’s polish without reversing the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: After PRO conceded exceptions for net-worse replacements, CON’s Rebuttal 2 reframed the clash as competing burdens—prove the mandated conversion better vs prove every feasible alternative worse—exposing the remaining decisive policy disagreement.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A built a highly logical and cohesive case, effectively dismantling Side B's "both/and" defense by showing that if welfare monitoring is necessary to prevent harm in replacement systems, the categorical ban is either redundant or actively harmful when replacements fail. Side A also expertly capitalized on Side B's poorly framed concession regarding exceptions, shifting the debate to the burden of proof. Furthermore, Side B suffered severe execution costs by exceeding the word limit and getting clipped in four out of five turns, leaving several key arguments and their final closing thought incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they sharply distinguished the burden of proof between requiring demonstrated welfare improvements versus presuming a housing ban is correct unless an impossible exemption is met, effectively neutralizing Side B's empirical arguments.
