### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON kept the debate anchored to the motion's five-year clock more consistently and answered pressure with sharper specificity. PRO's core claims — that a CBAM acts "immediately," that clean capacity exists to absorb redirected demand, and that a "race to the top" is already underway — were each directly tested and weakened: CON showed the UK's carbon price predates the EU mechanism, Turkey's scheme is unimplemented, EAF steel is scrap-constrained, and DRI is mostly gas-fired, none of which PRO adequately rehabilitated. CON also introduced a clean comparative reframe (subsidy-driven cost-curve collapse as a market-based form of global leverage) that answered PRO's "domestic tools are capped at 10%" framing without abandoning the five-year certainty argument. PRO's closing then mischaracterized this as a concession that domestic subsidies "have no mechanism" to affect foreign industry, ignoring CON's actual cost-curve rebuttal — a missed rebuttal that weakened PRO's final framing. CON's "certainty versus hypothesis" closing line crystallized the clash more cleanly than PRO's restated climate-club narrative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to PRO's pressure-question answers — showing the UK's carbon price predates the EU CBAM, Turkey's scheme is unimplemented, and clean steel capacity (EAF/DRI) is supply-constrained — did the most damage, since it directly dismantled the only concrete evidence PRO offered for both rapid redirection and a "race to the top."
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making the five-year horizon decisive and showing that the border adjustment reaches only the traded portion of selected industries, not Side B's repeatedly invoked foreign ninety percent. A also exposed unresolved implementation, carbon-accounting, clean-capacity, and fee-pass-through problems while offering already-deploying domestic measures as the more certain baseline. B presented a plausible leakage mechanism, but overstated its scale, relied on weakly grounded exact claims and causal country examples, and incorrectly treated A's concession that subsidies do not compel foreign mills as dispositive of the comparative motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions directly, distinguished guaranteed domestic reductions from speculative foreign compulsion, and showed that B's tariff likewise misses foreign production for domestic consumption. Its challenges to scarce clean industrial capacity and the claimed policy cascade substantially weakened B's immediate-abatement story.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): CON won the five-year window clash. PRO's 90%-vs-10% leverage frame was strong but depended on immediate procurement shifts and a foreign policy race-to-the-top that withered under pressure: clean industrial capacity to absorb redirected demand is thin and scrap-constrained, UK carbon pricing predates EU CBAM, Turkey remains unimplemented, and PRO conceded dirty mills would "initially pay the fee" — revenue, not abated tons. CON kept focus on implementation — no US domestic carbon price to anchor a charge, embedded-carbon verification must be built from scratch despite customs existing, traded heavy industry is a fraction of a fraction because Chinese/Indian demand never crosses a customs form — while IRA subsidies are already deploying gigawatts. That certainty-vs-hypothesis weighing fits the proposition's comparative burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 exposing the measurement gap — customs knows what crosses, not how it was made, US has no domestic price to anchor — plus the timeline counter that UK predates EU CBAM and clean capacity is scrap-constrained, turning PRO's "immediate redirection" into fee pass-through.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A built a tighter comparative case around the correct motion (CBAM vs. subsidies "alone") and consistently converted CON's own concessions into support: when B argued resource shuffling would occur, A turned this into proof that carbon intensity now determines market access, shifting marginal investment decisions faster than a decade-long subsidy buildout. A also deployed a genuinely damaging empirical counter—solar and batteries got dramatically cheaper while coal plants kept being commissioned—directly undercutting B's central "cheap tech displaces fossil fuels" mechanism, and B never squarely answered this point in either rebuttal 2 or closing, instead reasserting the same cost-curve claim. B's legal argument (no U.S. domestic carbon price = WTO National Treatment violation) was the strongest live thread for CON, but A offered a plausible rejoinder (sector-average intensities plus equivalence exemptions, no successful challenge to the EU mechanism in years of operation) that B did not meaningfully rebut beyond repeating the EU-had-an-ETS distinction. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions with proper A1/A2 formatting, but A's answers did more actual work tying back to the five-year marginal-decision framing, while B's answers largely restated prior assertions. Overall, A progressed the debate more, landed the more consequential unanswered rebuttal, and gave the cleaner reason to vote for it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 turn on resource shuffling ("if exporters can reshuffle... the fee is already sorting global production by carbon intensity") combined with the solar-cost-decline-but-coal-still-built counterexample was the most decisive exchange; B never adequately neutralized either point in the following rounds.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing a plausible near-term marginal-investment and foreign-leverage channel that domestic-only policy lacks. Side A raised serious implementation and resource-shuffling concerns, but relied too heavily on categorical, insufficiently grounded legal claims and never showed why subsidy-driven cost declines would outperform an added foreign price signal within five years.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer to resource shuffling: it conceded that immediate plant retirement was unnecessary and shifted the comparison to marginal utilization, upgrades, and new investment. That directly weakened Side A’s claim that only shipping manifests could change.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO kept the decisive comparison live: subsidies alone touch ~10% of emissions directly and leak, while a border adjustment reaches foreign emissions. CON's strongest swings - legal paralysis and resource shuffling - were turned: A showed EU went proposal to operation in ~3 years with market-access moves before full enforcement, that sector-average accounting and GATT XX environmental footing make litigation a drag not a wall, and that shuffling concedes carbon intensity now determines market access and raises the value of clean upgrades at the margin. CON's cost-curve theory was left without a conversion mechanism after A noted solar/batteries got cheap while coal kept being commissioned. That left PRO with a faster, broader emissions channel in the 5-year window.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal on resource shuffling [A2.4] / [A4.1] - reframing B's zero-sum rerouting claim into proof that the fee is already sorting production by carbon intensity and shifting marginal run/build decisions within a budget cycle, not requiring blast-furnace retirements.
