### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built and sustained the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. The military-and-education credits analogy [B2.1] was the debate's most decisive point: pension systems already depart from strict wage-recording for socially recognized contributions, and CON's counter—that those categories are "strictly bounded and objectively verifiable"—never addressed the structural principle PRO was invoking (that the ledger sensibly departs from wage-recording when society decides the contribution matters). That live concession left PRO's core claim—credits are not a category corruption but a consistent extension—unrefuted. PRO also held the moral high ground consistently: the "pay twice" framing and "cash today, poverty tomorrow" crystallization correctly identified that CON's alternative (direct cash and respite) deliberately leaves the pension record blank, perpetuating gendered retirement poverty rather than correcting it. PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions were responsive and on-format, particularly the A2 answer that the care need itself is the mechanism forcing exit, not the modesty of the credit. CON did land real blows—the distinction between verifying a recipient's medical eligibility and auditing a caregiver's labor intensity is sharp, and the "cash fixes the present; credits mortgage the future" inversion was rhetorically effective. The marginal-incentive argument on labor market distortion also retained some force. But CON's position required showing that its alternatives fully substitute for credits, and PRO's Q1 pressure question exposed the fatal gap: cash does not rebuild the contribution record or close the retirement poverty. CON's A1 answer reframed rather than answered this directly. Overall PRO made the decisive clash clearer, answered pressure honestly, and left the core injustice argument standing at close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.1 military-and-education credits rebuttal in Rebuttal 1 was the most decisive exchange. CON's opening predicated everything on pension credits "corrupting actuarial logic," but PRO showed that pension systems routinely award non-earnings credits for military service, education, and survivor status. CON's counter (those are bounded and verifiable) was narrower than the principle at stake, leaving PRO's fundamental claim—that the ledger already sensibly departs from wage-recording—intact through the closing.

- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side B wins by more effectively defending the actuarial integrity of contributory pensions and exposing verification challenges and incentive distortions in credits, while offering targeted alternatives that address immediate needs without long-term system risks. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): PRO wins a clear but competitive debate on three live routes that CON never fully neutralized. First, PRO's precedent argument—that governments already transfer general revenues to credit military service, parental leave, and unemployment periods—was never squarely answered. CON's rebuttal (those are "state-tracked statuses") distinguishes public records from private activity but doesn't explain why care registrations and dependency assessments couldn't serve the same anchoring function; CON needed to show why care specifically should be excluded when the mechanism already exists elsewhere. Second, PRO's transition argument—what happens to caregivers providing unpaid labor right now while CON's professional-services alternative is being built?—was never resolved; CON's counter that "credits don't pay rent today" applies equally to a service-expansion program that also takes years to stand up. Third, PRO's equity/poverty argument was strengthened, not weakened, by the exchange: the absence of credits doesn't stop women from providing care, it simply guarantees they reach retirement impoverished, and CON's own alternative lands the same people in means-tested poverty in the interim. CON's strongest moment was A4.1–A4.2, correctly noting that revocable cash allowances and permanent multi-decade pension annuities carry very different evidentiary stakes—the same administrative proxies that tolerate modest fraud in small programs become riskier when attached to irreversible lifetime benefits. That point genuinely damages PRO's verification defense but doesn't close the debate because PRO's trust-fund and precedent arguments remain live. CON's clipped opening is also a real execution cost. On balance, PRO built the cleaner comparative case: invisibility externalizes costs onto vulnerable workers, existing systems already use the proposed mechanism for other non-wage periods, and CON's direct-services alternative offers no near-term relief.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's trust-fund silo question (B4.3): governments already transfer general revenues to cover credited periods for military service, parental leave, and unemployment, so care credits would use the same proven mechanism rather than invent a novel accounting trick. CON's earlier "state-tracked statuses" distinction was the closest response, but it was lodged in Rebuttal 1 and CON never re-engaged it under direct pressure in Rebuttal 2, leaving PRO's precedent claim standing as the debate closed.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A won by effectively neutralizing the structural objections to the policy. By drawing parallels to existing general revenue transfers for military and parental leave, Side A demonstrated that pension systems can and already do accommodate non-wage periods, defeating Side B's strict contributory baseline. Furthermore, Side A successfully pressed the point that Side B's alternative (direct services) fails to address the core harm of fractured pension records for those approaching retirement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's R2 expertly answered Side B's trust-fund pressure by pointing out that governments already transfer general revenues to cover credited periods for military service and parental leave.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A wins by building a stronger overall case: it linked credits to real downstream welfare costs already incurred, showed workable proxies via existing care-allowance systems, and directly answered B's pressure questions on verification and funding transfers in rebuttal 2. Side B pressed verification and scale risks effectively but left the current-caregiver poverty problem unresolved and treated status-quo externalities as cost-free.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A4.1/A4.3 directly rebutted B's Q1/Q2 on proxies and trust-fund transfers, exposing B's failure to reconcile verification standards with already-operational allowances.
