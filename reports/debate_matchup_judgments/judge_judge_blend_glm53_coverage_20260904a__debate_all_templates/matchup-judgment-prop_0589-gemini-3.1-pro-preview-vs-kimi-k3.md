### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B built a more resilient case by consistently framing cost-effectiveness as a ratio (shootings prevented per dollar) and hammering the point that CVI's "numerator" is unverified while police effects on shootings, via directed patrol and focused deterrence, are measurable and scalable through academies. B's "supply wall" argument — that block-level trust is scarce and erodes once professionalized — was never adequately answered by A, who mostly reasserted that trusted figures "already exist" without addressing scarcity or dilution. A's strongest moves (recruitment crisis, pension/liability overhead, the clever point that police presence undermines interrupter credibility) inflated B's denominator but never supplied a comparably solid numerator for CVI. A's closing "lottery ticket" framing also mischaracterized B's repeatedly clarified point about *directed*, hot-spot patrol, weakening its rebuttal value. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions round with proper A1/A2 formatting and answered pointedly, but B's answers did more damage to A's live route (exposing the unresolved trust-scarcity and evidence-asymmetry problems) than A's answers did to B's (which mostly restated assertions about "structural" diminishing returns). B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly around the ratio metaphor, while A's closing leaned on rhetorical framing that didn't fully engage the strongest version of B's argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 turn distinguishing "money solves a price problem" (police recruitment) from "money cannot solve a supply problem" (credible messengers), combined with the "funeral vs. overhead" failure-mode asymmetry, most damaged A's scaling claim and was never fully neutralized.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B won by locking the debate onto cost-effectiveness as shootings-prevented per dollar and showing PRO offered only a low denominator plus a hard-to-scale, unmeasured CVI mechanism. B repeatedly neutralized PRO's "next marginal dollar" and specialization claims via documented police effects, complementarity, and the credibility supply wall; PRO's cost and targeting points were clear but never produced a verified numerator that survives scaling pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2 answers): credibility cannot be hired/produced and professionalization erodes it, while patrol mechanisms (hot spots, gun-carrying suppression, investigation) are measured—collapsing PRO's scale-and-precision ratio claim.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side B wins decisively by dismantling the scaling and cost-effectiveness premises of the proposition. B's distinction between a price problem for police and a supply problem for community trust brilliantly neutralized A's scaling arguments. Furthermore, B's logical strike that cost-effectiveness is a ratio and A only won the denominator exposed a fatal flaw in A's case. A's closing lottery ticket analogy was effectively swatted down by B's explanation of directed patrol.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantle A's claim that paying existing trusted figures equals scaling, pointing out that money cannot manufacture trust and professionalizing it erodes it, reducing A's plan to a pilot with salaries.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A built a tighter case around the resolution's actual metric — cost per shooting prevented — by consistently pointing to the retaliation-timing window and pricing both sides (officer cost vs. a small CVI workforce). A's pressure question on the cost of saturating hot spots was never squarely answered: B pivoted to "brief, residual-deterrence patrols" without giving any comparative cost accounting, leaving the core cost-effectiveness burden unmet. A also turned B's scaling attack into a tu quoque (police hiring itself faces a scaling/recruitment crisis) and repeatedly exposed that police "incapacitation" is contingent on an arrest that presupposes a shooting already happened, undercutting B's "guarantee" framing. B's rebuttals were competent and grounded (hot-spot patrol research, proactive carry enforcement, cost-of-failure logic), but the "catastrophic cost of failure" argument cuts both ways — police failures (uncleared shootings) are also catastrophic — and B never neutralized that symmetry. A's closing crystallized the clash more sharply and left B's pricing gap exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Questions round (Q1 forcing B to price permanent hot-spot saturation, Q2 on who exactly gets incapacitated given low nonfatal clearance rates) was decisive: B's answer pivoted away from actual cost figures toward "residual deterrence," which A then turned into "a confession that it evaporates" in the closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built the cleaner path by consistently linking concentrated retaliatory networks to a low-cost intervention that acts inside the decision window, forcing Side B to defend diffuse, high-cost police tools whose deterrence decays and whose incapacitation requires post-shooting clearance. Side B landed real pressure on scaling limits and the catastrophic cost of a failed truce, but never fully priced permanent or residual hot-spot coverage or resolved the clearance problem, so the marginal-dollar comparison tilted to A after both pressure rounds and the final weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's framing of arrest as a "receipt for a failed prevention" plus the linked pressure on nonfatal clearance rates, which forced B into residual-deterrence and forensics answers that never closed the timeline gap.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A built a highly compelling case around the "retaliation clock," effectively arguing that CVI interrupts violence before it happens, whereas police tools (arrests, deterrence) largely operate in the aftermath. A successfully pressured B on the true cost of scaling police presence and exposed the reactive nature of police incapacitation. While B made a strong point about the catastrophic cost of a failed CVI intervention, B failed to adequately price the police alternative or overcome A's timeline advantage, making A's comparative case much stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where they dismantled the police incapacitation argument by pointing out that an arrest is a "receipt for a failed prevention" and that police guarantees only trigger after a shooting has already occurred.
