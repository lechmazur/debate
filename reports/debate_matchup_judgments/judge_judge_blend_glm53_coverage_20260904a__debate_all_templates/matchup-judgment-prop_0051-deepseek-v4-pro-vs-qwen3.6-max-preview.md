### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (Side A) built a more mechanistically grounded case and executed cleaner throughout. Three key advantages: (1) The IP-enforcement-vs.-safety-verification distinction in Rebuttal 1 was genuinely sharp—showing that platforms' anti-counterfeit tools rely on brand databases and profit alignment, not physical factory audits, and PRO never answered it. (2) CON's pressure question Q1 ("how does delisting improve worker conditions rather than pushing them to informal workshops?") was the debate's best individual question; PRO's answer—that collective platform refusal creates market pressure and workers migrate to compliant facilities—was speculative and optimistic, while CON's immediate-layoffs counter was mechanistically plausible. (3) CON's A1 pivot in Rebuttal 2 ("volume makes targeted enforcement essential, not inadequate—mandates multiply paperwork, not inspectors") directly and cleanly neutralized PRO's strongest pressure question. PRO's moral framing was consistently compelling, and the "hidden cost already being paid in lead poisoning" flip was a genuine live route—CON never fully quantified why border testing is currently sufficient. But PRO's format issues (three separate clips across Opening, Rebuttal 1, and Closing) represent real execution costs, and the substantive gap in mechanism answers compounded that. CON's alternative (risk-based border testing) remained partially contested but was defended more concretely, while PRO's core compliance-cost mechanism was never adequately insulated from the pass-through-to-sellers critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 distinction between IP enforcement and safety verification: the point that anti-counterfeit tools work because brand holders supply exact databases and platforms profit from protecting those brands, while verifying fabric chemistry or factory fire exits requires physical audits no algorithm can perform, was the debate's most precise and damaging technical rebuttal—PRO treated these as equivalent throughout and never answered why the capability gap doesn't exist.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): B wins narrowly because it better framed the core tradeoff in the motion: ultra-cheap speed is not neutral when it depends on hidden product-safety and labor risks. A had the sharper mechanical objections, especially that paperwork may not catch toxins and costs may pass through to poor consumers, but B gave the cleaner weighing: status quo harms also fall regressively on vulnerable families, and obligations on the highest-volume marketplaces plausibly reduce mass exposure even if some diversion occurs. A’s alternative of targeted border testing was useful but underdeveloped on labor transparency and did not fully answer the volume problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive rebuttal was turning A’s affordability argument by arguing that unsafe products and opaque labor conditions already impose hidden costs on the poor; this made the slowdown and modest price increase look less decisive than A needed, though A’s paperwork-versus-physical-testing response kept the round close.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won the mechanical clash by proving that platform mandates translate to a "paperwork regime" rather than physical safety, while driving vulnerable consumers and sellers to unmonitored channels like TikTok and WhatsApp. Side B leaned heavily on the moral necessity of the motion but failed to overcome A's practical teardown of how these mandates would actually operate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction in Rebuttal 1 between automated IP enforcement and physical safety checks effectively neutralized B's claim that platforms already have the infrastructure to monitor factory conditions and fabric chemistry.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): PRO built a more coherent and adaptive case across the full exchange. The most important single moment was PRO's Q2 pressure question exposing the contradiction at the heart of CON's position: CON asserts harms are rare while simultaneously opposing the transparency mechanisms that would allow anyone to verify that claim. CON's answer—that the scale of harm would be visible if pervasive—is plausible but not decisive, and PRO's "you cannot claim safety in a black box" framing in the closing keeps the contradiction live. On the compliance-theater clash, PRO made a genuine advance in Rebuttal 2 by arguing that platform liability changes incentives entirely: platforms that bear consequences invest in real verification rather than rubber-stamping, which directly addresses CON's concern in a way CON never adequately answered. PRO's platform-as-only-practical-enforcement argument also held up against CON's customs-sampling alternative, since CON never resolved the micro-parcel volume problem—the risk-analytics answer was plausible but thin against PRO's concrete scale point. CON's regressive-tax and honest-small-seller arguments are genuinely strong, and the compliance-theater warning lands real pressure. But two clipped speeches (the Opening and the second Rebuttal) created real execution costs—missing conclusions at key moments—and CON's alternative enforcement model remained underdeveloped across the exchange. PRO's "subsidize velocity with silence" framing crystallized the stakes more memorably. The win is clear but competitive; CON kept meaningful pressure on PRO's cost-to-the-poor vulnerability throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 pressure question [B3.2]—pointing out that CON claims harms are rare while blocking the only mechanisms that could measure their true scale—was the most damaging single exchange. CON's response (visible harm at scale would already be obvious) was the best answer available, but PRO's closing correctly noted that a black-box system produces no reliable baseline, leaving CON's rarity claim epistemically unsupported. This contradiction was never fully resolved and meaningfully weakened CON's central empirical premise.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. A made serious affordability and compliance-theater objections, but B better tied the motion to platform capability, opacity, and externalized risks. A's targeted-enforcement alternative was more plausible for visible product injuries than for hidden labor abuse, and its claim that harms are rare sat uneasily with opposing transparency. B left the cleaner live route: imperfect platform obligations with liability and auditable trails are still superior to relying on overwhelmed customs sampling and after-the-fact recalls.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive rebuttal was pressing the contradiction that A calls harms rare while resisting the transparency needed to measure them, then pairing that with the scale argument that platforms, not border agencies, have the relevant data and leverage.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side A wins by demonstrating the practical failures of the proposition's mechanism, convincingly arguing that transparency mandates result in "compliance theater" that prices out honest budget sellers while fraudsters fake the paperwork. Side A's masterful response to Side B's pressure question—pointing out that billions of toxic garments would produce a visible toll in ER visits regardless of supply chain opacity—effectively neutralized Side B's safety panic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3) dismantled B's 'black box' argument by pointing out that if safety harms were truly pervasive, the sheer volume of sales would produce a visible toll of ER visits and media exposés.
