### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by converting PRO’s workability burden into the decisive clash. PRO’s core case—secret person-level extraction vs. visible categorical discounts, buyer asymmetry, and “if secrecy is required, the surplus story is suspect”—was normatively strong and landed early. CON absorbed that frame, then progressively showed the motion’s “usually disclosed or banned” default needs an administrable line and a remedy that neither becomes cookie-banner noise nor invites floor-raising gaming. Under pressure, PRO repeatedly rebuilt the test (visible rules → same-request laboratory → model-side person-level inference) and retreated from a true non-personalized baseline to bare notice plus factor classes; CON exploited that instability and correctly refused to treat regulator-only audits as satisfying customer-facing disclosure. By closing, CON had the cleaner decision rule: targeted proxy/audit tools without a usually-applicable regime keyed to an unstable distinction. Clipped PRO opening/closing and definitional drift cost PRO the edge they otherwise earned on principle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2/closing demolition of PRO’s shifting “person-level” line plus the dual-failure of bare notice (noise) and factor disclosure (gaming/floor-raising), while refusing to count back-end audits as the motion’s required customer disclosure.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): PRO kept the core distinction alive through pressure: transaction-variation (inventory, time, channel) vs person-level profiling, and showed disclosure does not entail uniform pricing. CON's strongest attacks — no baseline price and gaming raising the floor — were answered by a hold-context-constant reference plus minimal notice, while CON's regulator-only audit implicitly conceded need for transparency but leaves buyers unable to compare or avoid inferred-vulnerability surcharges. Weighing modest disclosure costs against systematic opacity favored disclosure/ban.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers to B's line-drawing questions, redefining exempt as same request/same terms and person-level inference as covered, and replacing a single fictional price with a contemporaneous non-profiled range or factor notice.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a stronger normative framework around the "basic term of a bargain" and successfully defending the distinction between transaction-level context (time, inventory) and person-level profiling. While Side B mounted strong practical attacks regarding line-drawing and "gaming," Side A effectively neutralized them by arguing that firms design their own models and can identify their inputs, and that avoiding surveillance is not harmful "gaming." Side B's late pivot to regulator-facing audits conceded the need for transparency while failing to protect consumer agency, which Side A rightly highlighted as the core purpose of the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal to the "gaming" and "subsidy" arguments, clarifying that avoiding tracking is a legitimate defense against surveillance surcharges, and that transparency does not destroy legitimate, visible market segmentation.
