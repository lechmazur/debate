### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): PRO built a tighter, more adaptive case: it converted the motion's own "slightly reduces liquidity" concession into a weapon, distinguished CON's Sweden/France/Italy examples as broad securities taxes rather than HFT-targeted taxes (a distinction CON never squarely rebutted), and forced a migration/stay dilemma that CON could only answer with "partial migration"—which PRO then absorbed into its own framing in the closing ("this is the trade, accepted in their own words"). CON's colocation rebuttal and circuit-breaker point were genuinely strong and forced PRO to work, but CON kept re-citing the same broad-tax examples without addressing PRO's targeting objection, and CON's Rebuttal 1 and Closing were both clipped for exceeding word limits, cutting off its argument development at key moments. PRO's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly and left CON's live routes (migration vs. spread-widening) less resolved than PRO's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's dilemma in Rebuttal 2/Closing—"if activity migrates, spreads don't widen; if spreads widen, activity stayed and paid, pick one"—forced CON into the "partial migration" concession, which PRO then reframed as proof of the acceptable trade the motion describes, and CON never fully dissolved that framing before its closing was clipped.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): B won a competitive debate by showing that A's historical examples involved broader taxes and therefore did not directly test the targeted HFT levy in the motion. B also made the cleaner comparison between reactive circuit breakers and reducing destabilizing churn beforehand. A effectively exposed B's false stay-or-exit binary through partial migration, but repeatedly characterized the proposal as taxing every transaction, never established that the targeted policy would cause more than the conceded slight liquidity loss, and suffered two clipped turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal most decisively weakened A's empirical core: Sweden, France, and Italy were presented as broad-tax cases with different targeting and migration dynamics, while B explained why frequent churn traders would bear the levy more heavily than long-term investors.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A won by dismantling CON's empirical anchor—Sweden, France, and Italy taxed broad transactions, not HFT specifically—and then exposing a decisive tension in CON's case: mass migration of trading and devastated domestic liquidity cannot both be true. CON's "partial migration" fallback conceded the motion's core trade (some churn exits, some revenue is collected, fair-weather liquidity is lost) without showing catastrophic harm. A's closing crystallized this cleanly, while two of CON's speeches were clipped by word limits, leaving key burdens unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4's dilemma that CON cannot simultaneously claim mass migration (avoiding domestic harm) and devastated domestic spreads, a tension CON never resolved and which A crystallized to victory in the closing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B built a cleaner, more consistent causal case: taxes cannot stop liquidity withdrawal at the actual moment of crisis, so the proposition's core mechanism (tax → stability) never connects, while the permanent cost of wider spreads is real and ongoing. B pressed this with the Sweden tax-flight point and forced A into a decisive self-inflicted concession in Rebuttal 2 ("firms exit when any perturbation drives exit"), which B then used in Rebuttal 2 and the closing to show the tax buys nothing during the crisis it's meant to prevent. A's case remained coherent but suffered real costs: Rebuttal 1 was clipped mid-argument (an explicit execution penalty), A never defended a concrete liquidity-loss magnitude despite being directly pressed on it, and A's late invocation of Italian/French tick-size effects read as under-grounded precision rather than solid support. A also implicitly abandoned the motion's own "slightly reduces liquidity" hedge by reframing wider spreads as healthy, which B correctly flagged as undermining the proposition as written. B's closing crystallized this clash sharply and memorably, while A's closing reasserted its risk-asymmetry framing without directly rebutting the crisis-moment critique that had just been proven against it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3), turning A's own admission that firms "exit when any perturbation drives exit" into proof that the tax cannot prevent withdrawal at the moment of crisis—collapsing A's central stability mechanism.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by exposing the mismatch between taxing trade frequency and preventing instability. It showed that the tax cannot distinguish stabilizing market-making from destabilizing strategies, may drive activity elsewhere, and does not ensure liquidity remains during crises. Side B offered a plausible ex ante dampening mechanism and strong risk framing, but never established that reduced HFT volume produces greater stability or that liquidity losses would remain slight.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange followed its demand for evidence that a transaction tax had reduced volatility. Side B conceded there was no definitive example and cited reduced trading activity instead; Side A sharply explained that lower volume is only a proxy, not the stability outcome required by the proposition.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing the tax is poorly targeted—it hits market-makers who provide genuine liquidity, does nothing to prevent algorithmic flight during crises, and lacks empirical evidence of reducing volatility. Side A's mechanism was plausible but unverified, their Rebuttal 1 was materially clipped, and they struggled to defend the "slight" liquidity cost under pressure, ultimately conceding that wider spreads were acceptable. Side B crystallized a cleaner comparative choice: permanent execution costs for no proven stability gain.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal that a transaction tax cannot keep algorithms in the market during a crash—reinforced by Side A's own admission that perturbations drive HFT exit—combined with the unanswered demand for any real-world example of an FTT reducing volatility.
