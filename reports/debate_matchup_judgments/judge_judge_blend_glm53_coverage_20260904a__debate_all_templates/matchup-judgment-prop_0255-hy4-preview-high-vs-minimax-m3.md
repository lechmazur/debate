### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but A's throughline was tighter and better survived pressure. A's core "deletion must bind commerce" frame was paired with a plausible, ordinary-meaning definition of "data broker" (dossier-vendor with no relationship to the subject) that neutralized B's strongest weapon—the overbreadth/no-textual-definition attack—more effectively than B neutralized A's counters. In the pressure-question exchange, A gave concrete, specific answers (a working definition, and identification of the domestic broker as the "specific seller" the ban targets), while B's answers mostly reasserted its general framework (regulated firms are accountable, offshore isn't) without directly rebutting A's sharp mechanism point that ordinary abusers access data through easy legal domestic brokers, not sophisticated dark-web channels. B's displacement/accountability argument was a genuine and serious challenge, but it was never fully closed off against A's rebuttal, and B's closing repeated the same claims with a citation slip (referencing nonexistent IDs), a minor execution cost. A's closing crystallized the clash cleanly with a concrete test ("may any broker lawfully sell it tomorrow?") that B did not fully dislodge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 answer to B's "which seller does the ban stop" pressure question—distinguishing the domestic legal broker (the actual on-ramp for ordinary buyers/abusers) from the already-illegal offshore/dark-web trade—was the most decisive exchange, because it directly defused B's displacement argument in a way B's subsequent closing never fully answered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A narrowly won by showing that the categorical motion also reaches legitimate credit, fraud-prevention, identity-verification, and investigative data flows. Side B compellingly showed why opt-outs cannot undo a dangerous sale, but its assurances that credit checks and other proper channels remain intact relied on carve-outs absent from the proposition. A's offshore-displacement claim was speculative and its closing used nonexistent reference IDs, keeping the margin narrow.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest rebuttal was that Side B could not simply define legitimate dossier vendors out of “data broker” or preserve proper credit channels under a blanket prohibition. This left Side B's overbreadth answer incomplete despite its effective distinction between lawful accessibility and harder illicit access.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side B won by decisively winning the clash on efficacy and harm mitigation. B successfully showed that A's alternative (regulation and post-sale accountability) fails because it cannot 'un-sell' sensitive data like a survivor's address once an abuser buys it. B also effectively neutralized A's overbreadth argument by relying on the common-sense, commercial definition of a data broker.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 cleanly defeated A's 'dark web' argument by pointing out that the domestic ban closes the easy, legal 'on-ramp' that an ordinary abuser would use, making the data practically inaccessible rather than legally buyable.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): CON built a more durable theoretical frame (deletion as license revocation against one platform, not a universal injunction over independently-held lawful records) and used it to consistently rebuff PRO's sovereignty rhetoric and "peeping Tom" analogy, which never squarely answered why a lawfully-obtained independent copy must vanish on demand. CON's pressure questions were sharper: forcing PRO to admit the counterparty keeps its own record (undercutting the "text as written" claim) and pressing the real-time fraud-detection gap that KYC/subpoenas cannot fill against synthetic identities built to pass first-party checks. PRO's Rebuttal 2 answers were reasonable but didn't fully close that timing gap, and CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3-B4.4) pressed the point home. Decisively, PRO's own closing conceded brokers may retain records for "compliance, legal hold, or audit," which CON's closing weaponized into a clean dilemma between a narrower-than-written ban and continued blinding of the fraud-detection use case—an unresolved tension CON got the last, uncontested word on, compounded by PRO's closing being clipped for length, a real execution cost under the judging frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing (B5.2-B5.4) turning PRO's own concession about compliance/audit retention into a dilemma against "vote for the text as written," combined with the unanswered real-time fraud-detection point from B4.3-B4.4, was the most decisive exchange, landing after PRO's closing was clipped.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A won by holding Side B to the motion’s express prohibition on archiving, not merely harmful resale. Side B repeatedly narrowed the proposal into a commercialization rule and ultimately conceded that brokers could retain records for compliance or audit, contradicting its defense of the written text. Side A also persuasively distinguished real-time fraud prevention from warrants and post hoc investigation. Side B established serious privacy harms and a strong consent critique, but relied on unsupported claims that broker data more often enables fraud and never adequately resolved the ban’s overbreadth. Its clipped closing was an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s closing exposed the decisive contradiction: Side B claimed the motion bans archiving, yet allowed compliance, audit, and legal-hold archives. Side A then paired that textual concession with the temporal point that KYC and subpoenas cannot replace real-time cross-institution fraud signals.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A wins by holding Side B to the actual text of the proposition. Side B attempted to narrow the motion by claiming it wouldn't ban archiving for compliance or fraud prevention, but Side A correctly pointed out that the text explicitly prohibits "archiving." Side A also effectively demonstrated that Side B's alternatives for fraud detection (warrants and first-party KYC) fail to solve the real-time threat of synthetic identities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 expertly dismantled Side B's reliance on warrants and KYC, proving that real-time fraud detection requires the very cross-institution data linkages the motion would ban.
