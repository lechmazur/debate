B kept the choice cleaner: pay the role revenue still reflects.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s repeated demolition that the mover’s alternatives and the firm’s replacement hire remain national remote offers (not Tulsa office wages), collapsing A’s re-localized market story.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON wins by exposing that PRO's fairness and market-based justifications collapse under pressure. CON demolished the housing argument by showing that even a trimmed salary outbids local workers, so the cut does not solve the problem PRO raised. CON also trapped PRO into admitting that moving to a more expensive city should trigger a raise with zero extra productivity, undermining PRO's claim that pay should follow market "mirrors" rather than output. PRO's strongest route—tying pay to local replacement costs—never recovered from CON's "who replaces the mover?" test: firms search nationally and pay near-headquarters rates for remote roles, making the local-labor-market framing implausible. The decisive clash resolved in CON's favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal showing that PRO's housing justification fails on its own admission—the distortion remains after the cut—and that PRO's two-way symmetry actually endorses zip-code-based raises without productivity gains, exposing a core contradiction in PRO's market frame.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully proving that failing to adjust pay would destroy the incentive for companies to approve relocations and would create purchasing power inequality. Furthermore, B's critique of A's "leveling up" alternative as "fantasy math" that would collapse remote hiring went largely unresolved by A, leaving B with the stronger practical impacts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 dismantling of A's "leveling up" argument as fantasy math that would lead to firms simply halting move approvals.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the live decision rule tied to sustainability and incentives: permanent HQ bands after moves make managers stop approving moves, collapse remote hiring, or push offshoring, so adjustment is what keeps the geographic “yes” possible while preserving purchasing power. B landed real damage on selective enforcement against less-mobile movers and on “level up locals, don’t dock movers,” but never neutralized the budget/incentives clash; “use office savings” and national-talent claims stayed thinner than A’s labor-market clearing and “generally, not blindly” framing. A’s closings crystallized that comparative path more cleanly than B’s toll/bait-and-switch framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2 answers (leveling to SF is fantasy math that kills approved moves; “generally” already bends for true national outside options) directly undercut B’s fairness-and-loyalty pressure while restoring the proposition’s default rule.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON wins because it demonstrated that PRO's "generally" standard functions as a leverage test: pay cuts only bind movers who lack rival bids, penalizing loyalty rather than adjusting for location. CON's distinction between new-hire local replacement cost and known-producer retention value—which PRO never squarely answered—cemented a live route to victory about monopsony power disguised as fairness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 leverage critique, which converted PRO's "generally, not blindly" concession into proof that the rule tracks bargaining power rather than place.
