### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B won by tying chronology, audience composition, and electoral leverage into a clearer account of cumulative causal weight. A established that social media independently amplifies and produces polarization, especially through QAnon and Stop the Steal, but increasingly defended current marginal influence rather than the full proposition. A never adequately resolved B's central demographic pressure: the heaviest cable-consuming cohort is also the most polarized and politically consequential, while younger heavy social-media users are less polarized. B also directly answered A's native-online examples by distinguishing organization from the prior construction of frames and audiences.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response to A's shrinking-cable argument was decisive: polarization can persist after identities harden, so continued hostility does not prove that social media supplied the greater original causal force. Combined with the unresolved age comparison, this undercut A's strongest inference from recent trends.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by anchoring the debate in a demographic reality (the young scroll most but are least polarized; the old watch most and are most polarized) that Side B never successfully neutralized. Side A consistently subsumed Side B's mechanisms, successfully framing social media as merely the distribution pipe for audiences and grievances manufactured by cable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4), which effectively dismantled Side B's strongest example (QAnon/Stop the Steal) by tracing its underlying voter-fraud framing back to cable, while solidifying the demographic argument that over-fifty voters decide elections.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B keeps the stronger causal-weight story alive: polarization predates the feed by decades, the most polarized cohort watches most and scrolls least, and that cohort dominates primaries and turnout. A lands a clean hit on the pipe theory with QAnon as native production, but never answers why heavy social users — young people — remain least polarized, redefining the relevant group to "engaged users." The counterfactual and shrinking-audience point is met by stickiness and the fact that cable's self-selected audience is precisely the electorate that polarizes parties.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 demographic + upstream authorship: young scroll most but are least polarized while older cable-heavy voters drive polarization — QAnon answer of one native movement doesn't outweigh two decades of audience capture and frame authorship in studios.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by making magnitude, participation, personalization, and counterfactual dependence the decisive comparisons. Side B persuasively showed that cable creates partisan frames and disciplines political elites, but repeatedly assumed that authorship means greater causal impact. Its claim that online conflict becomes politically meaningful only through cable was too broad given A's platform-native examples and explanation of social enforcement. A better established that social media both amplifies cable and independently generates polarization at population scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest exchange answered the depth and participation challenges: incidental algorithmic exposure reaches beyond cable loyalists, while public online conflict creates an escalation ratchet that passive viewing cannot. Side B's primary-enforcement response was sharp, but it established a cable mechanism among activists rather than overcoming A's broader scale comparison.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side A won by effectively framing "democratic politics" around institutional and party-level mechanisms (like primary elections) rather than just interpersonal conflict. Side A's argument that cable provided the partisan "script" and the electoral enforcement mechanism proved slightly more durable than Side B's reliance on the scale and interpersonal friction of social media.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A2 was the decisive moment, brilliantly answering Side B's pressure about "enforcement" by pointing to primary elections and litmus tests, proving cable creates systemic political enforcers, not just spectators.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): PRO won the magnitude debate. CON recast social as mere multiplier of cable, but PRO's reach-person-hours, incidental exposure, and participation-as-enforcement arguments plus the counterfactual (remove cable feed still polarizes via QAnon/local rumors; remove feed cable collapses) were never fully neutralized. CON's depth/primary answer redefined enforcement rather than matching the pile-on/group-chat mechanism. On the proposition as written - who did more to polarize politics broadly - PRO's scale + personalization + participation path remained live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1/R2 counterfactual that cable's clips depend on the feed but the feed has platform-native engines (QAnon, rage-bait influencers, local rumor networks) and that targets like school boards/election workers were never cable characters, directly undercut B's downstream/multiplier defense.
