### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending the motion as a conditional, targeted trade and by showing that developing non-historic sites does not justify permanently excluding valuable, transit-rich historic land. Side A effectively exposed the weakness in B's claimed contradiction: protections can make redevelopment uneconomical now while relaxation changes that math. But A relied too heavily on unsupported claims that historic redevelopment yields only marginal luxury housing and that abundant alternative sites can satisfy the need. B more convincingly weighed recurring housing scarcity and displacement against finite architectural loss while accepting the motion's difficult cost directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was its answer to the alternative-sites argument: cities can build on parking lots and commercial corridors while also relaxing preservation where it blocks substantial density. This deprived Side A's central comparison of its assumed either-or structure, even though A successfully challenged B's separate claim that its position was internally contradictory.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by forcing a clean dilemma on CON's economics (protections either fail to bind or bind only where unit gains are largest) and then weighing compounding housing harms against finite architectural loss under the motion's actual conditional trigger. CON's non-historic alternatives and irreversible-loss case stayed live and coherent but never escaped the frame or showed why the motion's modest trade was worse than status-quo freeze of high-demand land.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 trap (B4.3–B4.5) converting CON's "uneconomical" and "sliver" claims into self-limiting proof that the motion fires only on high-payoff sites.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by sustaining a decisive comparative: abundant non-historic core land offers far more housing with no cultural loss, while historic-district relaxation sacrifices irreplaceable buildings for marginal, high-cost luxury units. PRO’s central R2 dilemma—that historic districts either bind tightly or not at all—was cleanly resolved by CON’s explanation that protections themselves shape economic feasibility; relaxing them changes the math and invites speculation. PRO never overcame the third-option argument or justified why the motion’s explicit sacrifice of significant buildings was necessary.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal (Turn 8) defusing B’s “self-limiting” dilemma by showing protections make redevelopment uneconomical, so relaxing them changes land economics and invites luxury assembly rather than yielding the affordable density PRO promised.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B narrowly won by showing that location-specific density near jobs and transit cannot always be replaced by housing elsewhere, and that preserving significant but non-landmark buildings can impose irreversible human costs too. A offered an excellent reform alternative, but repeatedly treated one responsive hypothetical as if it were B's entire yield and imposed an insufficiently justified requirement to exhaust virtually all other land first.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer that streamlined procedure cannot overcome a substantive historic-height or demolition veto directly weakened A's central distinction between reforming process and relaxing protection. The subway-corner example established at least one concrete case where preserving the structure itself blocks substantial housing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) built the clearer path by granting procedural and non-contributing reforms while forcing the motion's unique price tag—losing significant buildings—to justify itself, then showing under pressure that PRO's necessity case shrank to a thin hypo that alternatives (infill, inner-ring upzoning, height/zoning changes) already cover. Side B (PRO) kept location, human irreversibility, and veto power live but never fully neutralized the "exhaust reversible remedies first" weighing or the limiting-principle critique, leaving the motion looking like permanent demolition for marginal yield.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's distinction (process/board vetoes fixable without demolition of significant fabric) plus later dismantling of the corner-bank necessity hypo after Pressure Questions.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): CON successfully narrowed the debate to whether demolishing architecturally significant buildings is necessary to achieve needed density. By absorbing PRO's housing goals into a coherent reformist agenda—streamlined review, by-right infill on vacant and non-contributing parcels, and zoning changes on underzoned inner-ring land—CON showed the motion's explicit price was largely avoidable. PRO could not overcome this false-tradeoff framing: their concrete case for necessity shrank to a single hypothetical corner bank, and their limiting principle proved chronically vague because housing need never ends. CON's pressure questions exposed both weaknesses, and its crystallizing refrain of "take the reform, refuse the bill" gave a cleaner, more durable path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's reframing in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 that PRO's process pathologies—ADU vetoes, demolition-by-neglect, and board delay—could all be fixed by procedural reform and infill on non-significant sites without paying the motion's price of losing architecturally significant buildings. This collapsed PRO's practical case and forced it onto a single hypothetical bank.
