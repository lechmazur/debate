### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases and handled the pressure questions with proper A1/A2 formatting and substantive answers. A's strongest asset was the coordination-failure mechanism (chicken-and-egg demand/training/supplier problem) and its charge that CON never offered a concrete mechanism for domestic suppliers to reach scale absent state-coordinated demand—B's "competitive openness/diversified buyers" answer stayed abstract and never fully rebutted this. However, B built a sharper cumulative reframe: it repeatedly showed that the safeguards PRO needed to make nearshoring policy safe (portable standards, multi-user infrastructure, clawbacks, sunset rules) were indistinguishable from ordinary horizontal reform, so the *distinctively* nearshoring content of the motion—local content rules, targeted tariffs, bespoke parks—was exactly the risky, capture-prone residue. B paired this with a political-economy point (once a state has sunk fiscal and reputational capital into an anchor investment, it cannot credibly let the anchor fail, so sunset clauses and gates tend to be extended rather than enforced) that PRO's Rebuttal 2 answered only procedurally (naming gate criteria) without engaging why those gates would actually bind given the incentives B described. B carried this thread cleanly into the closing, landing the final and most decisive articulation of the clash, while PRO's closing reasserted its framing without directly dismantling the "safeguards=horizontal reform" charge. That unresolved reframe, reinforced by the capture argument going essentially unanswered at the political-economy level, gives B the cleaner and more damaging path through the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4–B4.6), showing that PRO's own reversibility tools (industry-wide standards, multiple customers, declining tariffs, clawbacks) are just horizontal discipline in disguise, leaving only the protectionist/fiscal-burden residue exposed to the unanswered capture/sunk-cost critique—repeated decisively in B's closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): A won a competitive debate by keeping the coordination-failure / chicken-and-egg problem live and making it the decisive burden on the motion as written. A showed that CON’s preferred horizontal tools (credit, standards, openness, generic grids/skills) do not by themselves generate the simultaneous demand, scale, and certification domestic suppliers need against established foreign incumbents, so temporary preference and focused industrial policy can justify bounded fiscal and protectionist costs during a time-sensitive nearshoring window. B landed strong political-economy hits on capture, unbinding gates, and footloose “China-hedge” capital, and cleverly argued that A’s portability safeguards collapse into ordinary horizontal reform—but never fully solved supplier entry without a demand bridge, so the comparative case for accepting the proposition’s tradeoffs still favored A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Q1 on supplier coordination plus A4.5/closing: CON’s “diversified buyers / niche first” answer never closed the gap on how small firms finance certification and scale before orders exist, while A treated temporary preference as a bridge with performance gates rather than permanent shelter.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO maintained a live coordination-failure path: generic grids/schools without demand don't get suppliers certified/scaled. That left PRO with the cleaner weighing: bounded fiscal/protectionist costs buying reusable capability versus continued low-productivity structure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently and stayed grounded without fake citations, but B built the more resilient case. B's early identification of the "local-content triangle" (duty-free inputs vs. meaningful targets cannot both hold) and its "strawman" callout of PRO's passive-openness framing forced A into a defensive retreat: A's answer to the trade-off (restricting preferences to packaging/logistics/services) effectively conceded that the policy either produces no real transformation or imposes needless cost, exactly the dilemma B predicted. B's pressure questions (limiting principle, the triangle) were sharper and more load-bearing than A's, and B's rebuttal to A's answers (crowding-out persists, sunset enforcement is politically hardest exactly when needed) went unanswered in A's closing, which mostly reasserted "the exit is written" without engaging the political-economy critique. A's opening and creative conditional-design mechanisms (sunset clauses, drawback regimes, differentiated sectoral policy) were strong and original, but under sustained pressure they narrowed into concessions that B exploited cleanly. B's closing framing—"assets that survive error against bets whose costs survive failure"—crystallized the clash more effectively than A's rebuttal, which kept relying on a "passive openness" characterization of CON that B had already flagged as inaccurate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3–B4.4), showing that A's "limiting principle" doesn't prevent crowding-out or political capture, and that A's retreat on local-content targets to packaging/logistics/services concedes the core industrial-transformation claim, was the most decisive exchange, and A's closing never squarely answered it.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won the decisive clash on whether nearshoring should be prioritized even when protection and fiscal costs rise. PRO’s strongest path was the closing-window urgency claim plus “bounded, reversible” conditionality; CON kept converting that into an institutional-and-competitiveness burden PRO never fully discharged. Sharpest damage came from the local-content triangle (meaningful targets raise costs; weak targets create no linkages) and the incomplete-reversibility point (sunsets do not restore sunk schools/grids/admin capacity or survive job politics). PRO’s later retreat to packaging/logistics preferences and fiscal caps narrowed the prize without neutralizing CON’s baseline of broad tools that still move location choices and retain value after error. A competitive debate, but CON made the motion’s “even if” cost easier to refuse.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 1 triangle (B2.2–B2.3) on local content vs competitiveness, later locked in when PRO’s packaging/logistics retreat undercut the transformation claim.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): B exposed the core tension in PRO's design: if local-content binds, it harms the import-intensive competitiveness nearshoring needs; if waived or limited to packaging/logistics, it abandons industrial transformation. That left CON's robust-to-failure path more convincing.
