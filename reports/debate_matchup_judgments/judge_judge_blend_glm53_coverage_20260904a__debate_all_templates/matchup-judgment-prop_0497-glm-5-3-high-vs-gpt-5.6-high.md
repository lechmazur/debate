### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won by making the decisive clash fitness-for-purpose rather than abstract principle. PRO’s silent-film and “imperfect copy beats no copy” frame was initially strong, but CON repeatedly showed that a deposited book/film print still is the work, whereas a cloud client, incomplete rights package, or frozen major version often is not—especially once PRO conceded tiered/source-where-rights and shutdown escrow. That concession undercut both the “just as books and films” parallel and the claim that mechanical publication criteria cleanly dissolve gatekeeping and completeness problems. CON’s opportunity-cost reply also landed: mass fossil intake can displace the complete, runnable captures that actually preserve culture. PRO kept a live enforcement critique of voluntary/shutdown alternatives, but never restored a workable object of deposit that matches the motion without sliding into a different, broader compulsion regime. Across rounds CON narrowed the dispute more cleanly and left the clearer reason to reject the mandate as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s reply that a film print contains the cultural experience while a dead-server client is possession without preservation, later crystallized as opportunity cost against PRO’s absolute “imperfect copy” claim.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON successfully demonstrated that software's networked, continuous, and dependency-heavy nature breaks the book/film analogy at the heart of the motion, showing that PRO's "published artifact" often yields non-functional digital fossils rather than preserved culture. CON's opportunity-cost argument—that mass ingestion of unusable executables would displace targeted, runnable preservation—remained largely unanswered, while PRO's boundary solution of depositing "everything published" explicitly abandoned the motion's "culturally significant" limit and reintroduced gatekeeping through concessions about source code and server escrow. PRO's rhetoric was polished but could not overcome the structural mismatch CON exposed between the mandate and the medium.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 dismantling of the book/film analogy by distinguishing self-contained film prints from cloud-dependent software clients, and the subsequent opportunity-cost turn showing that mass compulsory intake displaces serious preservation rather than supplementing it.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON maintained the live fitness-for-purpose clash: a book or film print preserves the work, but a client-only deposit for cloud software is possession without preservation. PRO's tiered answer - deposit the client plus escrow server code "where it matters" and accept incomplete records - conceded the dilemma, reintroduced gatekeeping ("who decides what matters?"), and abandoned the "culturally significant" filter for "everything published at major versions," which A showed is neither objective nor covering freeware/browser art. A's targeted verified escrow and shutdown duties answered B's enforcement question while keeping the opportunity-cost point that mass intake of inert fossils displaces runnable preservation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal dismantling B's A1/A2 answers: showing that "source where rights permit, server where it matters" recreates gatekeeping, that major-version rule is not objective and abandons "culturally significant," and that cloud clients can't preserve databases/communities - possession vs preservation.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON won by collapsing PRO’s decisive refusal case against PRO’s own exemptions. After PRO framed owner veto as the motion-deciding failure, CON showed the mandate still arrives too late, exempts impossibility/third-party materials, and seizes only a partial package—while reliably imposing source-disclosure, retroactive designation, and systemic breach risk on cooperative and small developers. PRO kept a live “imperfect primary materials beat none” thread, but never restored a net gains case once CON proved coercion is weakest where PRO claimed stakes are highest. Analogy-break, honeypot asymmetry, and rolling-designation chill remained cleaner comparative reasons to reject.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2/closing turn of PRO's A1/A2 concessions (exemptions, impossibility, partial deposit) into the fatal-asymmetry claim: mandate fails the veto cases it denounces yet still creates permanent coercion/breach harms.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by showing the mandate is structurally mismatched to the hardest cases PRO uses to justify it. When studios dissolve, third-party materials are required, or owners time their refusal badly, legal deposit exempts itself or arrives too late. Meanwhile the coercion and trade-secret risks land heaviest on cooperative creators and small developers—exactly the works voluntary channels already preserve. A crystallized this as a fatal asymmetry: the mandate cannot remove the veto it denounces, yet imposes permanent, systemic harms. PRO’s principled framing—that culture should not depend on an owner’s permission—lost force once CON demonstrated that the proposed mechanism fails precisely where that permission is withheld.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s turnaround of PRO’s decisive refusal-case argument: PRO built its closing on the concession that voluntary preservation often fails when owners refuse, but CON showed the mandate is weakest in those exact scenarios because dissolution triggers impossibility exemptions, third-party dependencies are excluded, and designators must act before the code disappears. The closing line that the mandate’s “coercion is weakest exactly where PRO says the stakes are highest” collapsed PRO’s central comparative claim.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central clash on necessity vs. cost. B established fragile, server-dependent works disappear without deposited materials, and CON conceded in R2 that when owners refuse, "often nobody preserves" that code. CON's best attacks—book/film disanalogy, trade-secret honeypot, retroactive burden—were directly answered by B's scoped safeguards (no credentials, reusable engines, or third-party code; sanitized configs, embargoes, compartmentalization, proportionality/hardship exemptions). A showed mandate would also be imperfect, but imperfect primary material plus community reconstruction still beats community traces alone, leaving CON with a gamble that private incentives preserve public memory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Q1 about encrypted clients and server-code refusal forced A's explicit concession that often nobody preserves it, which B then crystallized as the decisive veto problem in closing.
