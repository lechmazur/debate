### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): PRO maintained a cleaner three-way comparative structure throughout — prohibition, legalization, and the asymmetric model — and never abandoned that frame, which the motion requires. CON landed the sharpest single exchange (the deterrence/distortion paradox: you cannot claim the policy scares buyers enough to work without scaring them enough to distort the transaction), and PRO's "transition friction" answer was only partially adequate. However, PRO's A4.1 response was reasonably well-grounded: deterrence thins demand at the margin rather than requiring every buyer to panic, and the remaining furtiveness, while real, is weighed against the permanent structural violence of prohibition where the seller is the criminal. CON never fully neutralized PRO's trafficking-infrastructure argument (that licensed legal markets provide corporate fronts and banking that hide coercion) — CON's counter that underground markets are harder to inspect is plausible but less specific. CON's strongest framing — that decriminalizing sellers is what generates all of PRO's claimed benefits, making buyer criminalization the net-harm variable — was sharp, but PRO's counter (demand reduction and denied commercial legitimacy are added benefits from buyer criminalization specifically) held the line. Three execution penalties for CON (Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2, and Closing all clipped for exceeding word limits) represent real losses: we cannot evaluate the arguments CON chose to cut, and the pattern signals preparation cost rather than editorial discipline. PRO's closing crystallized the clash cleanly without late evidence dumping. On balance, PRO defended the motion with adequate comparative closure; CON had superior individual moments but incomplete execution across the back half of the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q1 (the deterrence/distortion paradox: deterrence operates through distortion, so PRO cannot claim deterrence works without conceding the transaction-safety damage) was the most incisive single move in the debate. PRO's A4.1 response — deterrence thins demand at the margin rather than requiring panic, and remaining furtiveness is weighed against prohibition's permanent structural criminalization of sellers — was the pivotal answer. It acknowledged the distortion but reframed it comparatively, keeping PRO's route to victory alive without fully resolving the tension CON had exposed.

PRO conceded this furtiveness but failed to prove that pushing the entire market underground was safer than regulating it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing cleanly isolated the comparative by showing PRO's unique benefits were actually shared by full decriminalization, leaving PRO to defend only the added danger of a criminalized buyer pool.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by cleanly isolating the incremental burden of the motion. While B competently defends demand-shrinking and anti-trafficking infrastructure points, B never fully resolves the logical contradiction A exposes. A proves that if buyer criminalization deters through criminal risk, that risk necessarily contaminates every transaction permanently, making rushed negotiations and lost screening a structural feature rather than a temporary adjustment. This logical trap forced B onto the defensive and clarified why A’s preferred regime offers superior net safety.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): This was a genuinely competitive debate decided by several marginal but consistent advantages for B. B's "transaction radioactivity" thesis — that buyer criminalization makes each encounter more dangerous regardless of the seller's nominal legal status — was the structural spine of the CON case, and A never fully neutralized it. A's best answer was that sellers can report violence without self-incrimination, but B's responses about police surveillance of workplaces, confiscation of earnings, and the inability to advertise or unionize maintained real purchase. B also scored a meaningful point by distinguishing Germany and the Netherlands (managed-brothel commercialization) from worker-centred decriminalization, a distinction A never adequately answered — A's rebuttal simply called the latter "untested," which was fair but left B's structural logic intact. A's pressure Q1 was the debate's sharpest question, genuinely forcing B to name a real-world success for full decriminalization; B's answer (New Zealand, plus honest acknowledgment of bilateral data uncertainty) was adequate rather than winning, but the intellectual honesty of conceding mutual empirical weakness and then pivoting to structural analysis as the tiebreaker was more sophisticated than A's continued reliance on Sweden's contested street-prostitution numbers, which B correctly reframed as consistent with displacement rather than safety. A's aggregate harm argument (Q2) was logically sound — market expansion could increase total trafficking even if per-transaction risk falls — but B's response that screened, collective, indoor work is categorically different from an expanded exploitative pool gave a plausible structural counter. Multiple clipped speeches for A represent a real execution cost: at least two substantive turns were truncated mid-argument, leaving the aggregate-harm thread and the Nordic model's empirical record underbuilt. B's case was internally consistent throughout, complete in every round, and grounded in a coherent mechanism even where empirical proof was absent. The "safety is not a pamphlet; it is the concrete ability to say no without losing rent" framing was rhetorically effective precisely because it crystallized a live argumentative distinction rather than offering mere eloquence.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 1 distinguishing Germany and the Netherlands as "commercial brothel legalization, not decriminalization centred on workers' rights" [B2.3] was the most decisive single exchange. A had leaned on these cases as cautionary evidence against full legalization, and B's reframe — that A was conflating managed mega-brothels with labour-rights decriminalization — stripped A of its main empirical comparator without A ever producing a satisfying counter-move beyond calling the labour-rights model "untested."
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won by successfully proving that criminalizing buyers inevitably drives the trade into dangerous shadows, stripping sellers of leverage and safety. Side A argued that a smaller market is inherently safer, but Side B dismantled this by showing that the resulting scarcity and police surveillance empower dangerous buyers and make the sellers' work environments "radioactive." Side A also suffered from significant execution errors, being cut off by the word limit in four out of five speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's explanation that police still surveil and raid sellers' workspaces to gather evidence against buyers, directly neutralizing Side A's claim that sellers face no legal jeopardy.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully dismantling PRO's core causal link between buyer criminalization and seller safety. CON cleanly distinguished worker-centered decriminalization (New Zealand) from commercial legalization (Germany/Netherlands), neutralizing PRO's primary empirical attack. CON's mechanism—that criminalizing buyers forces secrecy, destroys screening leverage, and empowers predatory remaining clients—directly outweighed PRO's aggregate harm reduction framing. CON also more effectively grounded the practical reality of policing, showing how raids targeting buyers still render sellers' workspaces radioactive and reporting functionally impossible. PRO's case was competent but relied on a conflated view of decriminalization and an abstract deterrence model that CON's structural power arguments consistently out-weighed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 and 2 sharply separated decriminalization from legalization, turning PRO's Germany/Netherlands examples into cautionary tales of overregulation rather than worker rights, while systematically explaining how buyer criminalization structurally strips sellers of screening power and labour standing, making per-transaction danger outweigh PRO's claimed market shrinkage benefits.
