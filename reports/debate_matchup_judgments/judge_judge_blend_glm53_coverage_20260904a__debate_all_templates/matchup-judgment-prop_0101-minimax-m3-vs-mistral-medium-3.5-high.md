CON never answered this directly. CON's Rebuttal 1 was notably short (254 words), leaving the Australia exemption point and the friction-as-product reframe unanswered. Both sides had clipped speeches for exceeding limits, roughly canceling as an execution cost. On the live routes to victory: PRO's peer-environment argument stands; CON's LGBTQ+/support-community argument was substantially reduced by the exemptions point and never recovered into a decisive counter-weight.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B (CON) wins on the strength of a sustained, logically coherent pressure campaign that A never fully neutralized. The decisive blow is the internal contradiction B identifies and returns to throughout: PRO's own case calls these platforms "addiction-engineering predators," yet PRO's enforcement mechanism requires trusting those same platforms to build and police a government-fused childhood identity database. A's answer—platforms bear liability, behavioral analysis and device fingerprinting detect misuse—is thin and hand-wavy compared to B's grounded rebuttal that fines pressure platforms to collect more data, not less, and that error margins in behavioral age estimation remain unresolved (plausibly illustrated by the Australia example both sides cited). B also executes a clean turn on the car seat analogy: car seats work via passive, supply-side enforcement; A's scheme interrogates every child online, which is categorically different. PRO's strongest claim—the collective action failure, i.e., 90% participation makes parental opt-out socially impossible—is genuine and B never fully solves it, which keeps the debate competitive. But B correctly reframes the burden: "PRO must prove ONLY a ban works," and the layered design regime (UK Age Appropriate Design Code shifting defaults measurably) is enough to show an alternative path exists, even if incompletely specified. A's closing is thin (176 of 220 words), repetitive on "window dressing," and ignores the predator-keys contradiction entirely—a real execution cost. B's closing is clipped but delivers its central framing before the cut. Overall, B made the clash cleaner, answered pressure more directly, and left the most damaging unrebutted argument on the table.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 turn on the car seat analogy [B2.4] and the "predators building the identity database" framing [B4.5]: B correctly noted that car seats and lead paint bans work at the supply side passively, not by interrogating individual users—thereby collapsing A's own analogies. Paired with the observation that PRO's enforcement mechanism hands Meta and TikTok the role of government-verifying every teenager's identity, this created a contradiction PRO never honestly resolved: you cannot call a company a predator and simultaneously deputize it to guard the gate it is incentivized to leave open.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.5 directly weaponizes PRO's own predator framing against the proposed verification regime, exposing the trust paradox that A fails to resolve.

While PRO built a compelling case around collective action failure and the inadequacy of soft measures, CON consistently demonstrated that a ban requires platforms to build and police a government-fused ID database.
