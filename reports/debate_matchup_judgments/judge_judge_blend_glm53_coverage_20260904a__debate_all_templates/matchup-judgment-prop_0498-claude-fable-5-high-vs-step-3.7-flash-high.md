### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won by making the remedy comparison decisive: separation removes one ownership conflict but leaves the store's gatekeeping levers intact, while A repeatedly relied on transparency and nondiscrimination assumptions that are conduct rules rather than consequences of structural separation. B also better weighed unique costs of separation, including coordination friction and overbreadth, which A mostly dropped. A had a plausible conflict-of-interest story, but used some shaky factual claims and did not adequately answer why conduct rules plus enforcement are worse than separation plus conduct rules.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was turning A's claim that paid placement would be clearly labeled and optional: B showed that honest labeling is a transparency conduct rule, not something structural separation itself guarantees, making A's solution depend on B's preferred remedy.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by demonstrating that every benefit PRO claimed for structural separation—fair rankings, transparent promotion, honest API access—actually depended on stapling conduct rules (nondiscrimination, transparency, labeling) onto the broken-up entity. Meanwhile, CON showed that separation introduced unique, certain costs: fragmented security coordination, defunded developer tooling, and an overbroad trigger requiring no proven harm. PRO never recovered from CON's "pay-to-preference" turn, which proved a spun-off store still had both the means and a fresh profit motive to bury indie developers, and PRO's clipped second rebuttal left key pressure unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's first rebuttal (Turn 4) showing that PRO was inadvertently defending a nondiscrimination rule rather than structural separation, paired with the introduction of the "pay-to-preference" turn, which reframed separation as replacing self-preferencing with auction-driven exclusion.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): CON wins by successfully turning PRO’s core mechanism. PRO argued structural separation eliminates the incentive to self-preference, but CON demonstrated that a spun-off store retains gatekeeping power and gains a new incentive to auction placement to the highest bidder. When PRO defended this by claiming paid slots would be “clearly labeled” and “optional,” CON correctly identified this as a transparency mandate smuggled into the separation model, proving that PRO’s remedy still depends on the exact conduct rules CON advocated. CON also capitalized on PRO’s failure to answer the motion’s trigger (separation based on structure alone, without a dominance finding) and maintained tighter comparative weighing throughout. PRO’s case was coherent but ultimately conceded that behavioral rules are necessary to make separation work, undermining the proposition’s necessity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s exposure of PRO’s reliance on conduct rules to make separation function ([B4.2]-[B4.3], [B5.2]-[B5.3]). By pointing out that PRO’s assurance of “clearly labeled, optional” paid placement is itself a transparency mandate, CON decisively showed that the “chainsaw” of structural separation adds only costs while still requiring the “scalpel” of conduct rules to prevent abuse, collapsing PRO’s comparative advantage.
