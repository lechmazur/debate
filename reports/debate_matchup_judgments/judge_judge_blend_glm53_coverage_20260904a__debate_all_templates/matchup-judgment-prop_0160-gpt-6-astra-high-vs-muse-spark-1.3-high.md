### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins by effectively grounding the debate in the realities of biology and market incentives, demonstrating that Side B's theoretical "safe" consumer device is a structural impossibility. Side A successfully argues that brain variance and plasticity inherently require clinical monitoring and risk-weighing, which the consumer tech model is fundamentally unfit to provide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 A2 dismantled Side B's argument that consumer and medical devices face the same legal obligations, highlighting the structural difference in incentives, scale, and liability between the two markets.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B won the decisive clash by forcing a stable distinction the motion actually turns on: stringent safety controls (screening, limits, testing, withdrawal) versus an extra rule that even demonstrated-safe devices may only be used for treatment. A repeatedly showed why crude consumer launch is dangerous and why eligibility must be serious, but those points supported tight approval standards more than medical exclusivity; B absorbed them and kept returning to the unmet burden on the residual ban. A’s “never for a focus boost” crystallizes a purpose/value judgment rather than a unique post-safety protection, so B leaves the cleaner vote reason.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R1–R2/closing pressure that A defended screening and residual-risk intolerance, not why therapeutic purpose still bars a device after adequate safeguards—capped by exposing A’s “never” as non-evidentiary exclusion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that Side A never justified the decisive extra step in the proposition: restricting access by *purpose* (requiring a therapeutic justification) rather than by *risk* (safety screening, enforceable limits, and demonstrated low risk). Side A repeatedly showed why brain stimulation needs stringent safeguards and clinical oversight, but Side B showed that eligibility screening and ongoing monitoring are separable from the demand that the user be ill. A’s closing admission that nonmedical use could “never” tolerate even residual risk revealed a categorical, purpose-based exclusion that Side B successfully framed as unjustified once safety itself is established. Side B therefore left the cleaner live route: regulate by demonstrated risk, not by medical category.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it exposed that Side A had defended stringent safeguards but not the additional exclusion of therapeutic gatekeeping, and crystallized the clash around Side A’s admission that nonmedical use should “never” be permitted regardless of evidence.
