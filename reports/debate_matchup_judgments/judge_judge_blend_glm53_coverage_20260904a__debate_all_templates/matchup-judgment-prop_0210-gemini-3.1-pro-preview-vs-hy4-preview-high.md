### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side B won by isolating the remedy rather than disputing transparency itself. Its researcher-accessible registry preserved PRO’s benefits for regulators, doctors, meta-analysis, and watchdogs while avoiding an automatic market-access sanction that could deny beneficial medicines, especially in smaller markets. Side A’s answer that firms would simply comply was asserted rather than demonstrated and never adequately resolved the market-exit risk. Both sides used some nonexistent reference IDs, but this was roughly symmetrical.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s second rebuttal decisively answered PRO’s penalty dilemma: calibrated, adjudicated sanctions can punish proven concealment while keeping medicine available, whereas PRO’s binary condition blocks access immediately over any undisclosed negative result, even one irrelevant to the approved use.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won by locking the clash on the motion’s specific lever—binary market denial for any unposted negative, including unrelated ones—and keeping that route live. PRO’s harm case and “fines fail” story were strong early, but later rounds left PRO stuck affirming total public posting of every negative while never cleanly showing why a researcher/regulator registry plus scaled, adjudicated penalties could not achieve transparency without taking medicine hostage. CON answered the pressure questions directly, distinguished automatic product bans from calibrated remedies that preserve access, and crystallized that the sanction falls on patients; PRO’s reply that companies “just publish” and that registries are secrecy with a waiting room was less persuasive after CON’s clarifying answers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s A2 on adjudicated disgorgement/exclusivity loss vs PRO’s automatic post-or-exit trigger, paired with the forced choice on irrelevant old negatives.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won the remedy clash. PRO proved selective disclosure is harmful, but CON conceded that and offered a researcher-facing registry plus scaled post-hoc penalties that preserve access. CON answered both pressure questions directly, showing why a public dump adds misinterpretation risk and why binary pre-market exclusion causes small-market abandonment while calibrated penalties do not. PRO never resolved the irrelevant-trial problem from A3 Q1 and misread CON's registry as secrecy, leaving its central leverage argument vulnerable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 directly answered B's gatekeeping and exit-buff questions, distinguishing binary public-posting as market-access condition from adjudicated disgorgement/exclusivity loss that lets patients keep the drug while punishing concealment.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B won by establishing a less harmful enforcement alternative: severe financial or exclusivity penalties can compel disclosure without making medicine availability depend on corporate compliance. Side A showed why disclosure matters, but its provisional-access solution weakened the proposition and never resolved what happens to patients if access is later withdrawn.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B decisively turned Side A’s provisional-access exception: allowing sales before disclosure no longer makes completed disclosure a true precondition, while later license withdrawal still risks interrupting patient treatment. This exposed the core tradeoff and made B’s financial-penalty alternative comparatively stronger.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive clash on burden and proportionality: once regulators already hold full safety/efficacy data, blocking or later withdrawing market access makes patients the enforcement casualty, while revenue-scaling fines and exclusivity revocation hit the same corporate wallet without removing the medicine. PRO’s provisional-deadline patch either softens the strict precondition in the motion or still threatens mid-treatment pullback, leaving CON’s comparative cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s collapse of provisional access (A4.2/A4.5 into A5.1–A5.3): if sales proceed before full disclosure it is post-market enforcement; if the license is later withdrawn, a responding patient loses a safe drug over a compliance fight.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a live path that CON never neutralized: regulators seeing data does not fix the published record that guides prescribing, guidelines, and future trials. CON's patient-hostage and delay objections were materially answered by summary results, narrow redactions, and provisional access with a binding deadline that still makes sales conditional. CON's alternative of massive fines/exclusivity revocation conceded the revenue lever while leaving the skewed evidence base intact during sales. B's weighing of state-licensed privilege vs private entitlement gave the cleaner reason to vote PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's B2 distinction between approval and evidence ecosystem plus the reframe of the oncology example as temporary non-compliance, not permanent denial, which defanged CON's central collateral-damage claim.
