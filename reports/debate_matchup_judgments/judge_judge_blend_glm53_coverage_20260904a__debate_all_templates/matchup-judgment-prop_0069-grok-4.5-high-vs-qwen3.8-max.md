### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): CON built a cleaner, more sustainable case by exposing a genuine structural problem in PRO's position: when pressed, PRO quietly narrowed "a right to disconnect" to exclude adjudicated findings and domain-bound accountability, a limit the motion's text does not actually contain. CON's Pressure Question forced this into the open, and PRO's answer ("the limit is the motion's wording") never showed where that wording exists, instead just reasserting the narrowed frame. This let CON present a decisive fork in Rebuttal 2 and Closing: either the disconnect right reaches accurate, serious cross-context signals (which is unsafe, per CON's teacher/tenant/fraud examples) or it doesn't, in which case PRO's proposal collapses into CON's own "targeted repair" framework (correction, deletion, time limits, anti-retaliation) and is no longer a distinct case for the motion as written. PRO's strongest counter—that correction and deletion mechanisms arrive "too late" once data has propagated—was a good point and never fully neutralized by CON, giving PRO a real but narrower live route. But it addressed mechanism efficacy, not the more fundamental scope problem CON identified, and PRO's closing leaned on rhetorical repetition ("cut the wire") rather than resolving the fork. CON's adverse-selection and privilege arguments (portable ratings help people without pedigree; disconnection risks pushing evaluation back toward biased proxies like interviews and referrals) were also never squarely answered—PRO's Rebuttal 2 response admitted decision-makers would fall back on "interviews, references, and documentation" without explaining why that is preferable, effectively conceding ground. Overall CON provided the clearer, more internally consistent path to a verdict.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question ("does the right cover accurate fraud/violence flags, and if not, where is that limit in the motion?") followed by Rebuttal 2's fork argument was the most decisive exchange: it forced PRO into a self-narrowing move that was never textually justified, undermining the distinctiveness of PRO's proposed right relative to CON's "targeted repair" alternative.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side B won by successfully framing a dilemma: either the right to disconnect hides accurate risk, or it only targets errors (which is better solved by targeted repair). Side B effectively forced Side A to concede that, without portable scores, decision-makers must rely on interviews and references, which Side B convincingly argued are more opaque and class-coded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 decisively capitalized on Side A's admission that decision-makers would fall back on interviews and references, framing this as a regression to class-coded, opaque human biases that harm the uncredentialed.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): B won the scope clash. A tried to limit the right to "portable residue" not verified findings, but the motion text has no such limit, and A conceded it would still block porting accurate signals across contexts. B's fork - if it reaches accurate recent risk it's unsafe, if it doesn't it's just correction/deletion/time-limits - survived, plus concrete downstream-cleanup answer to A's propagation question and privilege argument that outsiders need portable proof more than insiders.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B2.1 fork: showing A's narrowed definition either still hides accurate fraud/violence when packaged algorithmically or collapses into B's targeted repair of provenance, traveling dispute flags, and deletion duties reaching sold/mirrored copies.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly within the evidence rules, but B better exploited the central seam in A's case: A's repeated claim that disconnect only blocks the "black box" while leaving court records, references, and role-specific checks fully intact. B's rebuttal that "once portable ratings and ban histories can be shut off on demand, what remains is precisely the blind restart they denied" directly attacked this distinction by noting that courts are slow and incomplete and references are curated/self-selected, so the supposed substitutes are functionally inferior, not equivalent. A never squarely answered why these alternatives would work as well once the portable trail is severed; A mostly reasserted that "relevant evidence" remains available without engaging the speed/completeness critique. B also introduced a sharp adverse-selection point (the actors most incentivized to disconnect are exactly the highest-risk ones) that A's closing did not meaningfully rebut. Pressure Questions favored B slightly, since B's questions (who benefits first, does the right shield banned high-risk actors) went to the structural weak point of A's case, while A's answers, though direct, mostly restated existing framework rather than resolving the mechanism gap. A's philosophical framing (fresh starts, coerced consent, liberty) was compelling and consistently argued, but B's more mechanism-grounded rebuttals did more damage to A's live route to victory than A's rebuttals did to B's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4's argument that severing the portable score collapses A's "we preserve role-relevant checks" distinction into a de facto blind restart, paired with the adverse-selection point that high-risk actors benefit first from disconnect, was the most decisive and least answered exchange.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by consistently pressing the practical consequences of an opt-out: it would be utilized most by high-risk actors to hide legitimate, patterned failures. Side A relied on alternative checks (courts, references) to mitigate this harm, but Side B effectively dismantled that defense by pointing out that such checks are slow, incomplete, and curated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 expertly dismantled Side A's claim about 'stale flags' being the primary beneficiaries, logically demonstrating that the highest-risk actors have the strongest incentive to use the disconnect, which would force institutions to raise barriers for everyone else.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): PRO won the necessity vs convenience clash. A narrowed the right to blocking cross-domain composite scores, not erasing underlying court judgments, bans, or role-relevant checks, and pressed why a landlord needs a machine composite across unrelated domains. CON never justified cross-domain portability and relied on intra-domain safety cases that PRO showed could be handled by direct checks. CON's strongest incentive point was answered by PRO's accurate-but-irrelevant/outdated distinction, leaving PRO with the cleaner path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's R1/R2 carve-out [A2.1-A2.2][A4.1-A4.2] that disconnect stops automated ratings/ban-feeds but leaves lawful, role-relevant evidence intact, which blunted CON's blind-restart/free-pass charge and shifted burden to why cross-domain spillover is necessary.
