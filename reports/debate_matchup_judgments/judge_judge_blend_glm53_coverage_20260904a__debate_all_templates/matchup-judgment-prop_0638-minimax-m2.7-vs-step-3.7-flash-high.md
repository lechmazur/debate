PRO's case was more coherent across the full exchange: the core accountability argument CON relied on failed its own test case, the historical examples cut against CON more than for it, and PRO's concession strategy (accepting less auditability while reframing what accountability actually prevents) held up under pressure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): PRO built the stronger cumulative case across the full exchange. Its core arguments—administrative burden (30–40% time on proposals), the systematic exclusion of high-risk fundamental science from short-cycle grants, and the HHMI structural lesson—held up reasonably well throughout. Most decisively, PRO neutralized CON's elite-capture argument by turning it back: the current NIH system already concentrates 70% of funding at 10% of institutions through the very mechanism CON defends. CON never recovered a live comparative advantage on that point. PRO's A1 answer to CON's best pressure question (Q1: how is block grant + peer review structurally different?) was competent and honest—the real distinction is eliminating post-award recurring reporting requirements, and PRO defended that difference as a feature for fundamental science rather than a flaw. CON's counter-characterization that this is "just the competitive system with oversight removed" was rhetorically forceful but question-begging: the entire dispute is whether that type of post-award oversight is valuable for long-horizon fundamental science, and CON never convincingly showed that annual progress reports and recurring re-applications advance democratic accountability rather than merely simulate it. CON's strongest live argument—the democratic contract for public funding—is principled but was never operationalized into a specific harm that block grants (with rigorous initial selection and institutional-level oversight) actually cause. CON's "statutory guardrails are politically fragile" rebuttal was the sharper exchange win, and CON's pressure questions were the best single round in the debate. But after the full exchange, PRO had more arguments that remained genuinely unrefuted and more clearly pointed to what the motion is actually asking: whether the tradeoff of less per-dollar accountability is justified for fundamental science. PRO argued that case directly; CON repeatedly defended accountability in the abstract without squaring it with the specific type of accountability the motion puts at issue.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q1 ("If block grant recipients are still selected through peer review, how is your proposal structurally different from competitive grants—except that winners face fewer accountability requirements?") was the most incisive moment in the debate, forcing PRO to define the actual mechanism. PRO's A1 was the decisive response: the structural difference is the elimination of recurring post-award reporting and re-application requirements, and PRO defended that removal as the specific feature that allows fundamental science to proceed without being redirected toward safe, short-term deliverables. CON's rejoinder—"that is just removing oversight"—was rhetorically effective but never demonstrated why that specific post-award cycle serves democratic accountability rather than bureaucratic compliance theater. PRO's prior turn had already landed the unrebutted 70% concentration figure as evidence that the current system fails on CON's own terms, leaving CON's democratic-contract argument floating without a comparative anchor.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): A won because it gave the clearer comparative reason why short-cycle project grants are specifically ill-suited to fundamental science: they incentivize safe incremental work, repeated reapplication, and heavy administrative burden. B landed real concerns about accountability, elite capture, and public legitimacy, but often overstated A as abolishing accountability altogether and never developed a sufficiently concrete alternative beyond vague restructuring of competitive review. A had some brittle and late statistics, but its core mechanism remained more responsive to the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's best exchange was against B's claim that existing competitive long grants solve the problem: A argued they mostly reward already proven performers and preserve the short-term deliverable treadmill, leaving high-risk fundamental science still underfunded.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (CON)): B won by consistently defending the necessity of ongoing democratic accountability and exposing A’s core concession that the model removes post-selection oversight. B’s rebuttals directly neutralized A’s HHMI and waste arguments while sharpening the public-funding tradeoff. A’s opening and examples were solid but weakened by later clipped execution and failure to answer the accountability burden.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.5–B2.7 and B4.1–B4.5 exposed that A’s “peer-selected block grants” equal competitive selection minus accountability, crystallizing the democratic-contract clash.
