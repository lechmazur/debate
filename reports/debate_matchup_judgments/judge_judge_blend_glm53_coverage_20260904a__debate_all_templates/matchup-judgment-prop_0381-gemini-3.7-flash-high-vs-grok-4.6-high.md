### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built the more rigorous and better-defended case. It kept the debate anchored to a clear metric (maximizing safe indoor capacity tonight) and consistently supported its claims with concrete institutional mechanisms: fire load, bite/allergen risk, cart searches, and—most damagingly—the realism problem that a frightened survivor standing beside her abuser at a rushed intake will not disclose abuse, so "easy screening" is not a real safeguard. PRO's opening and early rebuttals were strong on the utilization/refusal story, but its later speeches leaned on assertion ("waitlists," "trauma-informed protocols separate individuals") rather than engaging CON's multi-causal explanation for empty beds (intoxication, warrants, rule violations) or the square-footage tradeoff argument. The pressure-question exchange was pivotal: CON's Q1 exposed a real tension in PRO's case—if targeted couple/pet sites already work, why must the *default* emergency shelter be redesigned rather than simply expanded targeted capacity—and PRO's answer (framing carve-outs as inherently doomed to "massive waitlists") was conclusory rather than demonstrated. CON's answers to PRO's questions directly re-asserted and sharpened its volume/safety framework. Both closings crystallized existing clash competently, but CON's safety-and-volume frame remained comparatively unrebutted on the survivor-disclosure point, giving it the cleaner, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated point that private, rushed intake screening cannot reliably surface hidden abusers posing as "couples" (first raised in A1.3/A2.3, sharpened via Q2 in Turn 6, and never fully neutralized by PRO's "trauma-informed protocols" answer in B4.2) was the most decisive exchange, since it directly undercut PRO's core safety and throughput claims.

B exposed those unsupported leaps and retained live concerns about animals, storage, abuse screening, and displaced single adults.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that an empty cot does not prove demand for couple rooms, kennels, and expanded storage was decisive. It separated A’s utilization concern from A’s proposed remedy and showed why targeted sites could address that concern without sacrificing emergency-shelter volume.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a sharper, more coherent core argument: that "empty beds" under separation/no-pet/no-storage rules are not actually saved lives if the very rules cause refusal, turning CON's zero-sum capacity math into a fiction for the couples/pets who never take those cots anyway. PRO pressed this reframe consistently, answered the pressure questions directly with proper A1/A2 labeling, and distinguished screening reforms from a "blanket ban," while conceding the debate's own proposed metric ("who is indoors at dawn") and using it to its advantage. CON's opening safety and capacity concerns were reasonable, but its rebuttals largely repeated the same zero-sum assertion round after round without ever squarely engaging PRO's central claim that a couple sharing two adjacent existing cots costs no net capacity, or that a refused single occupant was never truly "saved" by an unused bed. CON's answer to Q2 in Rebuttal 2 dodged the actual dichotomy PRO raised (impossible vs. merely imperfect screening), and Rebuttal 2/Closing introduced malformed, nonexistent reference tags (e.g., "[P4.1]", "[C1.4]") that do not correspond to the transcript's real IDs, a real execution flaw under the grounding rules. PRO's closing crystallized the clash cleanly; CON's closing mostly restated its opening thesis rather than resolving the live occupancy-vs-capacity dispute.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 reframing ("zero-sum math only works if the rejected would have entered... they will not") and its Pressure Questions forcing CON to admit vacant beds don't pull in the very people being excluded — CON's Rebuttal 2 answer never squarely resolved this, instead reasserting capacity loss without addressing why a couple sharing two adjacent cots costs any net beds.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that nominal capacity is not equivalent to protection when exclusionary rules deter occupancy, while offering scalable measures such as paired beds, kennels, lockers, screening, and retained gender-separated options. Side B raised a serious high-demand capacity concern, but repeatedly inflated the proposal into private suites, unrestricted baggage, and unvetted animals in dorms. It also never adequately answered why specialized future housing solves immediate freeze-night exclusion. Its unsupported capacity-halving claims and several nonexistent reference IDs further weakened an otherwise competitive case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A most decisively answered the safety clash by arguing that imperfect screening cannot justify categorical exclusion: shelters already manage missed risks through separation, treatment, and removal, while preserving protected spaces. This exposed Side B's tendency to treat any possible failure as proof that accommodation is impossible.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): B won the occupancy vs ledger clash. A kept asserting zero-sum capacity and overwhelming single demand, but never disproved B's core that beds people refuse do not save lives. B narrowed the costly conversion to paired cots, off-floor kennels, and locker bins, answered safety with same screening logic A already relies on, and framed the vote as who is indoors at dawn. That route stayed live while A's “another single instantly fills” claim was asserted not shown.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.9 directly answering A3.1/A3.2 and turning A’s lifeboat metaphor: vacant singles pull nobody if the couple/pet owner stays outside, exposing that zero-sum only bites if every locker displaces an entrant, which B denied.
