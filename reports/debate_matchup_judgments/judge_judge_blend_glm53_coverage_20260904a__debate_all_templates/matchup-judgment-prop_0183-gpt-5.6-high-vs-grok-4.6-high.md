### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and avoided fake precision, but B built the more disciplined and ultimately more damaging case. B's early distinction between "preserve" and "serve" set up a durable frame, and its pressure questions forced A into the key concession that "no regime can literally uncopy a file"—a concession B then exploited relentlessly, showing that revocation, quarantine, or loss-of-status sanctions only reassign institutional custody rather than restore the creator's actual right to decide whether a copy exists. B also directly neutralized A's central necessity argument (that ownership can't be identified before disappearance) by offering a narrower "good-faith last-copy rescue" privilege that handles the dying-webpage scenario without requiring blanket institutional immunity; A never squarely re-engaged this scale-vs-identification distinction in Rebuttal 2 or the closing. A's asymmetric-irreversibility framing (over-preservation is governable, under-preservation is permanent) remained rhetorically strong through the closing, but it rested on enforcement architecture (regulatory quarantine, eligibility revocation) that B repeatedly showed was more promise than remedy, especially once A conceded that "preservation" already includes indexing, testing, migration, and researcher access—undercutting the "unread dark copy" framing B attacked as "wordplay." The debate was close and both sides adapted well round to round, but B's rebuttals did more concrete damage to A's live mechanism, leaving B with the cleaner path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to A's Q1 (A1 in B3/B4): countering the "impossible clearance" necessity claim with a narrow good-faith rescue privilege for failing files, which both answered A's pressure question and removed the strongest real-world justification for full institutional exemption, a point A never adequately re-engaged afterward.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): PRO won because it built a coherent, practical architecture for preservation at scale and showed that CON's alternative could not solve the orphan-work and pre-disappearance clearance problem. A's distinction between capture and access—copying broadly while fragile, then restricting access before use—directly answered the irreversibility problem and explained how creator interests could be protected through enforceable access rules rather than pre-copying licenses. CON landed sharp blows about the inability to "uncopy" a file and the risks of ingestion-first regimes, but CON repeatedly asserted that "targeted privileges" or "narrow rescue" rules could suffice without explaining how an archive could identify orphaned or failing works across millions of items before they vanished. Because the motion centers on preserving humanity's digital heritage, A's demonstration that B's framework would leave most of that heritage to disappear before clearance gave A the clearer path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's dismantling of the "vault" metaphor and the subsequent "uncopy" pressure was the most damaging material, as it exposed that A's regime ingests in-copyright works at scale before any market-status check and that post-violation sanctions cannot restore a creator's exclusive control over reproduction. However, A survived this by reframing possession as non-harmful when access is restricted and showing that prospective control—through quarantine, transfer, and reading-room closure—adequately protects creators without requiring impossible pre-clearance.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a compelling asymmetry of errors: over-preservation (keeping a dark, unread copy) is governable and causes no market harm, while under-preservation (losing a fragile file while attempting to clear rights) is irreversible. Side A effectively separated capture from access, providing a practical mechanism that addressed Side B's market substitution concerns without sacrificing the archive. Side B's formalistic argument that "possession is the taking" and that files cannot be "uncopied" failed to outweigh the practical necessity of capture-first preservation for digital heritage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they answered the pressure questions by distinguishing between capture and access. Explaining that market checks can happen before lending rather than before preservation neatly resolved Side B's paradox about how to filter marketed works without doing impossible item-by-item clearance upfront.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON built the more disciplined case by insisting throughout on the distinction between securing a copy and distributing it, and by offering a concrete alternative (immediate safe-harbor capture plus retained remedies against misuse) that directly answers PRO's central urgency concern without requiring blanket exemption. Crucially, CON caught PRO narrowing the motion's scope ("not a license to dump a living author's new novel") without textual support, then exposed a live contradiction: PRO's own claim that "a later court can narrow access" is itself the traditional enforcement tool PRO asks to exempt. PRO never resolved this tension in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, instead reasserting emotional framing ("amnesia," "corpse") rather than explaining why full exemption, rather than CON's tailored safe harbor, is necessary once the urgency problem is already addressed. Both sides executed the Q&A format cleanly, but CON's answers were more responsive and its closing sharpened the decisive comparison, while PRO's closing largely restated prior claims without neutralizing CON's contradiction point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.4 catch that PRO's "a later court can narrow access" is itself the injunction/traditional enforcement PRO claims to exempt — a contradiction PRO never cleanly resolved in Rebuttal 2 or Closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by maintaining that preservation and distribution are separable, and that safe harbors for capture can coexist with traditional enforcement against misuse. B successfully showed that A's own appeals to judicial limits and later court review rely on exactly the enforcement tools A seeks to exempt, creating a decisive internal tension. B's framework offered a coherent middle path—protect the archival master while retaining remedies for market harm—whereas A's exemption was too blunt to credibly police the boundary between heritage and commercial substitution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.4 demonstrating that PRO's promise that a later court could narrow access presupposes the availability of injunctions, which are part of traditional enforcement, thereby forcing PRO either to concede CON's tailored regime or admit creators would be defenseless.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively separating the act of preservation from the act of distribution. While Side A relied on the emotionally resonant but legally imprecise claim that copyright enforcement inherently means the deletion of the last copy, Side B demonstrated that courts can enjoin public distribution while protecting the archival master. Side B effectively trapped Side A when Side A admitted access could be "narrowed later," pointing out that this requires the very injunctions Side A sought to exempt. Side A's reliance on an institution's "mission" to prevent overreach was a fatal weakness that Side B exploited to show the necessity of retaining enforcement tools.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it pointed out that if a court can compel restricted access while protecting the master copy, that is precisely the tailored enforcement model CON advocates, trapping PRO in a logical contradiction.
