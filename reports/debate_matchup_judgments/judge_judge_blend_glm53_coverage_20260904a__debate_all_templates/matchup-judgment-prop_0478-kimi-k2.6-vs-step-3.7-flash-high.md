### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate. Three decisive factors explain the result. First, PRO effectively neutralized CON's central surveillance/data-sharing argument: by explaining that each platform independently reports its own payouts to a state-managed account—exactly like quarterly tax withholding—no platform ever learns what rivals pay, and the state verifies cross-platform eligibility through existing 1099/tax infrastructure. CON's counter (that 1099s don't distinguish which platforms paid) has some merit but never fully broke PRO's mechanism, since contributions from multiple platforms to a single worker account are themselves the signal. Second, PRO's Social Security/Medicare parallel in Rebuttal 2 (A2) was the debate's most damaging blow: every salaried worker pays into the social insurance system regardless of private retirement accounts or spousal coverage, so CON's "opt-out of redundant benefits" argument dissolved under its own logic—CON never answered why gig workers should be uniquely exempt from the social contract others cannot escape. Third, CON partially conceded the spousal-coverage point in A4.3 by acknowledging that coverage can disappear due to divorce or job loss, which actually validated PRO's core case while CON then retreated to "this still doesn't justify the broader harms"—a weak pivot. PRO's framing that a legal fiction about paycheck shape should not strip workers of health credits and retirement credits remained largely unanswered throughout. CON's strongest recurring point—that high earners already have adequate tailored coverage—was real but too categorical, and the compliance-cost argument was asserted more than demonstrated. PRO's clipped opening is a real execution cost that prevents a wider margin, but PRO recovered fully in the rebuttal rounds with sharper comparative work and cleaner weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 responses: the Social Security/Medicare parallel demolished CON's opt-out framing, while the 1099-based mechanism answer effectively collapsed the surveillance apparatus CON had constructed as its structural objection. CON never recovered a coherent counter to why gig workers deserve an exemption from social insurance obligations that salaried workers cannot escape.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by making the central comparison clearer: portable benefits preserve schedule flexibility while correcting the gap created when substantial full-time-equivalent work is split across payers. B also leaned heavily on unsupported claims that high-earning contractors mostly already have better coverage, and its second rebuttal accidentally conceded that portable contributions can protect workers whose spousal coverage disappears. A’s social contract framing and answer to the flexibility objection remained the cleaner live path to the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s rebuttal to the privacy objection was decisive: by arguing that each platform can remit based only on its own payouts and the state can aggregate through ordinary tax reporting, A undercut B’s strongest claim that the policy requires rival platform data-sharing or destroys worker privacy.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A systematically dismantled Side B's practical objections by correctly pointing out that pro-rata withholding functions like ordinary tax administration, neutralizing the "surveillance" argument. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but CON (Side A) built a stronger cumulative case across the exchange. The most decisive advantage came in A4.1's response to PRO's Q1: CON drew a clean and underexplored distinction between self-employment taxes (paid by the worker) and platform-funded contributions (which create an employer-side financial stake in controlling who qualifies and how much they work), directly answering why this crosses the reclassification line in a way PRO's "schedule-agnostic" framing did not rebut. PRO's rejoinder—that workers can instantly switch platforms to escape synchronized rate cuts—is plausible in principle but underweights the oligopolistic reality CON identified, where a universal mandate removes any competitive differentiation and makes coordination easier, not harder. The eligibility cliff concern, raised repeatedly by CON, was never given a satisfying answer; PRO's annual self-report mechanism punted rather than resolved the fraud-versus-surveillance dilemma CON posed sharply in Q2. PRO did land an effective blow on the targeting advantage over universal transfer, and the administration-via-existing-platform-data response in B4.2 was cleaner than CON credited, but the clipped Rebuttal 2 and under-length closing represented real execution costs that left live PRO burdens undefended. CON's closing crystallized the "worst of both worlds" framing coherently, while PRO's closing was thin and largely recapped without sharpening the residual clash. On balance, the unaddressed cliff, the employer-stake distinction, and CON's stronger late-round execution tip a genuinely competitive debate to Side A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was A4.1 answering PRO's Q1. PRO challenged CON to explain why an earnings-based contribution differs from ordinary self-employment taxes paid without reclassification. CON's response drew a sharp, principled line: self-employment taxes are calculated and remitted by the contractor, whereas a state-mandated platform contribution tied to labor output forces the platform itself to fund benefits as a condition of using the worker's time, creating a direct financial stake in controlling access and hours regardless of the statutory label. PRO's earlier rebuttal that the mandate is "schedule-agnostic" did not engage this economic-substance argument, leaving CON's key causal mechanism intact through the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A established a real benefits gap and had a strong answer that portable contributions do not legally require full employment control, but its cost-pass-through answer was weaker: worker mobility did not fully answer B's point that a universal mandate in an oligopolistic, price-sensitive market can still be shifted into lower task rates, lower volume, or rationed access. B also kept the threshold and administration problems live, while offering a cleaner comparative frame that universal social insurance avoids platform-managed cliffs and monitoring. A's second rebuttal was clipped and included some reference errors, which slightly hurt execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was pressing A on why identical platform mandates would not simply be passed through to workers. A answered with worker mobility, but B persuasively replied that universal rules and concentrated platforms weaken that escape valve, making the hidden pay cut argument a live and decisive objection.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling the economic and administrative viability of Side A's proposal. Side B proved that a universal mandate would force all platforms to squeeze pay rather than absorb costs, and correctly pointed out that Side A's 'annual tax refund' mechanism for casual workers would still impose a universal payroll architecture on every platform.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response in Rebuttal 2 differentiating self-employment taxes (worker-paid) from an employer-side mandate, perfectly explaining why the latter incentivizes platforms to tighten access and control hours.
