### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but PRO's three-pronged argument (physical limits to engineering, equity under unmanaged retreat, and decades-long lead time for receiving capacity) survived scrutiny better than CON's central mechanism. CON's most original move—that a mandate is a self-fulfilling "verdict" causing capital flight—was clever but remained largely asserted rather than substantiated; PRO's direct rebuttal that markets already reprice risk based on physical hazard data (insurance maps, hydrology) rather than municipal paperwork went materially unanswered in later rounds. CON's own alternative ("contingent receiving capacity," funding triggers) increasingly resembled the very planning PRO advocated, just relabeled to avoid the word "mandatory," which blunted its distinctiveness as a competing policy. PRO's pressure questions exposed a real temporal bind (where do people go, how are buyouts funded post-collapse) that CON answered adequately but not decisively, while CON's own pressure questions produced a genuine dilemma (funded mandate accelerates flight vs. unfunded mandate is empty) that PRO answered with a plausible but thinner "certainty stabilizes" claim. On balance, PRO's throughline—physics and lead time make delay itself an unequal, chaotic policy—was clearer and better defended across rounds, giving PRO the more decisive path to the vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.1/B4.1 response that market abandonment (insurance withdrawal, credit tightening) is already driven by physical hazard data rather than municipal planning documents directly undercut CON's core self-fulfilling-prophecy mechanism, and CON never fully neutralized this by later rounds.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by making the instrument itself the decisive clash: mandatory early relocation planning is not neutral lead time but an official retreat verdict that front-runs capital, equity, and trust, undermining the resources humane relocation needs. PRO pressed hard on biophysical limits, unmanaged inequality, and multi-decade receiving capacity, and CON never fully dissolved the political-paralysis risk. Still, CON’s distinction between contingent capacity/threshold-triggered voluntary buyouts and a capitulation mandate answered PRO’s pressure more cleanly than PRO’s claim that “certainty stabilizes,” and CON crystallized that dilemma through the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.6): contingent receiving capacity and external/threshold-triggered finance answer B’s lead-time/buyout questions without branding neighborhoods as sacrifice zones, while collapsing PRO’s “certainty” reply into either unfunded paper exile or accelerated disinvestment.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): CON successfully undermined PRO's solvency by demonstrating that mandatory relocation planning triggers premature capital flight and wealth destruction, gutting the very tax base and equity needed to fund orderly retreat. PRO's lead-time argument remained strong, but CON's distinction between contingent preparation and mandated capitulation offered a coherent alternative that avoided the self-fulfilling collapse. PRO never fully answered why "certainty of abandonment" would stabilize rather than destabilize markets, and CON's closing crystallization—that the mandate drains the preconditions for humane movement—was the cleaner route to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.3), distinguishing contingent receiving capacity from mandated retreat, and the crisp logical trap that if evidence is clear enough to mandate retreat, it is clear enough for voluntary buyouts.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON built a tighter, mutually reinforcing case: mandatory designation is self-fulfilling economic damage, it institutionalizes climate redlining, and it undermines the very tax base needed to fund the buyouts it promises. CON's sharpest moment was catching PRO's drift between a "binding" mandate (hazard maps, rules against rebuilding, compulsory relocation) and a softer "scenario review" framing, forcing PRO to own the coercive edge of its own model. PRO answered the Pressure Questions honestly and offered plausible financing tools (resilience bonds, land-value capture, phased buyouts), but these answers stayed mostly at the level of listing mechanisms rather than resolving CON's core causal claim that formal designation itself, not just eventual disaster, crashes property values and credit before any money can be raised. PRO's strongest move—that unmanaged private retreat via insurance withdrawal is already chaotic and unequal—was a good frame but never squarely rebutted CON's distinction between probabilistic market repricing and a legally certain abandonment designation. Both sides complied with format and avoided fake citations, but CON's rebuttals did more direct damage to PRO's live route (the coherence of "mandatory yet flexible" planning) than PRO's rebuttals did to CON's fiscal-collapse mechanism, giving CON the clearer path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [A2.2] catching PRO's shift from a hard-edged mandate (binding hazard maps, rules against rebuilding) to "flexible scenario review," which exposed an unresolved tension in PRO's model that PRO never fully closed in later speeches.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON won a competitive debate by keeping pressure on two live burdens PRO never neutralized: codified retreat turns risk into certainty and collapses values/credit before physical necessity, and mandatory planning still requires choosing who gets walls versus who faces compulsory relocation—converting the equity claim into climate redlining/urban-renewal dynamics. PRO’s strongest route (private market retreat is already unequal; only a public mandate creates compensation) stayed live but was undercut by the fiscal-paradox answer (avoided losses are not liquid capital today) and the Rebuttal 2 concession that compulsion remains on the table. CON’s answers to pressure questions supplied a non-mandate alternative path (subsidized elevation, voluntary buyouts, equity-first capital rules), making the proposition’s specific tool look self-defeating rather than necessary foresight.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s A4.3/A2.4 capture of PRO’s B2.3 + B4.2 concessions: planning that chooses which cores get seawalls and eventually uses compulsory relocation is the machinery of dispossession, not an equity floor.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won because it showed retreat is already occurring through private market withdrawal and that mandatory planning is the only framework that can make it organized, compensatory, and equitable. Side B’s economic death-sentence argument never fully overcame A’s rebuttal that predictable schedules beat surprise disaster collapses, and B’s adaptation alternative remained underdeveloped on how it would protect areas that cannot be engineered. While B landed a sharp equity turn by highlighting that planning forces explicit redlining choices, A parried that the same capture problem exists under informal retreat without any binding compensation or democratic oversight, maintaining the cleaner live path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 1 reframe that private markets are already retreating through insurance withdrawal and lending risk, making mandatory planning a stabilizing public response rather than the cause of economic collapse, most decisively blunted CON’s core “self-fulfilling death sentence” argument.
