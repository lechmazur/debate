### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A won by consistently distinguishing electronic waste from verified reusable goods and by emphasizing the certified regional-processing option. This undercut B’s repeated assumption that the policy necessarily sends everything to wealthy-country shredders. B raised a serious classification-cost objection, but overstated it into an immediate collapse of secondary markets and mining surge using unsupported exact figures and an enactment timeline not required by the motion. A also better weighed irreversible health harms against reversible market adjustment, while B’s licensing alternative remained vulnerable to A’s original enforcement and mislabeling critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange was its answer that manual recovery could continue in certified regional facilities, directly challenging B’s domestic-shredding premise. B replied with a capacity bottleneck, but that depended on assuming an immediate ban without concurrent certification or transition and therefore did not fully restore the argument.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON won the feasibility clash that PRO never resolved. PRO promised to ban only waste while keeping genuine reuse, but once it conceded in B4.1 that exporters must pay high-wage verification costs, CON's $50-to-test-a-$30-laptop math showed reuse becomes uneconomic and defaults to domestic shredding. That undercuts PRO's mineral-recovery and regional-hub defense and turns the ban into digital exclusion plus more virgin mining, against which PRO's irreversible-harm weighing was asserted but not proven superior to A's targeted tariff/license alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4-A4.6 exposing that PRO's own A1 concession on testing costs collapses the promise that verified repairables keep flowing, converting the policy into de facto shredding with supply shock and mineral loss.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by exposing the practical and economic contradictions in Side B's (PRO) policy mechanism. While PRO built a compelling moral case against the health impacts of toxic dumping, CON successfully demonstrated that PRO's required testing burden to distinguish "waste" from "repairables" would economically collapse the secondary electronics market. CON's sharp use of the $50 testing cost versus $30 laptop value example in Rebuttal 2 effectively turned PRO's own concession against it, proving that companies would simply shred devices domestically. Because PRO failed to adequately rebut CON's claim that automated domestic shredding destroys rare earth recovery and drives ecologically devastating virgin mining, CON secured both the economic and environmental impacts, rendering the ban practically self-defeating.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it weaponized Side B's concession that exporters must pay for waste verification. By illustrating the prohibitive cost of high-wage testing against the low value of a used laptop, Side A proved that companies would bypass the secondary market and shred devices domestically, thereby collapsing the Global South's tech supply and triggering the exact environmental harms (virgin mining) Side B sought to prevent.
