### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides opened strongly—B with vivid framing (mandatory driving as fictional consent, "tollbooth," trip-mapping into health/religion/politics) and A with a fairness-in-underwriting case and a "de facto ban" warning. The debate's decisive turn came in Pressure Questions, where A forced B to specify exactly what "no-penalty revocation" means. B's answer (A1/A2 in Turn 7) clarified that revocation forces the insurer to fully "forget" pre-revocation observations and revert to the ordinary baseline. A then converted this into the sharper "one-way informational put option" argument in Rebuttal 2: drivers can display good scores while they last and vanish exactly when their observed risk turns unfavorable, without any repricing, which over time erodes the baseline and the discount itself. B's Closing reasserted that the refuser only returns to the "no-program counterfactual" and "no one is subsidized," but never directly answered the systemic erosion mechanism A raised (that exiting bad-risk drivers get absorbed into the baseline pool, forcing insurers to shrink discounts or raise the baseline for everyone). That left A's central technical objection more damaging and less answered at the close, even though B's intrinsic-collection-harm argument (data exists and can be misused even before deletion) also went somewhat unrebutted by A beyond "regulate resale directly." On balance, A better controlled the decisive late-round mechanism that both sides made central, giving it a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question forcing B to admit revocation requires "forgetting" pre-revocation data, followed by A's Rebuttal 2 reframing this as a one-way informational put option that erodes the baseline pool—left substantially unanswered in B's Closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): PRO won by defining genuinely fair opt-in in concrete, workable terms and showing that CON's "de facto ban" framing collapsed once CON conceded that most of those terms—default-off, resale bans, deletion, audits—were directly regulable and narrow. The decisive clash narrowed to whether no-penalty revocation above the ordinary unmonitored baseline makes telematics unworkable. PRO's baseline framing was cleaner: refusers pay the status quo, not a surcharge, and the discount for monitored drivers remains a real reward. CON's adverse-selection collapse story was theoretically pointed but overasserted and treated the unmonitored baseline as static without proving that a default-off system could not sustain monitored discounts. PRO's "privacy toll on mandatory mobility" framing retained its normative force because CON never fully justified why insurers must be able to charge above the ordinary rate rather than simply reward consent. PRO also landed the sharper pressure questions, forcing CON to abandon the broad "de facto ban" claim and retreat to a narrower price-control argument. While CON's one-way bet rebuttal was the most technically inventive moment of the debate, PRO left the cleaner, more intuitive reason to vote for the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4) exposing the one-way informational put option and the adverse selection death spiral from no-penalty revocation with forced data forgetting, which narrowed the entire debate to a single, sharp mechanism and forced PRO into a defensive baseline defense.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): PRO turned the vague "genuinely fair" into a workable core (default-off, revocable, no resale, deletion, audit) and clarified the pricing rule: refuser pays ordinary $1,000 baseline, not monitored $800 discount, with no $200 penalty for refusal. That dissolved CON's de-facto-ban claim and answered Q1/Q2 directly. CON's strongest path — the one-way put option where a bad driver revokes to hide risk — still assumes insurers must be allowed to price above baseline using withdrawn data. PRO framed that as exactly the privacy toll the motion bans in a legally mandatory market, while concrete harms (trip maps revealing clinics, shift work, circumstance penalties) were never neutralized and were co-opted via CON's own direct-regulation list.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 clarifying baseline vs surcharge ($1,000 vs $800 vs $1,200) and that revocation loses the discount, answered by B's Rebuttal 2 put-option refinement — the decisive clash on whether forgetting pre-revocation data creates adverse-selection collapse.
