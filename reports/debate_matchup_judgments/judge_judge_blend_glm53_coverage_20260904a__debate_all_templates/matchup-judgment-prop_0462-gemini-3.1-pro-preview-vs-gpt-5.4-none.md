### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the stronger path to victory by attacking the causal mechanism PRO needed most. PRO's entire practical case rested on the claim that standardizing equal-length cables from the exchange perimeter would neutralize external speed advantages the moment an order crosses the venue threshold. CON directly dismantled this in Rebuttal 2: orders are timestamped by arrival time, so arriving at the door faster still confers the decisive microsecond edge. PRO's own closing essentially conceded this ("let firms compete on those external networks"), which fatally undermined the harm story — if the speed race survives the ban in a less visible form, the hidden tax on ordinary investors survives too. CON's buy-versus-build argument also held: removing exchange co-location does not equalize access but advantages the largest incumbents who can replicate speed through private infrastructure. The "symbolism vs. substance" framing crystallized the key stakes cleanly. PRO's institutional-integrity framing ("referee selling a shortcut") was rhetorically strong and genuinely earned points, and the pressure questions from PRO were reasonable. But PRO never adequately answered CON's sharpest question — why ban this transparent, auditable service but not the equally speed-conferring data feeds, routing, and cross-venue links — and the conceded equal-cable mechanism left the practical case hollow. CON's repeated clipping was a real execution cost, but the core arguments survived truncation and remained live through the closing. Margin is clear but not dominant because PRO's framing was effective and the underlying policy debate is genuinely close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's equal-cable neutralization mechanism: pointing out that orders are timestamped by when they arrive at the venue, so getting to the exchange door earlier through external networks still confers the decisive advantage. This directly collapsed PRO's core mechanical claim and forced PRO's closing into a concession ("let firms compete on those external networks") that undermined the entire harm story driving the prohibition.

CON’s rebuttal that equalized internal cables cannot neutralize advantages timestamped at the exchange door, and that market makers still face cross-venue and news risk, collapsed PRO’s core mechanism. PRO’s integrity framing, while rhetorically forceful, could not overcome CON’s showing that the ban likely harms liquidity without delivering meaningful equality. Side A’s multiple clipped turns were an execution cost that prevents a more dominant margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO’s Pressure Question 2, showing that equal-length cables inside the venue do not neutralize external speed advantages because orders are timestamped at arrival and cross-venue/news risk persists, decisively undermined PRO’s claim that a ban would tighten spreads and restore neutrality.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): CON wins by decisively dismantling PRO's core mechanism. PRO argued that banning co-location and mandating equal-length internal cables would neutralize predatory trading and tighten spreads. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins on the two most important unrefuted lines. First, the shadow-race argument: banning exchange co-location does not neutralize the speed advantage — it moves the finish line to the property line, where only the wealthiest mega-banks can afford to play. PRO labeled this "speculative" and "a smaller external problem to police separately," but never explained the mechanism by which the physics problem disappears. That dismissal is an evasion, not a refutation. Second, the spread-mechanism argument: market makers pushed further from the engine face higher adverse-selection risk and compensate with wider bid-ask spreads, directly harming the retail and pension fund investors PRO claims to defend. PRO's answer — "electronic markets work at low latency even without being in the same room" — is asserted without any supporting mechanism and never engages with the specific causal chain CON built. PRO's strongest material is the principled framing: standardizing an unfair privilege does not cleanse it, and the courthouse analogy is genuinely memorable. The "institutionalize vs. tolerate" distinction is the sharpest rhetorical move in the debate. But that principle does not overcome the two practical deficits just described: (1) prohibition may produce a worse, unregulated shadow monopoly, and (2) prohibition has a plausible mechanism by which it harms everyday investors more than it helps them. PRO needed to either deny those consequences or show they are outweighed; neither was done with enough specificity. PRO also incurred real execution costs from clipping across four of five speeches, leaving the closing argument incomplete at a decisive moment. CON's closing crystallized the clash cleanly: physical reality versus symbolic fairness, regulated utility versus oligarchic property grab. The margin is real but not commanding, because PRO's core principle is legitimate and CON's "anyone can pay" fairness claim is rightly critiqued as mini-club logic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's Q1 pressure question ("How does 'rich firms might buy nearby buildings' justify the exchange itself selling privileged access?") and CON's A1 response in Rebuttal 2. CON reframed the exchange's monetization as standardization — converting an exclusionary private real estate advantage into a regulated utility — and sharpened the point that geography and the speed of light are immutable constraints the exchange is managing rather than creating. PRO called this "speculative worst case" in Rebuttal 2 but never explained why private firms couldn't simply buy adjacent real estate, leaving CON's mechanism standing. This exchange effectively decided the shadow-race sub-clash in CON's favor and is the pivot around which the rest of the debate turns.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO)): Side A won by demonstrating that prohibiting exchange co-location would not eliminate speed advantages but would instead shift them to an unregulated shadow market of private real estate, worsening inequality while raising transaction costs for retail investors through wider bid-ask spreads. Side B's institutional fairness argument never fully overcame this physical and economic reality, and its repeated word-limit violations added execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal exposing that banning co-location simply moves the latency race outside the exchange to private property monopolies, which PRO could only answer by calling it a speculative worst case without engaging the physics of the speed of light or the stale-price spread mechanism.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B wins by successfully turning Side A's fairness argument into a liability. B consistently demonstrated that banning exchange co-location does not eliminate latency advantages but instead privatizes them, creating an exclusionary real estate monopoly that favors only the wealthiest banks. While A relied on abstract appeals to market integrity and dismissed the geographic arbitrage as speculative, B grounded their case in the physical reality of the speed of light and the tangible benefits of standardized, equal-length cables. B's weighing—contrasting A's aesthetic purity test with the concrete harm of wider bid-ask spreads for everyday investors—provided a cleaner, more compelling path to victory. A's failure to adequately resolve the liquidity trade-off or offer a realistic alternative to the physical proximity problem left their case structurally incomplete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to the fair mini-club charge was decisive. By arguing that exchange co-location actually democratizes access through transparent fees and equal cable lengths, whereas a ban would trigger an unregulated shadow real estate grab accessible only to mega-banks, B flipped the fairness metric and forced A into defending an abstract ideal against concrete investor harms.
