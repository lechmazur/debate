### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A won narrowly but clearly by establishing two mechanisms that survived: labels intervene at purchase and incentivize reformulation. B compellingly highlighted affordability, fatigue, and category breadth, but often substituted vouchers and school meals for the consumer choice campaigns the motion compares. A also showed that reducing sugar to avoid a threshold can itself be beneficial. Repeated clipping hurt A, while B had the sharper closing, but B never fully displaced the practical advantages of labels.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange was on regulatory gaming: B argued that threshold avoidance is theater, while A answered that lowering sugar is already a health gain and that campaigns create no comparable manufacturer incentive. B’s sweetener-halo response exposed imperfection but did not negate that comparative benefit.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by showing that warning labels stigmatize shoppers without offering actionable alternatives, force arbitrary line-drawing that catches dietary staples, and invite manufacturer gaming that produces misleading health halos rather than real improvement. Side B (PRO) was pushed into retreating from ultra-processed foods to nutrient thresholds and never resolved the equity problem that poor consumers cannot act on a warning when affordable healthy options are absent. CON crystallized the debate more cleanly: information without capacity is a verdict, not a choice, and campaigns that change price, habit, and skill change behavior while labels merely scold.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal reframing PROs manufacturer-reformulation argument from a policy success into a deceptive health halo that misleads consumers while regulators chase industry adjustments.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by maintaining a tighter focus on the specific mechanism of the prompt: comparing warning labels to consumer choice campaigns (information and education). Side A successfully argued that labels operate at the critical "point of decision" and incentivize manufacturer reformulation, whereas education campaigns fail to reach the shelf. Side B delivered exceptional rhetoric ("stigma without agency," "information without an alternative is a verdict") and made a compelling equity argument, but stretched the definition of "choice campaigns" to include subsidies and fiscal policies, which Side A effectively challenged as slow, expensive, and distinct from information campaigns. Side A's structural advantage on the comparative mechanism and point-of-purchase impact secures a clear, competitive victory despite minor clipping execution costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's reframing of Side B's "gaming the system" argument. When Side B argued that manufacturers dodging labels by slightly reducing sugar is "regulatory theater," Side A sharply countered that reducing sugar and sodium is exactly the intended second-order health benefit of the policy, turning Side B's critique into a concession of the label's structural efficacy.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A won by making the cleaner comparison: warnings operate at the moment of purchase, impose little cognitive burden, and pressure manufacturers, whereas Side B never adequately showed that choice campaigns can reach exhausted and disadvantaged shoppers or overcome marketing at scale. Side B raised legitimate threshold-gaming concerns, but overstated education as universally fast and durable. Its cigarette comparison relied mainly on taxes and advertising bans, which are not consumer choice campaigns, and therefore did not establish the motion's proposed alternative. Both clipped B speeches also imposed an execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest turn was converting Side B's gaming argument into a concession that labels change manufacturer behavior: even strategic sugar reductions can improve products at scale, while education alone supplies no comparably direct reformulation pressure. Side B showed that labels can disappear after marginal changes, but did not neutralize the supply-side benefit or explain how campaigns reliably create sophisticated shoppers under time pressure.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing that industry gaming makes warning labels disappear from nutritionally hollow products, leaving the very shoppers PRO championed worse off, while education provides durable evaluation of any item. CON also turned PRO's cigarette analogy to show warnings served as supplements, not centerpieces. After the full exchange, CON's route to durable literacy remained cleaner than PRO's label-first approach, whose core reformulation and equity arguments took heavy damage from the gaming and regulatory-treadmill rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's demonstration that when firms game thresholds to shed warning labels, the octagon vanishes and the label-dependent shopper sees no cue at all, whereas the educated consumer still evaluates the product—directly undermining PRO's central "tired parent" scenario and proving education handles gamed and unlabeled items alike.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A wins by grounding the debate in the realistic constraints of consumer decision-making and successfully framing the motion as a choice between burdening the victim or the producer. Side A effectively neutralized Side B's strongest point about industry gaming by arguing that even gamed reformulations result in net harm reduction. Side B's reliance on the portable skills of an exhausted parent felt idealistic, and Side B suffered significant execution costs with two clipped speeches, including a cut-off closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they flipped Side B's cigarette analogy by arguing that warnings served as the necessary anchor for subsequent policies, and that the high frequency of food choices makes shelf-cues less prone to habituation.
