### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Both sides argued sharply, but the decisive clash centered on the motion's conditional trigger: PRO needed to show a workable line at which limits should attach. CON's repeated pressure on this ("name the tripwire") forced PRO into a vague standard (telemetry showing "conversions toward zero"), and CON's Rebuttal 2 landed the killer point that no major platform is remotely near that threshold, so by PRO's own test the duty to limit never fires — leaving the motion either toothless or enforced by regulatory guesswork. PRO's closing never directly answered this, instead reasserting finite-attention and burnout claims. CON also successfully defused PRO's cleverest maneuver — flipping the "designed to be deleted" app as proof limits work — by showing it was a voluntary market differentiation (with rivals still offering unrestricted volume, and the same firm still selling boosts), which argues against compulsion rather than for it. PRO's asserted harms (burnout, choice overload, "manufactured loneliness") were vivid but never distinguished from ordinary romantic difficulty predating apps, a gap CON exploited effectively. CON's case suffered real execution costs from clipped opening and closing speeches, costing some final-round polish, but the substantive exchanges that survived were more damaging and left PRO's central operative clause unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.5), showing that PRO's own stated tripwire ("conversions toward zero") never triggers for any real platform, and that PRO's sole counterfactual example was a voluntary business choice by a firm that still sells the mechanics PRO condemns — this simultaneously killed PRO's enforceability standard and defused PRO's strongest pressure-question exploit.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by pressing the missing trigger, separating dating frustration from harms caused by these mechanics, and identifying concrete costs to mandatory caps and friction. Side B’s finite-attention argument was strong, but it repeatedly assumed that organic ranking tracks compatibility and that platforms deliberately suppress reach without adequately grounding either claim. A also persuasively reframed the friction-based competitor as evidence for voluntary product differentiation rather than compulsory uniformity. A’s clipped turns were an execution cost, while B’s closing used nonexistent reference IDs and overstated A’s concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s answer to the friction-based app was decisive: a successful firm voluntarily choosing caps shows that users can select that design through competition, not that every platform must adopt it. Side B never fully answered why voluntary differentiation was insufficient or established that friction itself caused better matches.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively attacking the conditional nature of the motion ("when those features undermine..."). A forced B to define the trigger for limiting features, and when B offered "conversions toward zero" and the success of a friction-based app as proof, A effectively neutralized both. A pointed out that conversions aren't actually zero on major apps, meaning the condition never fires, and that the friction-based app proves the market already offers voluntary alternatives without needing a heavy-handed mandate. A's logical rigor and sharp rebuttals made the comparative case much cleaner, leaving B's harms asserted but its cures discredited by its own examples.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled B's answers to the pressure questions, showing that B's "conversions toward zero" tripwire never actually fires in reality and that B's counterfactual (the friction-based app) actually proves the market solves the problem voluntarily, arguing against compulsion rather than for it.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, competing causal stories (compulsion/positional-rent vs. scarcity-backfires/agency), but A pressed the more decisive contradiction: B simultaneously claimed match-quality outcomes are unmeasurable without "intrusive surveillance" and pointed to Hinge migration as proof users are flourishing. A's pressure questions targeted this directly, and B's answer in rebuttal 2 (revealed preference via app-switching) never squarely resolved the tension—A's closing crystallized it cleanly ("a choice you cannot evaluate is not agency"). B's core mechanism (caps cause risk-averse hoarding on top-tier profiles) was asserted repeatedly across rounds without much development, while A offered a direct counter (caps force recalibration; unlimited swiping already produces winner-take-all lookism) that B never fully rebutted. A also kept returning to an unanswered concrete question—"disclosed to whom, and when does the unpaid user see the auction?"—that B answered only abstractly ("the rules are transparent"). B's case was solid and internally consistent but grew repetitive rather than sharpening under pressure, giving A the cleaner, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure question exposing B's inconsistency between "outcomes are unmeasurable" (B2.1) and "Hinge's success proves users choose wisely" (B2.5/B4.1), which B never squarely resolved and A converted into the decisive closing point.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by connecting the platform's engagement incentives to manufactured congestion, disposable matches, and a positional boost arms race, while emphasizing that the motion requires limits only when quality and trust are harmed. Side A mounted a strong scarcity and time-poverty objection, and correctly dissolved B's claimed contradiction between personal satisfaction and platform measurement. However, it never adequately answered B's distinction between knowing boosts exist and knowing how paid ranking demotes unpaid users. B therefore retained the clearer trust-based route to the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange concerned boosts: Side A framed them as transparent efficiency for time-poor users, but B replied that disclosure to buyers does not inform unpaid users that their visibility has been displaced, and that widespread boosting becomes positional rent rather than genuine efficiency.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won by decisively exposing the contradiction in Side A's defense of user agency and by dismantling the argument for paid boosts with the "manufactured congestion" point. Side A's argument about swipe caps entrenching lookism through risk aversion was strong and plausible, but Side B's overall control of the clash, particularly in demonstrating how hidden algorithmic mechanics undermine informed market choice, secured a solid victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal to the paid boosts argument, pointing out that the platform manufactures the congestion through unlimited swiping and then sells the relief, while keeping the unpaid user's demotion hidden.
