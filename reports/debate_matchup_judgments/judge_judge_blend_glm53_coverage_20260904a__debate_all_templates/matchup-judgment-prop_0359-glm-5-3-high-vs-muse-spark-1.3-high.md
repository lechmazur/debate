A made strong pragmatic points—separate pricing, diner-menu context gaps, viral outliers, tip-skip/off-app risks, hourly-earnings metrics—and answered pressure cleanly, but never fully escaped B’s truth-vs-suppression trap. B better answered A’s dual-pricing and slippery-slope lines (display subsidies; opacity invites crude caps), used the Q/A exchange to force the concession, and crystallized a cleaner vote: if the split survives daylight it costs little; if not, that is the point. Opening clip cost B some polish, not the comparative path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s [B2.1]/[B4.5] line: CON’s predicted harms “depend on people discovering things that are true,” so the case collapses into keeping workers/voters ignorant of terms they would act on.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by distinguishing gross platform retention from profit, showing why pooled costs make the displayed split poor evidence of abuse, and offering aggregate audits as a better oversight mechanism. Most importantly, A neutralized B's claimed driver benefit: payout, time, and earnings history already identify profitable trip strategies, while rider price mainly adds a potentially distorting anchor. B preserved a strong autonomy and market-discipline case, but did not adequately overcome A's circumvention and misleading-framing harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal most decisively answered B's airport example: a driver can compare airport and downtown earnings using payout and time alone, so the rider's fare does not improve that work decision. This sharply weakened B's central claim that the split is necessary for rational driver choice.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): A (CON) won a competitive debate by forcing the decisive epistemic clash: the mandated per-ride “split” is either a bare cash figure framed as skim or an invented allocation of pooled costs, and either way adds little decision value beyond envy while raising ambient disintermediation risk against invisible insurance value. B’s asymmetry and sunlight case stayed forceful and cleanly answered pressure with the three cash-flow facts and the shift-strategy example, but A neutralized both by showing drivers already get payout/time and can learn trip mix from their own earnings history, while auditors can still police abuse from aggregates. A made the tradeoff clearer and kept answering live pressure; B never fully escaped the framing/insurance problem after the concession cycle.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2: auditors use aggregate books (not per-ride invention), and the “week of splits” strategy is already available from own payouts—so rider fare is mainly an envy anchor that kills profitable trips.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully reframing the transparency mandate as a simple disclosure of "cash moved" (rider paid, driver got, platform retained) rather than complex accounting, which effectively neutralizes Side B's "misleading vs. invented" dilemma. Side A also persuasively defends the behavioral impacts of transparency as necessary market discipline and highlights the harm of rider manipulation during surge pricing. Side B's reliance on regulatory audits and the assumption that users are too easily confused by overhead costs feels less compelling for a dynamic, real-time market.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.1 distinction between "accounting profit" and exact "cash moved" effectively escapes Side B's trap regarding arbitrary per-ride cost allocation, clarifying that the mandate only requires showing the actual money movement rather than an invented profit margin.
