### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): B built a tighter, more internally consistent case around reach, equity, and default-changing exposure reduction, and directly turned A's "chemical substitution" objection into a symmetry point (labels face the same substitution risk but leave the recipe untouched entirely), which A never fully answered. A's nutrient-reductionism/matrix-disruption framing was creative and its "health halo" compensation mechanism was plausible, but A's rebuttals leaned on an unsupported precise figure ("30% more") and never adequately addressed B's food-desert/point-of-purchase pressure question—A4.2's answer asserted labels "empower" affordable substitution without engaging the structural access problem B raised. B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("caps change what they consume; labels change what they know"), while A's closing largely restated prior points without closing the equity gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q1 (Turn 5) forcing A to concede that label-driven reformulation faces the identical substitution problem A attacks mandates for, which A's Rebuttal 2 answer (A4.1) only partially deflected by asserting warnings persist despite substitution—an assertion B had already preempted in B4.2.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won through the cleaner population-level comparison: mandates reduce exposure across the entire supply without requiring attention, literacy, motivation, or affordable alternatives. Side B offered a creative substitution and health-halo critique, but repeatedly treated uncertain claims about additives, satiety, and category-wide purchasing collapse as established facts. It never adequately proved that compensation would erase the direct nutrient reduction or that warnings would transform behavior at greater scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A decisively exposed the substitution symmetry: if warning thresholds pressure firms to reformulate, the same alleged chemical substitution problem applies to Side B’s mechanism. Side B answered by shifting toward category-wide denunciation, but did not resolve why warnings would reliably change constrained purchases while mandates immediately change every covered product.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A won by proving that CON’s central critique—harmful chemical substitution under mandates—applies equally to the label-driven reformulation CON relies on, leaving caps as the only intervention that guarantees a binding reduction in harmful nutrients before the point of purchase. A consistently showed that labels require a fragile chain of consumer awareness, comprehension, action, and affordable alternatives, whereas mandates deliver immediate, equitable dose reduction to everyone regardless of literacy or shopping behavior. CON’s aspirational claim that warnings could dismantle reliance on ultra-processed food remained more speculative than PRO’s direct, population-wide mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s pressure question demonstrating that chemical-substitution risks apply equally to label-driven reformulation, followed by A’s Rebuttal 2 proving that only mandates impose binding nutrient ceilings while labels alone guarantee no recipe change, collapsing CON’s comparative case.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases without fake sourcing, and the debate turned on a genuine clash: CON's claim that nutrient caps produce health halos, substitution, and compensatory consumption that leave the ultra-processed diet intact, versus PRO's claim that binding defaults guarantee equitable, universal biomarker protection that voluntary labeling cannot match. CON's mechanism critique was sophisticated, but under pressure ([B3.2]) CON explicitly conceded that labeling "does not give the same immediate blood-pressure guarantee as a cap" [A4.3], and PRO seized on this concession decisively in closing ("CON explicitly conceded... labeling cannot guarantee biological protection" [B5.2]). CON's closing did not directly answer this specific concession, instead repeating the health-halo/pass-fail point already made in Rebuttal 2, which reads as recycled rather than progressing the clash. PRO also handled the Pressure Questions cleanly, correctly limiting the comparator to the motion's actual scope (labeling/education, not procurement or ad bans) rather than being drawn into scope creep. CON's Rebuttal 1 being clipped for length is a real, if minor, execution cost. On balance PRO sustained a more consistent causal story (defaults over willpower, equity of automatic protection) and better exploited the decisive concession in the final round, giving it a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing use of CON's own concession from [A4.3] ("I do not claim that gives the same immediate blood-pressure guarantee as a cap") to argue labeling "cannot guarantee biological protection" was the most decisive exchange, and CON's closing never directly answered it.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a clearer comparative mechanism: mandates reduce harmful exposure by default, including for consumers least able to respond to information campaigns. Side B persuasively raised substitution, health-halo, and food-matrix concerns, but never showed that labeling or education would reliably produce broader reformulation or dietary replacement. Its aggregate-demand mechanism remained speculative and conceded the absence of guaranteed protection. Side A occasionally overstated universality and used erroneous C-reference IDs, while Side B incurred a clipping penalty, but these execution issues did not overturn A’s stronger default-versus-vigilance comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was exposing that voluntary reformulation under label-driven market pressure can use the same ingredient substitutions Side B attributed to mandates. Side B answered with ongoing consumer scrutiny, but did not establish why that scrutiny would be sufficiently informed, sustained, or equitable to outperform binding caps.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by proving mandates change the biological default equitably and automatically, while Side B conceded that labeling cannot guarantee the same immediate physiological protection. A effectively turned B's voluntary-reformulation argument and withstood the substitution critique by framing health gains as pragmatic harm reduction rather than culinary purism. B's clipped first rebuttal and reliance on idealistic consumer behavior shifts left its comparative case weaker.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 turn that labeling-driven voluntary reformulation is slower and less equitable than binding caps, combined with B's admission in Rebuttal 2 that demand-side pressure lacks the immediate blood-pressure guarantee of mandates.
