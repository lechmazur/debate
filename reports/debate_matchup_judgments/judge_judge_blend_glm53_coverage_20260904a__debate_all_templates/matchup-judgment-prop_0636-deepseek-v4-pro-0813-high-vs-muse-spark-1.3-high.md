### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly within the format, but B built and sustained the more damaging line of attack. B's "trust inversion / banner blindness" point (absence of provenance is normal for authentic grassroots footage, so "unverified" becomes noise) was never cleanly defeated; A's attempt to resolve it in Rebuttal 2 by proposing "narrow friction" for unverified political content during sensitive windows was seized on by B as a concession that the system cannot distinguish smear from real footage and will suppress dissidents while sophisticated/approved actors glide through. A's closing tried to dissolve this with a "wallpaper vs. suspicious" binary, but B's closing correctly exposed the flaw: if absence is wallpaper, the fake doesn't lose credibility, it simply blends in with authentic content exactly as B argued — "both tails belong to the liar." B's device/key argument ("decentralization is branding," "a camera needs a license to be believed") also landed harder than A's website-certificate analogy, since B noted evidence (unlike a website) cannot simply be avoided once tainted. A's case was coherent and well-organized, and the pressure questions round was handled competently by both sides, but B's rebuttals more consistently converted pressure into decisive comparative leverage, and B's closing crystallized the clash more convincingly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 turn, catching A's answer to Q1 (time-limited "friction" for unverified political media) as a concession that the system cannot distinguish real dissent from fakes and will suppress the former while letting resourced actors through — reinforced devastatingly in B's closing line "both tails belong to the liar."
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by consistently demonstrating how Side A's proposal would backfire in practice. Side B successfully argued that if legitimate grassroots media frequently lacks provenance, "unverified" labels will become ignored background noise rather than a meaningful deterrent. Furthermore, Side B effectively exposed the trade-off in Side A's fallback plan to throttle unverified media, proving that this would harm breaking dissent.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's attack on Side A's "narrow friction" proposal in Rebuttal 2 was decisive. By pointing out that throttling unverified political content during elections would suppress breaking protest footage ("delay is deletion"), Side B trapped Side A in a severe trade-off.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): CON wins by demonstrating that the mandate either fails to stop adversaries or backfires against authentic speech. PRO never fully resolves the analog hole: stripping metadata and screen-recording are trivial, so determined bad actors bypass the system while honest creators bear compliance costs. CON also sustained the critique that device-key infrastructure creates a de facto regulator-blessed hierarchy of trusted vendors—distinct in form from identity verification but similar in gatekeeping effect. PRO’s “narrow friction” response to the pressure questions was plausible but exposed a lasting dilemma: if absence is common, it becomes wallpaper that lets fakes blend in; if platforms actually throttle unverified content, breaking dissent is suppressed during the very crisis windows where speed matters most. CON crystallized this tradeoff more cleanly in the closing, framing the policy as one that taxes reality rather than deception.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal [B4.1–B4.6], showing that PRO’s “narrow friction” for unverified political media would inevitably suppress authentic breaking dissent during crises while still letting stripped fakes circulate, and that device-key “decentralization” still depends on regulator-approved root authorities who decide which cameras confer credibility.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's rebuttal sequence did more damage to the live clash. B's core move—that signed "captured" credentials can launder screen-recorded deepfakes into false trust, and that the highest-harm viral content (election audio, nonconsensual video, scam calls) arrives precisely in the stripped/re-encoded form the mandate can't reach—went substantially unanswered. A's mitigations (artifact detection, device attestation, revocable fingerprints, risk-scoring unsigned virality) were plausible but B turned them decisively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, showing these are themselves surveillance-adjacent mechanisms, directly undercutting A's central selling point that the plan achieves "authentication without identity." A never squarely answered the metadata-fusion/inference tracking concern raised in B1.7 and repeated in B2.5. A's closing reasserted its framing ("safety without surveillance") without confronting this late, sharpest charge, while B's closing crystallized the exact tension left on the table. A's opening and structural framing were strong and rhetorically clean, but the accumulated rebuttal work favors B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's laundering argument (B2.2–B2.3: a compliant device can sign a screen playing a deepfake, producing false "captured" trust) combined with the closing turn that A's own fixes for this (device attestation, artifact scanning, risk-scoring) constitute the very surveillance A claimed to avoid (B4.4–B5.3) was the decisive, largely unanswered exchange.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully trapping Side A in a contradiction regarding enforcement. Side B demonstrated that since viral fakes often arrive stripped of metadata or screen-recorded, Side A's labels would either be absent or forged. When Side A countered by proposing to downrank unsigned media and use device attestation and artifact scanning, Side B decisively showed that this recreates the centralized scanning and penalization of anonymous speakers (like whistleblowers) that Side A explicitly promised to avoid.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which synthesized Side A's concessions to show that A's defense against laundering (device attestation, artifact scans, and risk-scoring unsigned media) amounts to centralized scanning and surveillance under a softer name.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing that the mandate misses the very harms it invokes. Viral election fakes, forwarded voice notes, and nonconsensual video typically reach viewers stripped, screen-recorded, or re-encoded, so the credential is gone. Side A conceded that determined re-recording cannot be stopped, then patched the gap with device attestation, artifact scanning, and slowing unsigned virality—measures B showed were surveillance-like and burdensome. B crystallized that the system would teach users to treat visible labels as meaningful, making unlabeled fakes appear authentic by default. A preserved anonymity and raised source friction, but could not overcome the core efficacy problem that the mandate prices compliant content, not the worst fraud.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's sustained attack on the coverage gap and laundering problem, culminating in the closing argument that the mandate makes high-harm fakes "invisible in a system that has taught everyone to treat the visible label as meaningful."
