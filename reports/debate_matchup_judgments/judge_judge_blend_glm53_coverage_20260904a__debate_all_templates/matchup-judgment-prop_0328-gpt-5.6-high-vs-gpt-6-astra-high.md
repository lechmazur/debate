### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by demonstrating that the proposition's sole exception for narrowly tailored anti-scalping limits is structurally overbroad. PRO (Side B) defended a conceptual distinction between admission conditions and transfer restrictions, but CON showed that for hard cases—member presales, local lotteries, supporter allocations, and personal benefits—identity itself is the condition, and limiting transfer to eligible substitutes still constitutes a non-scalping restriction that the motion forbids. CON's strategic concession that ordinary unrestricted transfers are unobjectionable in isolation undermined PRO's attempt to justify the categorical rule with easy hypotheticals. While PRO landed strong ethical points about fan flexibility, they could not square their admission-conditions workaround with the proposition's narrow text. CON maintained that a legal right must fit hard cases, not merely sympathetic ones, and their pressure answers sharpened this clash without widening the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answers (A4.1–A4.3), where CON conceded PRO's easy-case hypothetical but showed that PRO's distinction between holding and using a ticket collapses in practice, and that the motion's categorical rule cannot be rescued by unstated qualifications like reasonable administrative costs.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON's overbreadth route survives: proposition permits only narrowly tailored anti-scalping limits, yet CON shows legitimate non-scalping designs - personal member benefit, local lottery anti-proxy - that PRO must smuggle in as 'admission conditions.' PRO's holding/using distinction works for student tickets but not for identity-as-condition cases, and its answer to cost/vagueness adds 'reasonable/workable' standards absent from the motion. PRO wins the sympathetic ordinary gift case - CON concedes it - but CON proves one valid personal-ticket design defeats a universal right.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1-A2.4 / A4.1: showing PRO's necessary student/member/community restrictions are non-scalping limits on effective transfer not authorized by the text, turning PRO's own qualifications into proof the exception is too narrow.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively attacking the structural wording of the proposition. While Side A argued well for a general right to transfer, Side B correctly identified that the motion's exception ("narrowly tailored anti-scalping limits") is too narrow, as organizers have many legitimate non-scalping reasons to restrict transfer (e.g., personal membership benefits, local lotteries, proxy prevention). Side A's attempt to distinguish between "admission conditions" and "transfer restrictions" broke down when the condition itself was the identity of the buyer, forcing Side A to smuggle in unwritten exceptions like "reasonable" costs and "workable" routes. Side B's logical rigor and textual discipline made their path to victory much cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8), where they dismantled Side A's distinction between "holding" and "using" an entitlement, showing that a right to transfer a personal ticket to an ineligible person is empty paperwork, while a meaningful right requires organizers to accept substitutes, which inherently permits non-scalping transfer restrictions.
