### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): The decisive clash was whether risk-equalization is itself "actuarial fairness" (CON's collapse argument) or a distinct mechanism that neutralizes the death-spiral objection while still limiting who bears the premium (PRO's map/target distinction). PRO's account tracks the motion's actual text — it targets *charging premiums* based on risk, not the existence of risk analysis — and matches a real, familiar design pattern (community rating plus equalization funds) rather than an invented statistic, so it counts as legitimate background knowledge rather than fake precision. CON's rebuttal that equalization "is" actuarial sorting is clever but conflates using risk data to determine transfers between insurers with using it to charge individuals more, and PRO's map/target metaphor squarely answered that conflation and was never fully dismantled — CON's closing repeated "the rescue is the targeting" without showing why moving money toward high-risk people is morally or mechanically identical to moving prices against them. On the democracy/transparency sub-argument, both sides scored points, but PRO's inversion (calling status-quo risk pricing itself a hidden allocation choice) at least matched CON's "hidden cross-subsidy" charge, keeping that exchange a wash rather than a CON advantage. PRO also handled the pressure questions cleanly with direct A1/A2 answers and used the "retreat" framing effectively to control the flow in the final rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's map-vs-target reframing in Rebuttal 2/Closing, directly answering CON's "equalization is actuarial fairness" collapse argument by distinguishing the direction risk data is used (toward the vulnerable vs. against them), which CON's final speech never fully overcame.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins because it showed that the proposition explicitly targets charging individuals different premiums, not all actuarial analysis, so risk-equalization and community rating are compatible with the resolution. CON’s strongest objection—that equalization requires actuarial sorting—misreads the scope of the motion, and PRO successfully crystallized that the same data can guide rescue (equalization) rather than exclusion (differential pricing). CON’s death-spiral and transparency arguments were well-developed but were each answered by PRO’s durable-design frame and the parity of democratic enactment. The closing exchanges left PRO’s moral line—limiting unchosen-misfortune pricing for essential coverage—standing while CON’s economic critique was shown to rely on a scope expansion the text does not support.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The actuarial-fairness-vs-equalization exchange in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 was decisive. CON argued that equalization secretly requires the actuarial apparatus the proposition condemns, but PRO replied that the motion limits patient-facing premium pricing, not regulatory risk analysis, and that regulating plan design can plug remaining gaps. This rebuttal defanged CON’s central contradiction and held through the closing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): PRO kept the debate inside the proposition's own definition — limiting charging different premiums based on risk, not banning risk analysis — so equalization paying insurers is consistent, not contradictory.
