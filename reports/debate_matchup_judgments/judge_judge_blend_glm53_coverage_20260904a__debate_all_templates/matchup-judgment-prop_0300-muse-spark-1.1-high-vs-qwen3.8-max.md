### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON built a consistent causal mechanism across the whole debate: formal apology functions as a legal admission that waives defenses, tolls prescription, and creates estoppel, shifting quantum-setting from voters to courts. CON also reframed PRO's own examples (Germany, Japanese American internment) to argue that apology worked there because compensation frameworks were legislated first and apology ratified rather than triggered them. PRO's opening and rebuttals asserted that apology could be "precise" and that legislatures could cap or phase remedies afterward, but in Rebuttal 2 PRO conceded (A1) that a meaningful apology "may make claims harder to deny and may remove procedural defenses," while still insisting caps and phased frameworks remain available. CON exploited this concession decisively, arguing that once liability is legally confessed, no legislated cap can bind a court that has already been handed an admission against interest — and PRO's closing never directly answered this mechanical problem, instead retreating to moral framing ("truth only when it cannot help claimants"). Both sides asked sharp, well-targeted pressure questions and answered in proper A1/A2 format, but CON's answers held together as a coherent, if costly, bullet-biting position (accepting that formal apology may be permanently withheld while still allowing regret/memorials/archives), whereas PRO's answers papered over the caps-after-confession problem rather than resolving it. That unresolved mechanism is the load-bearing crux of the debate, and CON controlled it from open to close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3–A4.5), which seized on PRO's concession in B4.1 that apology strengthens claims and removes defenses, then showed that PRO's promised legislative caps and phased settlements cannot legally survive a prior formal admission of liability — a point PRO's closing never rebutted.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): A won by making the decisive mechanism clearer: a formal apology is an admission against interest that strips defenses and tends to move quantum into court, so being "more willing" against counsel pre-empts the democratic frameworks B promised. B kept a live legitimacy path and pressed perpetual-non-apology pressure, but never neutralized the post-confession control problem after conceding apology removes procedural defenses. A's sequencing alternative (truth via regret/archives/memorials, apology only to ratify a capped deal) survived as the cleaner vote path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 (A4.3-A4.4): used B's B4.1 concession that apology tolls defenses to collapse B's "legislate caps afterward" remedy story.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON won by showing that a formal apology is a legally consequential admission that strips procedural defenses, tolls limitations, and shifts remedial control from democratic legislatures to courts. CON’s alternative—archives, memorials, and negotiated legislative redress before ratification—preserved moral honesty without the litigation trap PRO could not escape.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal, where they answered PRO’s pressure questions by distinguishing sympathy from legal admission, then leveraged PRO’s concession in [B4.1] that apology strips defenses to collapse PRO’s promise that remedy remains politically controllable, exposing the incoherence of confessing first and capping second.
