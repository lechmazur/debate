### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won a competitive debate by distinguishing financial pressure in general from fines imposed on the public-facing operator. Its case that fines may be absorbable for wealthy platforms yet destructive for constrained clinics was coherent rather than contradictory. Side A established the need for external enforcement, but its concession that it would fine a hospital unable to alter its vendor's code strengthened B's surcharge-on-incapacity objection. B also sustained the metric mismatch between checklist compliance and successful task completion. Side A's clipped opening was a minor additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's decisive response was that withholding vendor payment targets the party controlling the code and purchases a fix, whereas fining the clinic targets the site's operator after exclusion has occurred. Side A never fully overcame that targeting distinction.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that the motion as written misfires on three live fronts: targeting, metrics, and repeated failure. CON established that fining the public-facing service operator often punishes an agency that cannot rewrite the vendor code, while the vendor escapes untouched, and that "basic accessibility standards" incentivize cheap checklist patches rather than fixing the functional task. PRO's fire-code analogy and "twenty years of voluntary failure" frame were strong, but CON successfully rebutted the contradiction charge in Rebuttal 1 and crystallized in Rebuttal 2 and Closing that a post-hoc fine "purchases a receipt" while upstream procurement conditions "purchase a fix." PRO never fully overcame the incapacity objection: fining a locked-in hospital does not turn it into a procurement tiger, it reduces the budget for the next hire or helpline. PRO's closing metaphor that "teeth on the wrong jaw still chew" was rhetorically sharp but analytically unpersuasive against CON's sustained demonstration that the defendant named in the motion is not the party that controls the barrier, and that the metric named in the motion does not measure the veteran actually completing the booking.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's pressure questions (Turn 8), where CON distinguished withholding payment to the vendor—keeping cash with the buyer to force a code fix—from a post-hoc fine on the service operator, summarizing the difference as "One instrument purchases a fix. The other purchases a receipt." This sharpened CON's core theory that the motion misdirects cost and made the comparative mechanism intuitively compelling.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively proving that the motion's mechanism is misdirected. A establishes that public-facing services often lack control over their code (relying on third-party vendors or legacy systems), meaning fines merely drain their budgets without fixing the underlying barriers. A's alternative of withholding payment directly targets the vendors who write the code, exposing B's false equivalence between the two mechanisms. Furthermore, A effectively highlights that fining based on "basic standards" incentivizes cheap, superficial patches rather than actual task completion. B's argument that fines will force agencies to negotiate better contracts fails to address the reality of locked-in, under-resourced agencies that lack procurement leverage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantle B's claim that "withholding payment" is just a private fine. A clarifies that withholding payment targets the vendor who controls the code before the trap is deployed, whereas the motion's fine penalizes the incapacitated service after the fact, culminating in the decisive closing line: "A penalty the vendor never pays cannot open a vendor's hand."

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by showing that repeated, basic failures after notice require a predictable cost that makes repair cheaper than delay. Side B raised credible concerns about small providers, compliance theater, and platform exit, but its alternatives shared the same detection and administrative burdens while offering weaker deterrence or risking the shutdown it criticized. It also treated fines and remediation orders as mutually exclusive even though the proposition did not.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A most decisively answered the alternative-enforcement case by contrasting fines with lawsuits, procurement, scorecards, and injunctions: lawsuits are sporadic, procurement is limited, scorecards lack consequences, and channel withdrawal recreates the feared exit. This left fines as the clearest mechanism for pricing continued delay while keeping services open.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing a structural flaw in Side B's mechanism: revenue-scaled fines give platforms an escape hatch—dropping small customers—while leaving disabled users with no service at all. Side B never squarely answered why platforms would keep risky accounts rather than cut them, and their attempt to equate dropping customers with injunctive shutdowns collapsed under Side A's distinction between wallet-based penalties and pathway-based remedies. Side A's case grew stronger across rounds, turning Side B's own admissions about scaling bureaucracy against them and crystallizing the clash in a memorable, comparative frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing distinction between fines (which price the lock but make exit the cheapest path) and injunctive remediation orders (which target the broken pathway directly), leaving Side B with no effective response to the platform-drop incentive.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a highly persuasive, rhetorically sharp case that financial penalties are necessary to change the incentive structure of digital accessibility. PRO effectively neutralized CON's "small clinic" and "compliance theater" arguments by narrowing the scope to basic, binary failures and proposing revenue-scaled penalties. While CON introduced a strong alternative in injunctive relief, PRO successfully countered that CON's alternative also risks service shutdowns, whereas fines keep the service running while pricing the delay. PRO's consistent, memorable framing ("pricing the lock") and crisp rebuttals secured a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to CON's claim that fines would cause platforms to drop small clinics. PRO pointed out that if a platform would rather drop a clinic than fix a known lock, that is exactly why the lock cannot remain free, and later noted that CON's preferred alternative (yanking authorization) results in the exact same exit/shutdown CON claimed to fear.
