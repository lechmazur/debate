### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B won narrowly by making reach and uptake the decisive comparative burden. A persuasively showed that programs provide more direct connection, supervision, and protection for participants, especially during risky after-school hours. But B repeatedly demonstrated that public funding does not itself make anxious or deeply isolated youth enter opt-in spaces, while classroom literacy reaches them where they already are. A’s low-barrier-program answer reduced but did not resolve that problem. B’s clipped turn was an execution cost, and its claims about literacy’s effectiveness were sometimes overstated, so A’s violence route remained competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that “funding removes the price tag; it does not remove the tryout” most effectively damaged A’s case. A later proposed drop-in spaces, but B correctly noted that even these still depend on the isolated child choosing to attend.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the decisive clash on population-level strength and the opt-in exclusion of the loneliest kids live through every round. A had a strong root-cause/physical-safety story and scored on after-school violence and “cure vs. diagnosis,” but never supplied a convincing mechanism that converts funded programs into a tool for the bathroom-stall teenager; drop-in reframes still require showing up. B’s reach-plus-terrain comparison therefore remained the cleaner reason to reject the proposition, despite a clipped Rebuttal 2.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s repeated demolition of funding-as-barrier-removal (B2.1/B4.4/B5): money buys a nicer gym, not the courage to enter it, so PRO’s benefits flow only to kids already inclined to join.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A built a highly persuasive and logically airtight case around the "opt-in" nature of youth programs, effectively demonstrating that they structurally exclude the most isolated youth (the "bathroom-stall kid"). Side B struggled to overcome this selection bias, and their pivot to "low-barrier" drop-in spaces failed to address the fundamental requirement of social courage to leave the house. Side A's rhetoric was sharp, memorable, and consistently targeted the proposition's core weaknesses, making the comparative weighing heavily favor universal reach over transformative-but-absent interventions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's relentless pressing of the "opt-in" problem, culminating in the "bathroom-stall kid" metaphor, which Side B could not effectively neutralize even when pivoting to low-barrier drop-in spaces.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by offering the more direct and plausible causal chain: recurring activities create belonging, supervision, alternative status, and occupied time, whereas literacy primarily supplies knowledge. Side B’s universal peer-culture argument was strong, but it relied heavily on unsupported claims that social media is the central cause and that brief curricula can transform the whole digital ecosystem. A also exposed B’s double standard about clubs failing against a 24/7 feed while literacy lessons supposedly overcome that same problem. A’s clipped opening and assumption of school-embedded access were execution costs, but not enough to reverse the result.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s pressure question and second rebuttal most decisively challenged B’s mechanism: if engineered platforms overpower real teammates and coaches, B had not adequately shown why a few classroom hours would outperform them. B’s collective-norms answer preserved competitiveness, but remained more asserted than demonstrated.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): A won by making the decisive comparison stick: loneliness/violence as a belonging gap rather than an information gap, plus a live double-standard attack B never fully neutralized. A forced B to hold clubs to 24/7 digital perfection while treating a few literacy hours as sufficient against the same pocket-slot-machine problem, the same blind adults, and the same psychological opt-in. B’s peer-ecosystem and mandatory-reach case stayed competitive, but A’s adult-invitation mechanism and “what changes what the teen does next” weighing gave the cleaner reason to prefer structural programs as the stronger tool.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 answers plus close: A collapsed B’s 2 AM/encrypted-feed standard by noting literacy teachers are equally blind, then reframed belonging as what changes the response to provocation rather than knowledge alone.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by successfully framing the crisis as a "belonging gap" rather than an "information gap," which effectively neutralized Side A's (CON) case for literacy. Side B's rebuttals were exceptionally sharp, particularly in exposing the double standard in Side A's demands regarding 2 AM supervision and psychological opt-in. While Side A made a strong late pivot to "changing the peer ecosystem," Side B's core argument that manipulation exploits emptiness, not ignorance, remained the more compelling and grounded resolution to the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side A's "mandatory reach" and "2 AM supervision" arguments by pointing out that literacy teachers are equally blind to encrypted feeds and that psychological opt-in applies to classrooms just as much as clubs. Side B's conclusion that "manipulation works on the empty, not the ignorant" perfectly crystallized the debate.
