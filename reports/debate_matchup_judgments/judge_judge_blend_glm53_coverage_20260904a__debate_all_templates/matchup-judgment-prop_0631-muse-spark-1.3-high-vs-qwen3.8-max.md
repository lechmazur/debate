### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued cleanly and stayed on-motion, but CON's central objection—that conditioning protection on documentation punishes genuine authorship whenever records are lost or informal, turning copyright into a records-management test—was never fully neutralized. PRO's attempt to soften "document" into "any credible evidence" (A1 in Rebuttal 2) actually blunted PRO's own distinctiveness, since CON then showed this collapses into the ordinary evidentiary process courts already use, making the special rule redundant paperwork rather than a needed fix. PRO's closing tried to leverage CON's concession that raw mechanical output has no default owner as a "collapse" of CON's case, but CON's final speech directly rebutted this, correctly noting that this concession simply restates existing originality doctrine rather than validating PRO's documentation gate. CON also landed a sharp reversal of PRO's "landlord" framing: denying copyright to under-documented human-assisted works doesn't disempower platforms (who retain contract/trade-secret control) but does strip vulnerable individual creators of the one right that reaches strangers, undercutting PRO's own protective rationale. PRO's land-grab and prompt-farm concerns were vivid and real, but CON's answer—that existing filtering and evidentiary doctrine can address mechanical outputs without a blanket documentation precondition—was never squarely defeated. CON's execution in Q&A and final rebuttal was slightly sharper and better resolved the live tension than PRO's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 point that real creative control can be lost to a crashed drive or deleted platform history, making protection contingent on recordkeeping rather than authorship—paired with CON's final closing rebuttal showing PRO's "no default owner" concession restates existing law rather than proving PRO's case—was the most decisive exchange, since PRO's later softening of "document" into "any credible evidence" undercut its own rule's distinctiveness.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): CON won because the decisive clash was whether the proposition’s distinctive “document substantial creative control” gate is needed beyond ordinary human-authorship evidence. PRO’s enclosure/prompt-farm default argument was strong, but later speeches softened “document” into ordinary proof (layers, testimony, before-and-after), which ceded ground to CON’s rule while leaving the motion’s formality unrepaired. CON consistently showed lost logs, archival habits, and a documentation race would disarm real revisers and favor automated pipelines, and that raw mechanical output can already get no copyright without a tool-specific paperwork precondition. PRO’s closing cleverly used CON’s no-default-owner answer, but CON correctly reframed it as existing originality rather than a concession to the proposition’s audit rule. Net: cleaner comparative path for B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 lost-records and formality turn: real creative control can still fail under PRO if traces disappear, so the motion is a paperwork gate, not mere authorship; PRO’s “any credible evidence” reply never fully escaped that.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON wins because PRO's documentation requirement, while rhetorically appealing, functions as a tool-specific formality that makes copyright contingent on record-keeping rather than actual authorship. CON's core objection—that real creative control can be defeated by a crashed drive, deleted platform history, or fast creative iteration—survives the exchange largely intact. PRO never fully relieves this pressure; reframing "document" as "any credible evidence" still conditions protection on producible traces and does not explain why independent creators who lack archival habits should lose rights. CON also successfully shows that ordinary originality doctrine already denies protection to purely mechanical outputs, making PRO's special rule redundant for easy cases and harmful for borderline ones. PRO's scale and prompt-farm concerns are live but do not overcome the demonstrated costs of converting litigation evidence into a mandatory pre-filing gate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained attack on the documentation requirement as a paperwork formality that creates a two-tier system—penalizing AI-assisted artists for lost prompt threads or drive failures while painters and photographers face no similar archival burden—which PRO never fully defused.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON (A) built the more damaging rebuttal chain. Its camera-history analogy directly answered PRO's authorship claim by showing copyright has always protected human-directed but machine-mediated output without formal documentation. More decisively, A's "archiver, not author" catch exposed that PRO's own examples of qualifying documentation (prompt histories, selection reasoning) reduce to ordinary proof of control that courts could assess via testimony as with photographs — undermining the motion's specific demand for formal "documentation" as opposed to ordinary case-by-case originality analysis. A also repeatedly pressed the honest-discloser-vs-liar asymmetry (candid AI users face an audit; liars who claim full authorship sail through since judges can't forensically detect AI), and PRO's answer — that discovery, metadata, and fraud defenses catch liars regardless — never resolved why documentation is then necessary at all, nor why it doesn't specifically penalize candor. PRO's authorship framing and public-domain concerns remained coherent but its answers to the two sharpest attacks (the contradiction and the asymmetry) were assertions rather than resolutions, leaving CON's live routes to victory largely intact through closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal 2 point that PRO's own concession ("one brilliant prompt plus careful selection can count") shows the standard turns on demonstrable control, not volume of logs — meaning the documentation mandate "rewards the archiver, not the author" — was the most decisive unanswered blow, compounding the earlier unresolved honest-discloser-vs-liar asymmetry A raised via Q2 and repeated in every subsequent speech.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the decisive clash on human authorship of expression when a model supplies concrete form, and by making the documentation rule conditional and workable rather than a general formality. B landed strong practical hits on honesty-as-liability, indie free-riding, and archive-vs-vision inequality, but never cleanly solved how courts deny raw or barely prompted machine outputs without some control inquiry. A answered B's pressure more directly (specific evidence of control; one visionary prompt plus selection can pass) and better contained the camera analogy by showing why generative choice-supply differs from framing/moment. The path to vote A is clearer: protect only documented substantial human control; leave undirected generation free.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2: specifies what counts as control proof, admits one brilliant prompt + selection can qualify, and flips the fraud comparison against CON's automatic-vesting presumption.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON showed that requiring process documentation singles out a lawful tool, reintroduces formalities foreign to American copyright, and creates a perverse incentive where honest disclosure invites costly audit while lying preserves automatic protection. PRO’s theoretical case for human authorship remained coherent, but CON’s critique that paperwork measures archiving rather than art—bolstered by the photography precedent and the “filing cabinet” frame—proved more decisive across the exchange, especially since PRO never squarely resolved why an indie creator who lacks litigation-ready logs should lose protection to a larger rival that can copy verbatim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s sustained attack in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 that a documentation test rewards record-keeping over creativity, structurally punishes candor, and cannot stop trolls from auto-generating fake revision histories, which PRO answered only with circular appeals to post-hoc fraud detection.
