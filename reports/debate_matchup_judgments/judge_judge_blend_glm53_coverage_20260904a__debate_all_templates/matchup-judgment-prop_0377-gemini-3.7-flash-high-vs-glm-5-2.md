### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued crisply and followed the pressure-question format correctly, but PRO maintained the more coherent throughline: active/earned labor income is bounded and merit-tied, while inherited capital is passive, unbounded, and detached from the heir's own effort. CON's most damaging moves were the "same money, different timestamp" incoherence charge and the carve-out/avoidance critique, but PRO's "merit is personal, not transferable" answer defused the incoherence charge without contradiction (earner's income can be legitimate while the heir's windfall is still unearned), leaving that dilemma more resolved than CON's closing implied. CON's carve-out pressure landed and exposed PRO's gestural "solvable" answer as a real weak point, but this is a practical-implementation critique rather than a rebuttal of the core normative comparison the motion asks about. CON's parallel to education/networks was clever but PRO's active-labor-versus-no-labor distinction answered it reasonably well, and CON's closing didn't engage that specific rebuttal, mostly reasserting the "scale" framing. On balance PRO's case survived scrutiny slightly better and gave a cleaner reason to accept the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over A1.4/A4.3's "same money, different timestamp" incoherence charge versus B2.3/B4.5's "merit is personal, not transferable" response was the pivotal clash; PRO's answer plausibly dissolved the dilemma CON built its case around, while CON's parallel carve-out pressure (A3.2) exposed a genuine gap in PRO's practical case (B4.2's gestural "solvable" answer).
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a persistent dilemma in PRO's case: if merit dies with the earner and descendants have no claim to parental success, then every intergenerational transfer—education, networks, neighborhood advantage—is equally suspect, making inheritance tax an underinclusive band-aid; if the original earnings were legitimate, families have a coherent claim to deploy them across generations. PRO never supplied a principled reason that isolated bequeathed cash from these other channels, retreating instead to scale comparisons that collapsed the promised carve-outs. CON also landed the structural objection that a one-time death levy cannot counter the perpetual compounding PRO identified as the core threat, and crystallized these unresolvable tensions crisply across rebuttals and closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's "same money, different timestamp" dilemma and the underinclusiveness charge—that PRO's merit-based logic against inheritance must also condemn elite education and professional networks unless arbitrary distinctions are drawn—remained unanswered through the final speech and decisively weakened PRO's principled foundation.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): PRO wins because its core distinction survives: annual high income is bounded by active labor and lifespan while large inherited capital compounds passively across generations, granting structural immunity. CON's best pushes—family-business collateral, same-money-different-timestamp, and principle-isolation—are met by PRO's threshold/carve-out answer and its human-capital vs passive-capital principle (education requires effort, passive fortunes do not), plus the reset-at-transfer mechanism that directly counters perpetual compounding. CON never establishes that high earners' yearly income itself is a larger threat to opportunity, leaving PRO's comparative live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's principle-isolation attack [B2.1][B2.5][B4.5] that if merit dies with the earner every parental advantage is suspect vs A's reply [A4.3-A4.4] that schooling develops human capital requiring work while dynastic financial capital grants structural command without labor - the central clash that PRO ultimately carries.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Both sides opened with clear, plausible causal stories, but PRO adapted better across rounds. PRO's rebuttal directly neutralized CON's "dynastic wealth dissipates" claim with the modern family-office/trust counterpoint, offered concrete policy fixes (exemptions, installment payments) for the liquidation worry, and cleverly flipped CON's avoidance argument into evidence of dynastic privilege rather than a reason to abstain from taxing it. PRO's pressure question exposed a real weakness in CON's chronology argument (that inheritances only matter at probate), and CON's answer to that question (A4.1) was evasive, essentially redefining lifetime trusts/gifts as unrelated "living cash flow" without engaging the substance. CON's later rebuttals largely re-asserted the "active income is the real driver" claim without re-engaging PRO's family-office rebuttal to fragility, making CON's case feel repetitive by the end. PRO's closing sharpened the decisive asymmetry (identical harm mechanism, but only one channel already taxed) and left it substantially unanswered, while CON's closing mostly restated earlier claims without directly confronting that asymmetry or the compounding-across-generations point. This gives PRO the cleaner, more adaptive path through the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to CON's "dynastic wealth naturally dissipates" claim (B2.2), pointing to family offices and professional wealth management as a modern counter-mechanism, combined with PRO's pressure question exposing the weakness in CON's chronology argument and CON's evasive answer (A4.1), was the most decisive exchange.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins because it gave the cleaner, more durable comparative frame. After conceding that both high earners and heirs purchase identical positional goods, PRO argued that annual income is already heavily checked by progressive taxation, leaving large intergenerational transfers as the structurally unfinished threat. This reframing turned CON’s own chronology argument against it by showing that dynastic advantage moves through trusts and gifts long before probate, and it exposed a persistent imbalance that CON never squarely answered. CON’s active-income and liquidation objections remained plausible but were increasingly outweighed by PRO’s integrated tax-design logic and its effective demolition of the “natural dilution” claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s concession that both channels inflict identical harm, followed by the argument that earned income is already aggressively taxed while inherited wealth faces no comparable check—thereby making dynastic transfer the bigger remaining structural threat—materially collapsed CON’s comparative advantage and shifted the debate to an unanswered policy imbalance.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won the weighing: after both sides agreed heirs and high earners buy the same positional goods, PRO showed annual income is already aggressively taxed while large transfers at death are not — the unfinished work. PRO neutralized CON's two best routes: dilution (family offices/trusts) and liquidation (exemptions/installments), and turned the chronology attack by noting dynastic wealth funds advantage throughout life via trusts/gifts, which an aggressive transfer-tax regime targets. CON conceded identical harm and never closed the tax-coverage gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's turn of B's chronology claim [B2.1] in A3.1/A4.5 — arguing wealthy families don't wait for probate and that inheritance taxation targets the trust/gift infrastructure that funds early-life advantage — which left B's scale/timing case without a response to the residual untaxed channel.
