  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 successfully dismantled Side A's "equivalent packages" hypothetical by showing that a visible tax concession naturally leaves the operator responsible for overruns and marginal consumption, whereas public infrastructure funding socializes those risks.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by showing that PRO's cap regulates accounting labels, not real harms. Because PRO conceded that a developer could fully pay attributable grid and water costs while receiving a matching larger tax abatement, the cap cannot prevent cost-shifting; it merely forces otherwise equivalent deals into a preferred financing form. CON's alternative—directly protecting residents through binding utility tariffs, connection contracts, and clawbacks—more coherently tracks the actual burden at every incentive level.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's equivalent-packages critique, sharpened by the pressure question about enlarged abatements and completed in Rebuttal 2, showed that PRO's condition tracks accounting form rather than substance, leaving the cap with no independent protective effect.

PRO's attempt to distinguish the two packages based on risk allocation and marginal conservation incentives was neutralized when A clarified that a fixed, bounded infrastructure grant could preserve those exact same marginal incentives. A's analytical rigor, tight framing, and direct answers to pressure questions made its case decisively stronger.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully separating the shared goal of ratepayer protection from the proposition's specific mechanism. By exposing the mismatch between the size of the uninternalized cost and the categorical nature of the cap, Side A proved the rule is both arbitrary and redundant to direct utility regulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal and closing effectively capitalized on Side B's concession regarding the cap's level, demonstrating that the rule could allow massive cost-shifting below the cap while blocking beneficial incentives over a trivial residual cost.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by demonstrating that the proposed tax-incentive cap is structurally mismatched to the harm it targets. B showed the cap is set by fiscal capacity rather than the magnitude of uninternalized infrastructure costs, making it simultaneously permissive for large cost-shifts below the cap and restrictive for trivial residuals above it. A never repaired this proportionality break: if enforceable utility contracts already protect residents, the cap is redundant; if they do not, the cap fails to stop the cost-shift. B’s alternative—direct cost allocation plus transparent net-public-value assessment—directly targets the liability without this arbitrary fiscal penalty, and A’s defense that the cap is merely a complementary safeguard conceded the mismatch rather than resolving it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s sustained critique that the cap is “symbolism, not exposure control”—either redundant if utility contracts already internalize costs, or arbitrary and disproportionate if they do not, because a huge socialized upgrade could remain eligible below the cap while a tiny disputed residual blocks a deal above it.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON decisively proved that the proposition's mechanism—a tax incentive cap triggered by a binary "full internalization" threshold—is logically mismatched to the harm of cost-shifting. By forcing PRO to admit in the pressure round that the cap is based on general fiscal capacity rather than the magnitude of the uninternalized cost, CON demonstrated that the rule arbitrarily blocks valuable incentives over trivial residual costs while permitting massive cost-shifting just below the cap. CON's advocacy for direct utility contracts and cost-reflective tariffs was a much cleaner, proportionate solution that directly targeted the actual harm without creating an arbitrary fiscal veto.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's exploitation of PRO's admission that the cap is a general fiscal ceiling, proving the tax cap does not proportionately track the uninternalized infrastructure costs and allows massive cost-shifting below the threshold.
