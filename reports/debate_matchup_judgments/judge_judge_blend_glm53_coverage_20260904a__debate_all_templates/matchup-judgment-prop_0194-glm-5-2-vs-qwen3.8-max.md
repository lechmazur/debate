### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly within the rules, but CON built the more damaging pressure sequence. CON's pressure question asked whether the veto privileges delay, since inaction can also lock in irreversible harm. PRO's answer—that the commissioner can also veto deliberate deferral—was meant to neutralize that charge, but it instead reopened the exact vulnerability CON had identified earlier: if the commissioner can block both a coastal barrier (habitat harm) and the failure to build it (flood harm), the office is no longer a narrow "threshold checker" as PRO insisted, but is functionally choosing which irreversible harm society must bear. CON caught this contradiction cleanly in Rebuttal 2 ("the office is not checking a narrow line; it is deciding which irreversible harm to accept") and crystallized it decisively in closing. PRO's closing reasserted the threshold framing without explaining how a single statutory bar distinguishes between two competing irreversible-harm framings, leaving the core "guardrail vs. planner" tension unresolved. PRO's strongest point—that existing advisory/statutory tools have already failed for decades on climate—was real and never fully answered, but CON's corrigibility argument (accountable, revisable institutions vs. an unappealable veto based on contestable forecasts) gave a coherent reason why that failure doesn't justify an irreversible fix. On balance, CON's rebuttal chain did more damage to PRO's central mechanism than PRO's rebuttals did to CON's alternatives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A2 answer) catching that PRO's extension of the veto to cover "deliberate deferral" transforms the commissioner from a neutral threshold-checker into an actor choosing which irreversible harm to accept—directly undercutting PRO's core "guardrail not planner" defense, a contradiction PRO never resolved in closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side A won by brilliantly exploiting a contradiction in Side B's case regarding how the veto operates in practice. When Side B argued that the commissioner could veto both harmful action and harmful inaction (deferral), Side A accurately pointed out that this transforms the commissioner from a narrow negative check into an active planner choosing between competing harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (A4.3-A4.4), which weaponized B's claim about vetoing deferral to prove the commissioner would end up making irreducible political trade-offs between competing harms.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): CON exposed a structural contradiction in PRO's mechanism: the veto either privileges delay or expands to let an unelected commissioner choose between competing irreversible harms, violating PRO's own "brake not steering wheel" framing. PRO's diagnosis of democratic short-termism was forceful, but CON showed that a binding veto sacrifices corrigibility without proving it would escape the same political pressures that make existing safeguards fail. The safer path is revisable democratic discipline, not unaccountable finality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4.3–A4.4) demonstrating that PRO's deferral answer turned the commissioner into a planner choosing between irreversible harms, directly contradicting the claim that the office only checks a narrow statutory threshold.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases without fake precision, and the debate stayed tightly on the motion. PRO's strongest asset was the irreversibility asymmetry (caution is correctable, ecological/physical harm is not) plus a plausible bounded-veto/trusteeship framing. But CON's pressure questions were the sharper tool: the "either the override is a super-legislature or it's toothless" dilemma forced PRO to commit to a specific mechanism (supermajority plus two successive legislatures), and CON's follow-up rebuttal turned that concrete answer against PRO, showing it amounts to a multi-year, unelected freeze on national policy — effectively conceding the "authority above democracy" charge CON had been pressing. PRO's own pressure questions were comparatively soft, mostly restating PRO's thesis rather than probing a genuine weakness in CON's position, so CON faced less exposure in that exchange. CON also successfully symmetrized PRO's central asymmetry claim by arguing that blocked infrastructure/adaptation is its own form of irreversible harm and that legislatures at least face electoral correction while commissioners do not — a point PRO's closing did not squarely re-engage, instead restating its opening asymmetry claim without addressing the override-freeze problem CON had just exposed. That unanswered pivot in the late rounds is the deciding factor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's own answer to the override pressure question ("two successive legislatures," supermajority) into evidence that the veto functions as unelected multi-year lawmaking power — a point PRO never fully neutralized in its closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A won by successfully dismantling Side B's key institutional analogies and undermining the core asymmetry of 'delay vs. destruction'. Side A repeatedly pointed out that unlike courts or central banks, the commissioner would lack a legislative text or mandate to interpret, making their power largely unanchored. Side B never fully resolved this structural critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively neutralized Side B's comparison to independent institutions by highlighting that courts and central banks operate under amendable mandates, whereas the commissioner would hold unanchored veto power over the legislature itself.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON wins because it exposed three persistent, unresolved weaknesses in PRO's model. First, the "preconditions versus preferences" challenge showed that the commissioner cannot neutrally identify future interests; the very examples PRO invoked (energy, infrastructure, fiscal choices) are live political disputes, so the commissioner merely picks sides with a veto. Second, CON demonstrated that demanding override mechanisms effectively turn an appointee into a gatekeeper with years-long lawmaking power, undermining the claim that this is only a "check." Third, CON successfully reversed PRO's irreversibility asymmetry: cautionary delay blocks adaptive infrastructure and compounds poverty across generations, making inaction itself a permanent harm. PRO's analogies to courts and central banks were cleanly distinguished by CON's textual-anchor argument, and PRO never recovered a clean way to show the veto is bounded, accountable, and produces fewer total errors than the status quo.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's argument that "preconditions versus preferences" is itself an irreducibly contested political judgment, showing that the commissioner cannot claim neutral trusteeship and instead empowers one faction's present-day guesswork with a binding veto. This rebuttal undermined PRO's epistemic foundation and remained unanswered through the final speech.
