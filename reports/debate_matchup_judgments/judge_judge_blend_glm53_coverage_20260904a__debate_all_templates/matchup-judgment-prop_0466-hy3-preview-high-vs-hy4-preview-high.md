### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON built a tighter, more durable line: the asymmetry between a certain, immediate loss of income and a speculative, delayed spillover benefit, sharpened by the fact that the motion never mandates compensation ("PRO merely permits transition aid, never mandates it"). PRO's Q2 tried to neutralize this by noting aid "can" be paired, but CON's A2 correctly showed the motion's wording makes that optional, leaving the core asymmetry intact through the final round. CON also successfully flipped PRO's enforcement argument: dock-based catch/gear checks are administrable even for weak states, while a distant 30% no-take box requires offshore detection capacity (satellites, radar against transponder-off vessels) that cash-strapped states likely lack — a point PRO's rebuttal (satellite AIS "even for cash-strained states") asserted rather than truly answered, especially since PRO had just argued quotas fail due to weak at-sea observability, creating tension in PRO's own case. PRO's spillover mechanism and Newfoundland example were solid openers, but PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions on mobile-species leakage and unilateral enforcement remained hand-wavy ("networked action," "port-state checks"), never squarely closing the leakage gap CON raised. Both sides argued the actual motion without evasive reframing, but CON's case survived the exchange with fewer unaddressed vulnerabilities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's "one boundary is easier to patrol than every hold" claim around — showing dock/landing checks are more administrable for weak-governance states than offshore boundary patrol against transponder-off vessels — combined with CON's direct answer that the motion only permits, never mandates, transition aid, which PRO never fully resolved.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing two unresolved burdens in PRO's case. First, the motion mandates a 30% closure but does not mandate transition aid; CON showed that dependent communities therefore face a certain, immediate loss of income for a speculative future gain that may leak to foreign or industrial fleets. PRO repeatedly answered that nations "can" fund relief, but never escaped the fact that the proposition does not require it, making the hardship compulsory and the compensation optional. Second, PRO's central claim that no-take reserves are uniquely enforceable was turned by CON: weak states can police quotas and gear rules at the dock, but lack the real-time offshore capacity to patrol distant 30% boundaries against transponder-off industrial vessels, turning the reserve into a paper park that displaces small-scale fishers while cheaters fish the line. PRO's spillover mechanism remained plausible in the abstract, but CON materially damaged its certainty, timeliness, and local capture, especially for mobile species in unilateral action. Across the exchange, CON narrowed the clash to the enforcement reality and the asymmetry of certain costs versus uncertain benefits, and PRO never provided enough guaranteed mitigation to outweigh the harm to dependent communities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal distinguishing dock-based enforcement of quotas from impossible offshore patrol of a 30% boundary, directly answering PRO's pressure question and flipping PRO's claimed advantage—showing that no-take becomes a paper park displacing small-scale fishers while industrial vessels evade the line.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but PRO handled the pivotal clashes more directly. CON's strongest weapon was the "redundancy trap" (if paired catch limits work, the reserve is superfluous; if they fail, the reserve can't feed anyone), but PRO answered it with a substantive distinction—catch limits manage extraction of an existing stock ("interest") while reserves rebuild spawning biomass and habitat structure ("principal")—and CON never engaged that distinction on its merits, simply restating the dilemma in closing. PRO also answered the enforcement pressure question head-on (dock/market enforcement of gear rules vs. patrol-dependent reserve fencing) with a plausible, on-point mechanism, while CON's answer to PRO's percentage question ("I never accepted a flat percentage... under 10% has worked") was a soft dodge that left its "smarter alternative" underspecified and vulnerable to PRO's closing charge that no rival share was ever shown to outrun extraction. CON's "pick one" dilemma about offshore-inert vs. inshore-ruinous reserves was a sharp rhetorical jab, but PRO's mixed-network response (most area offshore, targeted inshore hotspots) blunted its force by showing the dichotomy was overstated. Both used the required A1/A2 format correctly, but PRO's answers did more real work resolving the live tension, giving it the cleaner, more decisive path through the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over whether paired catch limits make the 30% reserve redundant (CON's A2.2/A4.3 "pick one" trap vs. PRO's B4.3 stabilize-vs-rebuild distinction) was the decisive clash; PRO's answer survived largely unrebutted on its merits.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO won by showing that a 30% no-take target can be met through a designed network combining mostly deep-water EEZ area with selected inshore spawning hotspots, leaving daily nearshore fishing grounds largely open and directly answering CON's narrow-shelf and sudden-destitution objections. CON failed to name an alternative EEZ-wide percentage that would outrun extraction under PRO's pressure questioning, and its redundancy argument—that catch limits alone suffice—was undermined by PRO's principal-versus-interest distinction showing reserves rebuild the asset that limits merely ration. PRO's sharper comparative weighing and more direct engagement with live pressure left the stronger route to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's rebuttal to CON's "pick one" challenge (B4.3), demonstrating that 30% of EEZ area is not 30% of the fertile ribbon and that a network can combine offshore zones with targeted inshore hotspots without swallowing the whole nearshore zone (A4.1, A5.3). This defused CON's most threatening dependency objection and preserved PRO's live path to victory.

CON's strongest route – immediate destitution on narrow shelves [B1.1][B1.2] and redundancy of reserves if limits work [B3.2] – was directly mitigated by PRO's geography reframe that a 200-mile EEZ is mostly deep water so 30% can include offshore plus designed hotspot slices while leaving most daily ribbon open [A4.1], and by distinguishing reserves as principal (age structure, habitat, resilience) from limits as interest management [A4.3].
