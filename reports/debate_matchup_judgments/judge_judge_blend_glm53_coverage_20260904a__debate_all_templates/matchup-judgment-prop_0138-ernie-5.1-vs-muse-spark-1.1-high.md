### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON (A) built a tighter, more resilient case. Its coded-registry proposal directly neutralized PRO's central health argument by showing medical alerts don't require a name, and its "outsourcing" rebuttal turned PRO's own UK/Scandinavia evidence against it: if supply held only because clinics imported anonymously-collected foreign sperm, a universal rule (as the motion demands, "even if supply falls") removes that safety valve entirely. PRO never squarely answered this. Pressure Questions were decisive: CON's Q2 exposed a real contradiction in PRO's claim that the motion doesn't affect future donors, since the motion text explicitly bites "even if anonymity would reduce donation supply." PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer (A2) called this a "false dilemma" but did not actually reconcile it with the motion's wording — a live evasion CON exploited cleanly in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing. PRO's own pressure questions (adoption unsealing, import undermining supply claims) were reasonable but CON handled them directly and turned them into further support for its own position. PRO's closing largely restated points ("fear dressed as fact") without resolving the outsourcing/universalization problem or the future-donor contradiction, while CON's closing crystallized the clash sharply around the motion's own "even if" clause. Both sides avoided fabricated sourcing, but CON's mechanistic reasoning (persistent coded links, universalizability of the rule) was more rigorous and did more actual comparative work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's UK/Scandinavia "supply held up" evidence into proof of the opposite — that stability was achieved only via anonymous foreign imports, which a universal rule would eliminate — combined with PRO's unresolved dodge (in A2) of the motion's explicit "even if supply falls" language regarding future donors.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): B built the cleaner path by forcing the motion's explicit tradeoff—identification even if fewer families form—and never let A escape it. B neutralized health claims with the coded-registry alternative, exposed A's prospective/retrospective inconsistency, and used the UK import adaptation as concrete evidence of supply harm rather than resilience. A repeated autonomy and identity appeals effectively early but weakened under pressure by redefining the motion and underplaying real-world registry durability and non-response costs. B's comparative weighing made the decision easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1 and R2 dismantling of A's UK-stability claim (imports as outsourcing anonymity) and the motion-contradiction on future donors.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A decisively won the core clashes. On the medical necessity of a name, Side A's "Donor 1487" coded registry mechanism was far more plausible than Side B's claim that a surname allows independent medical searching, especially since Side A highlighted the "second rejection" reality of open-identity contact. On the supply argument, Side A brilliantly exposed that the UK's stable numbers were an illusion created by importing anonymous foreign sperm—a loophole that would close if the proposition were universally applied. Side B failed to overcome this outsourcing critique. Side A's rhetoric was also sharper and more memorable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the UK supply argument by pointing out that the UK merely outsourced its anonymity reliance to foreign sperm banks, meaning universal application of the rule would inevitably crash the global supply.

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built the more cohesive case: it directly rebutted CON's contract/reliance framing with a clean principle (a contract cannot waive a third party's core personal rights), offered a concrete alternative model (identity-release donation) that addressed the supply objection without conceding the rights point, and used the pressure-question exchange to trap CON. CON's answer to B's Q2 (A4.3) conceded that even under a prospective-only regime it would still oppose the right, which PRO exploited devastatingly in closing to show CON's real objection is defending secrecy, not protecting reliance. CON's opening house-sale analogy for third-party contract effects was weak and never adequately repaired, and CON's final rebuttal speech was clipped for exceeding the word limit, costing it closing firepower exactly when it needed to answer B's strongest turn.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4/B5's use of A's own pressure-question answer (A4.3, admitting CON would still oppose even a prospective-only right) to prove CON's real objection is protecting future secrecy rather than reliance interests — a self-inflicted concession PRO converted into the decisive closing point.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): A built the cleaner decisive path: third-party identity rights cannot be contracted away, so secrecy promises bind no one, supply dips are secondary (and adaptable via identity-release), and the system’s dependence on lifelong blackout is itself the harm. - **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B built a much stronger comparative case, effectively dismantling Side A's supply and reliance arguments. Side B's distinction between "waiting longer" (inconvenience) and "lifelong erasure" (identity) decisively won the weighing debate. Side A's rebuttal 2 was clipped, which hurt their final substantive push, but even before that, Side B's rhetorical and logical framing was superior.
