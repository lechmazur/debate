### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built vivid, competent cases, but CON (A) did more damage to the opponent's core burden: showing that PRO's proposed line between "banned personal quotes" and "allowed posted/scarcity pricing" is unworkable. PRO's own audit test ("hold scarcity fixed, swap the customer") was exposed by CON as unenforceable given that no two real transactions are identical, and CON's reductio that identical-treatment logic would outlaw the very discounts PRO claims to preserve (the cart-abandoner mercy discount) was never squarely answered—PRO's closing dismissed it as "camouflage" without rebuilding the line. CON also landed a genuine economic point (price discrimination funds both low and high prices; banning one erases the other) that PRO answered only with unsupported assertions about posted markdowns sufficing. PRO's pressure question about proxies profiling thin-history shoppers scored a real hit, but CON's answer, while imperfect, at least engaged directly, whereas PRO's rebuttal to CON's coherence attack mostly reasserted moral framing rather than repairing the enforceability gap. Both used the Q&A format correctly and neither invented fake evidence, but CON's cumulative pressure left PRO's central "clean, enforceable ban" claim more damaged than CON's "inclusion via personalization" claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that PRO's own "hold scarcity fixed, swap the customer" audit test would criminalize the very cart-abandoner mercy discounts PRO claims to preserve, since no two checkouts are truly identical—PRO's closing never resolved this beyond calling it "camouflage."
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by drawing and defending the decisive line between publicly available, condition-based price changes and opaque prices based on the individual. Side A's inclusion argument was plausible, but it repeatedly assumed that personalized discounts require personalized surcharges and that vulnerable consumers will reliably receive the discounts rather than be targeted for urgency or low search capacity. Side B showed that automatic public discounts and inventory markdowns can remain legal while avoiding an untestable personal markup.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal most effectively answered the boundary and enforcement pressure: identical posted conditions should produce the same offer, while customer-swapping audits can isolate prohibited personalization. Side A raised real practical complications but overstated them as making any enforceable distinction impossible.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A established a cleaner, enforceable rule—same offer for the same posted situation—and showed that inclusion does not require hidden personal surcharges. Side B’s economic cross-subsidy story was plausible but relied on assuming algorithmic benevolence and opacity that A successfully framed as unaccountable extraction. A answered pressure questions more directly, exposed the unjustified reliance on secret quotes, and crystallized the clash around whether commerce should tax the person or price the good.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2, which introduced the “hold scarcity fixed, swap the customer” test and sharply distinguished public cart-state triggers from private model guesses, directly neutralizing Side B’s line-drawing and inclusion arguments.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built vivid, competent cases, but the decisive exchange was over B's central selling point: that a ban is "narrow and workable," banning only hidden profile-surcharges while preserving coupons, student discounts, and loyalty programs. A pressed this repeatedly (PQ2, R2), and when B offered a concrete test in Rebuttal 2 ("hold cost/scarcity constant, vary the profile"), A immediately turned that test against B's own kept exception, showing loyalty programs run on purchase-history "profiles" and would fail B's own criterion. B's closing reasserted the open-category/hidden-profile distinction but never re-defended it against this specific collapse, and since A closed last, this blow went unanswered on the record. B's strongest surviving point — that shared data-broker algorithms let rivals "copy" rather than "check" each other, undermining A's maverick-undercutting story — was real and under-addressed by A, keeping the debate competitive rather than lopsided. But on the more heavily contested axis (can the ban be drawn coherently without gutting legitimate discounting), A landed the cleaner, unrebutted final hit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's move in Rebuttal 2/Closing turning B's own "hold scarcity constant, vary the profile" audit test against B's conceded exception for loyalty discounts (which use purchase history) was the most decisive exchange; B's closing did not specifically repair this hole.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): B drew the clearer line between open, claimable discounts and secret person-specific willingness scoring, then tied opacity to surveillance, vulnerability, and weakened comparison. A established a real cost in lost targeted discounts and challenged B’s competition story, but overstated that the ban would eliminate ordinary coupons and sidestepped the direct desperation-surcharge hypothetical. B’s fairness and administrability routes remained more intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it directly answered both pressure questions, offered a concrete audit test holding scarcity constant while varying the profile, and explained why walking away is weaker protection when individualized pricing is hidden. A’s response showed that some personalized discounts would also be prohibited, but not that B’s distinction from open discounts was incoherent.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by showing that the decisive harm of personalized algorithmic pricing is its secrecy: it destroys the public price information that makes comparison shopping and competition possible. Side B’s central defense—that consumers can compare by leaving and that competition punishes overreach—collapses if prices are individually hidden and follow the shopper across tabs and stores. A crystallized this as “no maverick can undercut what shoppers cannot see,” and the point remained live through the closing. B landed thoughtful workability pressure and the standard worry that bans remove helpful discounts, but A’s distinction between transparent, claimable offers and hidden profile surcharges held up well enough, and B never fully neutralized the market-corruption argument. A’s rhetoric was sharper and more memorable, while keeping the argumentative burden focused on the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s rebuttal that secret profile pricing eliminates the visible price tags required for comparison shopping, directly neutralizing Side B’s claim that competition and consumer exit are sufficient safeguards. A’s framing that “the price follows you” and that “no maverick can undercut what shoppers cannot see” became the decisive, unanswered route to victory.
