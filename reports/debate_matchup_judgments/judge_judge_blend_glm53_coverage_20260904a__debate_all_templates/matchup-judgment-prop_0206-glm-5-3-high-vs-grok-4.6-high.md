- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully framing the statutory age as an inflexible 'gate' based on an unequal mean, contrasted with taxes as a 'dial' that can be aimed progressively.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized Side A's 'cushion' defense by arguing that targeted relief admits the mean is the wrong ruler, yet keeps using it as the default gate that traps worn-out workers who lack a formal medical diagnosis.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins because it kept the inequality critique live and showed that PRO's indexing mechanism unavoidably burdens the workers who gained the least extra life expectancy. PRO's "empathy test"—that a care aide pays higher taxes for forty years versus working slightly longer—was turned by CON into a sharper distinction between taxes spread across a career and extra work months concentrated at age sixty-seven when bodies fail and jobs vanish. CON's framing of the retirement age as a "gate" versus tax rates as a "tiltable dial" gave the cleaner comparative lens, and PRO never fully neutralized the problem of worn-out workers who are not formally disabled and thus slip through occupation-based exceptions. While PRO had a coherent structural story about dependency ratios, two clipped speeches and an unresolved distributional blind spot left its path to victory incomplete. CON's closing crystallized this by showing that indexing "stops choosing" who pays and silently charges the short-lived for the long-lived.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's reframing of PRO's care-aide empathy test in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2, arguing that extra work months are not "spread over decades" but land at the end of a career when hiring prospects and health are worst, paired with the "gate versus dial" distinction that showed age indexing is structurally untargetable compared to contribution design.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively turning Side B's strongest point—the "empathy move" regarding worn-out manual laborers—against them. Side A effectively argued that relying on payroll taxes forces these exact workers to pay a regressive premium for 40 years to fund the longer retirements of the healthy and wealthy, whereas indexing asks for a much smaller adjustment at the end of life, cushioned by targeted carve-outs. Side B's defense of recurring tax hikes as democratic "choosing" was less persuasive than Side A's framing of it as an inescapable "treadmill." Although Side A suffered minor execution costs from clipped concluding sentences in two early turns, their comparative weighing and direct answers in the later rounds secured a clear victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 dismantling of the "empathy move," contrasting the 40-year regressive payroll tax burden on a care aide with the comparatively minor burden of phased-in extra months of work at the end of a career.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully framing the tradeoff as 'money versus years.' Side A argued effectively that a frozen retirement age forces short-lived workers to subsidize the longer retirements of the wealthy. However, Side B decisively countered this by explaining that progressive taxes can shift the financial burden to high earners, whereas an age hike steals the only finite currency the short-lived worker has: their remaining years.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 distinction between paying in fractional, progressively structurable money versus paying in absolute, finite years perfectly neutralized Side A's 'follow the money' offense.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that PRO's automatic indexing rests on unfalsifiable labor-market assumptions and shifts costs onto disability systems. CON's "currency" distinction—drawing revenue from progressive capacity rather than from the scarce remaining years of low-income workers—provided a sharper, more durable comparative frame that survived the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (A4.5) turning PRO's admission that there is "no employment-rate tripwire" into a charge of "faith, not policy," while crystallizing the years-versus-money fairness distinction.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A built a more robust structural case, particularly by turning Side B's core fairness argument (the short-lived roofer) against them. A demonstrated that under B's tax-funded model, the short-lived worker subsidizes the extra retirement years of the long-lived worker, whereas A's model aligns the benefit with the individual's actual lifespan. A also cleanly defused B's pressure question about unemployment thresholds by pointing out that payroll taxes similarly collect nothing from the unemployed. B's rhetoric was strong, especially the "currency" framing, but A's logical dismantling of the cross-subsidy problem gave A the decisive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the "roofer" fairness argument by showing that a frozen age funded by payroll taxes forces the short-lived worker to subsidize the extra retirement years of the long-lived worker.
