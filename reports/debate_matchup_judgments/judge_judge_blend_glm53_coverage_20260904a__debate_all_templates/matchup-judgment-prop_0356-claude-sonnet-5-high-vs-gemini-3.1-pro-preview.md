### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending the proposition as a regulated middle path rather than a ban and by separating actuarially justified discounts from informed consent to broad data collection and downstream use. Side A effectively exposed tension in calling any higher opt-out rate coercive, and eventually supplied a limiting principle, but its larger claim that a fairness floor would eliminate telematics remained speculative and binary. Its generic appeal to existing privacy law also failed to answer B's concrete privacy risks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was the argument that Side A repeatedly defended risk-pooling mathematics while leaving consent to GPS collection, retention, sale, subpoena, and claims use largely unanswered. Side A's actuarial response narrowed B's coercion claim but did not defeat the independent case for fair disclosure and data-use terms.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won because it successfully maintained that the proposition targets the fairness of consent structures in a mandatory insurance market, not merely the actuarial math of risk pooling. Side A repeatedly defended the mathematical validity of telematics discounts but never provided a limiting principle for when financially punishing opt-out rates become coercive in a compulsory purchase market. B effectively exposed this gap through pressure questions and closing crystallization, showing that A's "actuarial accuracy" standard cannot alone guarantee genuine choice. B's framing of a surveillance consent floor as necessary consumer protection in a non-optional market proved more resilient across the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The pressure-question and second-rebuttal exchange on coercion, where B demonstrated that A had no limiting principle for when opt-out pricing stops being voluntary and starts being coercive in a mandatory insurance market, while A could only restate pooling math.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a close debate on the mandatory-product + continuous GPS-trail asymmetry. CON's strongest moves were the actuarial defense (discount = loss of subsidy, not penalty) and the math-vs-psychology distinction for vagueness, plus spotting tension in PRO's "ransom" language. But PRO held the core: insurers already live under "unfair discrimination" and similar standards, so a consent floor is not uniquely unworkable, and conceding the pooling math does not answer the consent question – safe drivers can get discounts while still deserving disclosure, retention limits and non-sale rules. CON's abandonment-to-zip-codes chain depends on the unproven claim that fair terms = program death, against PRO's concrete harms of data sale/subpoena and laundering of shift-work/roads into "behavior."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.4 where CON turns PRO's concession that pooling math is valid into a contradiction with the "punished default / ransom" coercion frame, forcing PRO to split pricing fairness from consent fairness.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won by distinguishing fixable scoring errors from the inherent privacy cost of continuous surveillance and by explaining why legally required insurance can turn a nominal discount into coercion. Side A offered a strong proxy-discrimination comparison, but wrongly treated the ability to calculate a conventional baseline as proof that fair terms are already guaranteed. Its claims that existing regulation prevents abuse and that the proposal would freeze telematics were insufficiently established, especially because the proposition permits telematics under fair opt-in conditions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer to the targeted-fix argument was decisive: correcting night-driving or braking inputs does not resolve the separate harm of continuously mapping personal movements. This preserved the need for genuine consent while exposing Side A’s narrower remedy as incomplete.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON successfully demonstrated that PRO's "genuinely fair opt-in" standard either described the existing market (making the resolution redundant) or remained dangerously vague. PRO's privacy and algorithmic-bias concerns, while real, were better addressed by targeted regulation than by the broad, speculative mandate proposed, and PRO never proved that current regulatory frameworks were actually failing to prevent coercive pricing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON in Rebuttal 2 [A4.1] turned PRO's answer in [B4.1] into a decisive concession: once PRO defined "true actuarial risk" using driving history, violations, and mileage, CON showed this described the existing status quo and gutted the resolution's necessity.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won the necessity clash. PRO defined genuinely fair as a non-inflated baseline priced on history/violations/mileage [B4.1], which CON showed already exists and which regulators already enforce. PRO never evidenced current punitive gaps or baseline erosion, leaving only speculation about future migration. With the fairness standard collapsing to the status quo, PRO's privacy and context-blindness harms reduced to a narrow input-weighting issue that CON's surgical-fix argument handles better.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.3/A4.1-A4.4 turn: using PRO's own [B4.1] definition of fair baselines to prove such baselines already exist via existing tools, erasing the contradiction charge and the need for a vague blanket mandate.
