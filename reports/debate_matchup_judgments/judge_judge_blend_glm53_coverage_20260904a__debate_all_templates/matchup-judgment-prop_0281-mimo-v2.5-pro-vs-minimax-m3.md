### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO built the stronger live path to victory across the full exchange. The decisive move was PRO's A2 answer in Rebuttal 2: proactive retention discounts would cost revenue across the entire subscriber base, while in-flow discounts only target the narrow slice actively trying to cancel. This exposed CON's "consumer welfare" defense of retention offers as primarily a profit strategy, and CON never recovered a clean counter-argument. PRO's core framing — that the asymmetric architecture is itself the harm mechanism, not merely individual deceptive practices within it — was never squarely answered by CON. CON's repeated invocation of "enforce existing law" was effectively dismissed as reactive and architecture-preserving: each company designs its gauntlet and dares regulators to challenge it. CON landed some real blows — the three-part analytical split of the proposition was sharp, the judo move on coexistence [B2.8] was clever, and the "firms will optimize sign-up friction upward" point in Rebuttal 2 was genuinely interesting — but none of these were developed enough to neutralize PRO's central argument. CON's closing "regulatory aesthetics" line was rhetorically punchy but substituted for rather than answered the administrability case PRO had already made. Multiple clipped endings on PRO's side imposed a small execution cost, but clipping occurred after main arguments were already established. On the key weighing question — bright-line symmetry versus case-by-case deception enforcement — PRO gave the cleaner reason to prefer its rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 answer in Rebuttal 2: companies offer retention discounts only at the cancellation moment (not proactively) because proactive discounts would cost revenue across the entire subscriber base, while in-flow discounts only extract value from those already trying to leave. This was the most analytically precise exchange in the debate and landed as a genuine profit-motive exposure rather than a rhetorical attack, and CON's reply — that behavior differs by channel, so the touchpoint matters — failed to explain why more targeted proactive outreach to at-risk customers was impossible.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling the specific policy mechanism in the proposition. While Side B effectively proved that asymmetric cancellation is a profitable corporate trap, Side A successfully argued that a strict symmetry mandate is a brittle "procedural counting exercise" that regulates the ratio rather than absolute friction, and would destroy in-flow retention offers that deliver measurable household savings. Side B also suffered from consistent word-count clipping.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized Side B's "bright-line" defense by showing that symmetry merely relocates ambiguity from a substantive "is this deceptive?" test to a procedural "do these flows have the same number of steps?" test, allowing firms to just add friction to sign-ups.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B wins by successfully reframing the symmetry mandate as a brittle procedural rule that misses the substantive goal of consumer protection. While Side A effectively highlighted enforcement latency and the profit motive behind friction, Side B delivered a decisive structural critique: symmetry governs the ratio, not the absolute friction level, incentivizing firms to simply make sign-up harder. Side B also grounded its case in measurable consumer welfare by defending in-flow retention offers as genuine price reductions that meet customers at the moment of decision, whereas Side A’s tollbooth framing remained more psychological than economic. Side B’s weighing of concrete household savings against regulatory aesthetics provided the cleaner, more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing dismantled the symmetry principle by showing it converts a substantive deception test into a procedural counting exercise, relocates rather than solves consumer guesswork, and perversely incentivizes companies to add friction to sign-up flows to match cancellation, ultimately trading real consumer savings for a cleaner rulebook.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A built and maintained the cleaner path to victory. Its core distinction—natural inertia versus deliberately engineered friction—was established early and never neutralized. Side B argued this distinction is "unworkable as a regulatory standard," but never seriously challenged the distinction itself, which is where the harm actually lives. A's pressure questions exposed a fundamental tension in B's position: if willing subscribers stay voluntarily, the cancellation wall only matters for people who have already decided to leave—which is exactly the harm A identifies. B's A1 response (impulsive cancellation is symmetrically real) is not without merit, but it's a narrower phenomenon than firms consciously designing multi-step cancellation funnels, and B never weighed these against each other explicitly. B's strongest line—"passive retention subsidizes low prices and free trials"—was turned against it by A's closing: calling that arrangement a "confession" that the model runs on revenue from people who would otherwise quit was rhetorically effective and analytically sound. B's workability challenge (FTC litigation, vague "comparable steps") was its best attack, but A answered it adequately by noting existing consumer-protection standards use similar comparatives, and that large firms—not small SaaS startups—have the most elaborate cancellation funnels. B's backfire pricing argument relied on theoretical mechanisms without weighing them against the costs A identified (retention call-centers, chargebacks, reputational drag). B's closing was mostly recap and shorter than the cap without adding new sharpening. A's "what is the wall for?" framing in the closing crystallized the live clash cleanly and gave the clearest reason to vote for the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal addressing the gym logistics pressure question (A4.1–A4.2): A correctly noted that sign-up parity means a gym that signed members online cannot demand in-person cancellation "because of equipment return"—equipment return is a post-cancellation logistics issue, not a cancellation-process requirement. This directly and cleanly neutralized B's most sympathetic concrete example while preserving space for genuinely complex logistics, which was the decisive exchange on workability.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B won by decisively reframing Side A's core economic defense into a concession. When Side A argued that friction-based retention revenue is necessary to subsidize free trials and low base prices, Side B effectively exposed this as an admission that the business model relies on exploiting trapped consumers to fund others. Side A also suffered from clipping in all three of its major speeches due to poor word management.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal and closing beautifully dismantled Side A's 'passive retention' argument by labeling it a 'confession' that the subscription model relies on trapped revenue as a loss-leader subsidy.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side B wins by successfully weighing the structural economic consequences against Side A's friction-reduction narrative. While A delivered a rhetorically sharp case on engineered friction and consumer traps, B consistently demonstrated that passive retention functions as revenue smoothing that subsidizes low monthly rates, free trials, and flexible terms. B's rebuttal effectively turned A's behavioral economics premise—noting that if inertia traps consumers, frictionless exits also enable impulsive cancellations—and grounded the workability critique in real compliance disparities and ongoing regulatory litigation. A's responses to the backfire argument relied on idealized competition dynamics and dismissed pricing shifts as speculation, failing to neutralize B's core trade-off. B's closing crystallized this clash cleanly, offering targeted enforcement as a superior alternative to a blunt mandate that risks raising consumer costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 turn of A's behavioral premise was decisive. By pointing out that A applied inertia asymmetrically—treating it as harmful when it keeps people in, but inert when frictionless exits might push people out during impulsive moments—B undermined the coherence of A's harm narrative and reinforced the case for why some exit friction stabilizes consumer-friendly pricing models.
