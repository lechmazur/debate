B ultimately admitted that deductions do not directly nudge the key non-participant and never adequately established that capped deductions would generate more retained net saving. A's clipped turn and late reliance on employer matches narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal was decisive: it reframed B's debt, leakage, and low-default objections as frictions affecting a positive balance, then demanded comparison with a deduction that may induce no contribution at all. Its symmetry point also exposed B's selective reliance on redesigns such as credits while criticizing escalation and portability.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing successfully dismantled B's 'net worth/debt' counterfactual by pointing out that forfeiting an employer match to pay a credit card is mathematically worse, and that the match exists in the baseline world for both sides.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate. B effectively distinguished enrollment from adequate balances and exposed A's improper reliance on automatic escalation, but never adequately answered A's central comparative objection that larger deductions mainly benefit people already saving and may shift existing assets rather than create new wealth. A supplied the cleaner mechanism for reaching non-savers early, unlocking available matches, and compounding genuinely new contributions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange attacked the tension between powerful inertia and A's claim that distressed or high-saving workers would readily override defaults. A's salience distinction partly answered that pressure, while B overextended by assuming opt-outs remove the very households auto-enrollment targets and that remaining enrollees would have joined anyway.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by masterfully turning Side A's own logic against them. B exploited A's reliance on 'inertia' to force a double bind: either inertia traps poor households in high-interest debt, or they opt out, meaning A's policy fails to help its intended target. When A introduced 'salience' to resolve this, B perfectly flipped it to explain why deductions (loud) work while defaults (quiet) anchor middle-earners low.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 responses to the Pressure Questions, specifically using A's 'salience' theory (quiet vs loud choices) to explain why savers respond to tax deductions but fail to override default contribution rates.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side A won by building a more analytically rigorous case and systematically dismantling Side B's core mechanisms. A successfully distinguished between participation (headcount) and security (wealth), forcing B to rely on the employer match to prove net wealth gains. A then sharply rebutted this by pointing out the match is a feature of the plan, not the auto-enrollment mechanism, and that if financially stressed workers opt out (as B conceded), the remaining participants are simply anchored at a lower contribution rate than they would have chosen. A also effectively caught B smuggling in "auto-escalation" (which was not in the motion) and turned B's own "salience" argument against them in the second rebuttal. Although A's second rebuttal was slightly clipped, the core arguments were fully delivered, and A's closing crystallized the clash perfectly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they turned Side B's "quiet vs. loud" salience argument against them to explain why deductions move the intensive margin while defaults remain frozen, and exposed that the employer match belongs to the plan rather than the auto-enrollment policy itself.
