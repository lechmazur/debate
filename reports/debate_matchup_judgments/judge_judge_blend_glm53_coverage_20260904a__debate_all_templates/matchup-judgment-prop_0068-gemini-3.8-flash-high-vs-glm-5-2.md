### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): The decisive clash was whether "AI-optimized persuasion campaign" can be disclosed as an objective, non-judgmental fact (PRO's nutrition-label/"Sponsored"-tag analogy) or whether it inescapably requires a purpose/intent classification that platforms cannot mechanically read off their dashboards (CON's core claim). CON pressed this with concrete, unanswered counterexamples—a union GOTV campaign, a local bakery's lookalike-audience ad, a public health agency's demographic messaging—all using identical predictive-optimization infrastructure to malicious actors. PRO's rebuttal ultimately conceded that "union, retailer, PAC utilize the same optimization pipelines" and would all be labeled identically, which validates rather than defeats CON's warning-fatigue and line-drawing objections. PRO never articulated a workable threshold distinguishing "basic A/B testing" from qualifying "AI-optimized persuasion" after CON called this a spectrum, not a bright line. CON's closing crystallized this unresolved gap cleanly, while PRO's closing largely restated assertions of objectivity without engaging the specific counterexamples. Both sides argued with discipline and no fabricated sourcing, but CON's feasibility critique constituted a live, undefeated route to victory against a "should be required" proposition that depends on a coherent, administrable category.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 point that nutrition labels disclose objective content (grams of sugar) while the proposition's "persuasion campaign" label demands a purpose classification, paired with the union-vs-predatory-political-ad example showing identical mechanisms — a point PRO never squarely resolved in later rounds.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by sustaining the implementation dilemma: either the label mechanically covers most optimized advertising and loses signal value, or platforms must make contestable threshold and purpose judgments about AI optimization, persuasion, and micro-groups. Side A persuasively defended mechanism-based transparency, but repeatedly equated ordinary predictive targeting with exploitation of psychological vulnerabilities without establishing that the disclosed mechanism proves such exploitation. Side A also used several nonexistent reference prefixes, modestly reducing execution quality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its response to the Sponsored analogy: payment is a binary fact, while AI-optimized, persuasion campaign, and micro-group each require boundary choices. Its bakery and public-health comparisons exposed the gap between disclosing technical targeting and identifying especially manipulative persuasion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): CON wins because PRO could not defend the proposition as written. The motion requires labeling "AI-optimized persuasion campaigns," yet PRO consistently collapsed that category into mere technical use of predictive optimization, eliding the subjective purpose-judgment that "persuasion" demands. CON showed that the same AI-targeting infrastructure powers benign ads (local bakeries, public health agencies, unions) and manipulative ones, so a mechanical label would either cover nearly all digital advertising—causing warning fatigue and diluting existing AI-generated content labels—or force platforms to make exactly the subjective intent distinctions PRO promised to avoid. That line-drawing dilemma survived the full exchange and undermined the workability of the mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated demonstration that PRO equated "persuasion campaign" with "used predictive optimization," using the local bakery and public-health examples to show the label would be either vacuous or arbitrarily subjective.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): The debate turned on whether "AI-optimized persuasion" is a coherent, administrable category distinct from routine algorithmic ad delivery. CON pressed this relentlessly: political/commercial ad disclosure tracks objective facts (money, identity), while "persuasion optimization" requires judging subjective intent; when PRO finally offered a technical line ("multivariate creative testing against inferred psychological segments"), CON turned it against them by noting this is precisely what standard dynamic creative optimization already does for charities, job postings, and health drives alike—meaning PRO's own definition proves the category is ubiquitous rather than narrow. PRO never adequately closed this gap; its final answer ("hard is not impossible") restates faith in administrability without rebutting the specific technical convergence CON identified. PRO's normative case (power asymmetry, informed resistance, analogies to nutrition/allergen labels) remained rhetorically strong and partially landed against the "label fatigue" objection, but the deeper practical-implementation objection—central to a motion about a legal *requirement*—was left substantially live for CON. CON's closing crystallized this cleanly ("a warning that applies to everything protects no one"), while PRO's closing mostly reasserted asymmetry without directly answering the DCO-collapse point. PRO also suffered a real execution cost: its first rebuttal was clipped before the judge could see the full argument, cutting off mid-thought at a rhetorically important moment. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format correctly, but CON's questions exposed the more decisive weakness and were never fully resolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's move in Rebuttal 2 (B4.3-B4.4), turning PRO's own proposed technical definition (multivariate creative testing against inferred segments) into proof that the "persuasion optimization" category is indistinguishable from routine dynamic creative optimization used by charities and public health campaigns, was the most damaging exchange—PRO never adequately answered it in the following speeches.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): B won the central administrability clash. A identified a real information asymmetry and correctly argued that funding, privacy, and optimization disclosures serve different purposes. But its proposed distinction between psychological persuasion and ordinary behavioral variant testing remained fuzzy. B convincingly argued that the identified trigger overlaps with routine dynamic optimization, creating either broad label fatigue or subjective platform line-drawing. A’s clipped rebuttal and B’s erroneous reference IDs were execution costs, but neither reversed that substantive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest response was turning A’s proposed technical standard against it: matching creative variants to inferred behavioral segments plausibly describes ordinary ad optimization, not a clearly separable category of psychological exploitation. A repeatedly asserted that platforms know the difference but did not establish an objective enforcement rule.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory by defending a narrow, technically coherent distinction between routine delivery and AI-optimized persuasion that tests message variants against inferred psychological vulnerabilities. Side B repeatedly collapsed this into "default plumbing" and "subjective intent" but never squarely refuted A's claim that platforms already track this technical operation. A neutralized label-fatigue and evasion arguments, while B's downstream case depended on a conflation that A had already dismantled. B never gave a convincing reason why the power asymmetry should remain invisible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where A defined an objective technical standard (multivariate creative testing based on inferred psychological vulnerabilities vs. simple demographic delivery), showed that B's objections about subjective intent missed the mark, and demonstrated that B's arguments about fatigue and censorship all depended on conflating two distinguishable processes.
