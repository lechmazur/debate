### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B built a tighter, more adaptive case. B's core epistemic challenge (blind-breaking/expectancy contamination) and its ketamine "regulatory capture" argument—that oversight frameworks get written or eroded after commercial industries form and lobby against constraint—were never squarely neutralized. A's rebuttal to this was largely assertion ("the framework precedes the market") rather than a mechanism showing why this time regulation would hold. B also answered A's pressure questions more concretely, offering a specific evidentiary threshold and timeframe that defused A's "permanent denial" framing, which A's closing failed to directly engage. A's strongest point—that expanded access is only a "trickle" serving few patients—landed, but B's closing reframed this as an intentional, honest tradeoff rather than a fatal concession, leaving the exchange live but not clearly won by A. Overall, B's rebuttals did more direct damage to A's key analogies and consent framing, giving B the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point that ketamine clinics could have been required to report outcomes but weren't—because industries lobby against oversight once established—directly undercut A's "the proposition specifies regulation upfront" defense, and A never offered a comparable mechanism for why this framework would resist the same capture.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by keeping the decisive clash on epistemology and political economy: blind-breaking/expectancy contamination plus the ketamine precedent that scaled non-trial access produced patients and billing, not usable long-term knowledge or durable oversight. A was strong on access gaps and urgency, and landed real pressure with the “trickle” critique and unreachable-standard charge, but B answered directly—bridge sized to uncertainty, concrete multi-year replication threshold, and scale/incentive differences—while A never fully neutralized observed industry drift beyond paper frameworks. Comparative weighing favored B’s bridge over A’s bet.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s ketamine line (clinics coexist with approval yet yield no usable long-term evidence; access at scale freezes science and erodes oversight) surviving A’s “gray zone / under-regulation” reply.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): CON won the two decisive clashes. On epistemics, the blind-breaking/expectancy contamination of self-reported mood was never answered beyond "doesn't erase effect sizes," while PRO's permanent-denial charge was defused by a concrete, achievable threshold (larger independent multisite trials with active comparators, years of follow-up). On political economy, the ketamine precedent - clinics generating billing records not knowledge, and regulation promised but never arriving because an industry lobbies against it - went largely unrefuted; PRO's "demand a framework before opening" asserted funding via REMS-like fees but did not explain why this market would avoid the observed sequence. CON also handled Pressure better, conceding expanded access doesn't produce knowledge and explaining scale/incentive difference.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 billing-records line: ketamine's tens of thousands of clinic patients produced essentially no usable long-term data, proving regulated outside-trial access does not generate knowledge and that commercial pressure erodes oversight - directly undercutting PRO's registry/REMS solution.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON's rebuttal chain proved more durable. CON's reversal of PRO's anesthesia analogy (unconscious body-work vs. conscious, suggestible dyadic therapy) was sharp and went largely unanswered. CON's "category mismatch" point—that if remission is expectancy-driven, patients bear pharmacological psychosis/dissociation risk for a psychological benefit—struck at the evidentiary core of PRO's case and was never fully neutralized; PRO's closing reasserted the asymmetry framing but didn't directly dismantle the mismatch logic. PRO's rebuttal to the screening dilemma (severity doesn't require psychosis/mania; chronic SI without acute intent still counts as "severe") was genuinely strong and mostly landed, but CON's closing simply restated the dilemma without engaging that specific carve-out, leaving the exchange a wash rather than a CON loss. PRO's reframing of the FDA decision as a process/data-integrity issue rather than a safety verdict also stood largely uncontested, helping PRO. On balance, though, CON's Pressure Questions answers were more intellectually honest (clearly conceding "not blanket, contingent on future evidence" rather than dodging), and CON's closing more precisely isolated the decisive question ("does it actually work") that PRO's case never resolved. PRO's Rebuttal 2 being clipped before its closing flourish is a real, if modest, execution cost. The debate was close and both sides argued in good faith without fake precision, but CON edges it on cumulative rebuttal force and framing discipline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's reversal of the anesthesia analogy in Rebuttal 1 (conscious, suggestible therapy vs. unconscious surgical monitoring) was the single most damaging, unanswered blow, undercutting PRO's central regulatory-parity argument.
