### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON built a more disciplined case by conceding the accessibility problem while denying that it requires full simultaneous release, offering a "short window plus mandatory accessible home editions" alternative that PRO never squarely dismantled. The decisive exchange was the pressure round: CON asked PRO to distinguish genuinely new revenue from displaced or merely time-shifted purchases, and what screening loss would matter. PRO's answers ("we do not need to prove zero displacement," "no marginal loss... justifies exclusion") effectively conceded that cinema access is treated as weightless in their framework, which CON exploited sharply in Rebuttal 2 and Closing. PRO's own pressure questions were rhetorically loaded but didn't expose a comparable unanswered burden—CON's answers (bounded wait, viability of a limited restriction) held up. PRO's case leaned heavily on moral framing ("second-class citizenship," "corporate protectionism") without ever answering the concrete mechanism problem of replaced ticket revenue, while CON kept returning to that unresolved asymmetry. CON's closing crystallized this cleanly: PRO counts one harm as human and the other as corporate, without weighing both sides' human stakes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3-A4.5), pointing out that PRO's own answer to the pressure question conceded displacement without addressing replacement revenue and that "no marginal loss of local screenings" effectively makes cinema access weightless, was the most damaging and unanswered exchange of the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A wins because it established a live, unresolved moral burden: forcing disabled and working-class audiences to wait weeks to participate in opening-weekend culture is an unjust structural exclusion that cannot be justified by preserving a business model that requires artificial scarcity to survive. CON never overcame the framing that a bounded wait is second-class citizenship, and while CON landed a sharp economic point about displaced ticket sales, A successfully absorbed it by showing that those displaced are precisely the marginalized populations the proposition is meant to serve. CONs attempt to weigh lost local cinema access against day-one exclusion was clear but could not overcome A's central story that theaters must earn their audience rather than hold films hostage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A Rebuttal 2 turning CONs substitution argument into proof that theatrical exclusivity exists only to artificially trap consumers who would otherwise choose affordable, accessible home viewing, thereby reframing industry protection as corporate protectionism at the expense of human accessibility.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (A) won the decisive economic weighing. PRO (B) established real accessibility benefits but never answered the core substitution critique: many home rentals are displaced tickets or purchases brought forward, not new revenue. A's R1/R2 distinction between distinct vs non-substitutable products and the temporal-shift point was never rebutted. B's A2 answer — "No marginal loss of local screenings justifies" a window — conceded an absolutist trade that refuses to weigh future audience access, which A effectively used to flip PRO's moral frame from corporate vs human to human vs human across time.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2/A4 hammering the additional vs displaced/forward-shifted purchase distinction and exposing B's "No marginal loss..." concession as making cinema access weightless.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): The decisive fault line was whether CON's "structural certainty" of mass substitution and theater collapse was actually established, or merely asserted with increasing confidence. PRO's pressure question (Q1) directly demanded that CON quantify why substitution outweighs new home revenue broadly enough to justify near-universal exclusivity, and CON's answer ("basic audience mechanics establish this... general audience vastly outnumbers the housebound audience") simply restated the intuitive mechanism rather than closing the gap—an unanswered burden that PRO correctly flagged in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing. PRO also successfully defended its narrower "end the presumption, not abolish the window" reading against CON's "retreat" charge by consistently distinguishing a changed default from blanket abolition, which is a defensible reading of the motion's wording rather than an evasive swap. CON's rhetoric (the "supreme irony," "lifeblood") was vivid and its economic mechanism plausible as ordinary background reasoning, but the case never advanced past reasserting the same causal story under pressure, while PRO sharpened the accessibility-versus-uncertain-harm tradeoff each round. That gives PRO the cleaner, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question Q1 ("what establishes that substitution outweighs additional home revenue broadly enough to justify exclusivity for almost every big title?") forced CON to answer with pure assertion ("basic audience mechanics establish this") rather than new grounding, a gap PRO then exploited decisively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by keeping the accessibility harm concrete and unresolved—housebound viewers are categorically excluded under theatrical-first—while showing that Side B’s industry-collapse scenario was an unsupported leap from possible substitution to structural certainty. A consistently distinguished ending the default presumption from abolishing all windows, answered pressure questions directly, and crystallized the clash around who bears the cost of uncertainty. B treated exclusivity, attendance, and cultural significance as interchangeable without proving the causal scale, and repeatedly asserted economic doom as mechanical law rather than demonstrated outcome.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s sustained attack on B’s economic-collapse narrative, beginning in Rebuttal 1 with the observation that B counted substitution but ignored additional home revenue from excluded viewers, and sharpening in Rebuttal 2 with the distinction between studio revenue and exhibitor viability [A4.6]. This exchange dismantled B’s conflation of exclusivity with survival and left B’s central harm speculative.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the proposition as "more simultaneous with scrutiny" while CON read it as "simultaneous for almost every title" and asserted collapse. B exposed the decisive gap: CON's $30 vs $80 substitution story shows a possible loss, not total-market balance, and ignores non-attenders who are zero revenue under exclusivity. CON never established scale for mass closures, relying on "structural certainty," and its cultural-zeitgeist claim assumes exclusivity is the only concentrator. PRO's cost-of-uncertainty weighing — certain exclusion for housebound vs unquantified theater risk — survived pressure and gave the cleaner voting reason.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B2.3 vs A1.2-A1.3 — PRO reframed revenue as total across channels (substitution + new demand) and forced CON to defend leap from some cannibalization to industry collapse, which CON never quantified in A4/R2.
