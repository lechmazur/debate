A's strongest counter-punch — that volunteer models concentrate risk on the less-advantaged — was met adequately by B's "pay and protect volunteers better" reply, keeping that exchange roughly even. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions format correctly with A1/A2 labels, but B's answers integrated more tightly into its broader argument, while A's answers introduced specifics (six months) that became a liability. Overall, B provided the cleaner, more mechanistically grounded path to a verdict.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's line of attack was more analytically disciplined and went less answered. B's core move—that conscription competes for the same scarce inputs (instructors, barracks, equipment) that produce actual deployable capability, rather than adding to them—was never squarely rebutted; A's attempted cost answer (A4.1) compared only reservist time to a professional's wage, and B's B4.3 caught that omission cleanly and it went unaddressed in A's closing. B also exposed a real internal tension in A's proposal (civilian tracks meant to soften resentment simultaneously dilute the promised military depth, B4.5), which A never resolved. B's scope argument—that Baltic/Finnish conscription reflects front-line-specific threat and institutional fit rather than a general European prescription—was raised in the opening and reasserted in closing without a direct answer from A beyond restating recruitment failures elsewhere. A's strongest asset, the 'twenty years of missed voluntary recruitment targets' point, was a good pressure point but B's answer (specific, if somewhat asserted, reforms: housing, family stability, employer-protected training) at least contested it rather than conceding, keeping that exchange live rather than decisive. On balance B narrowed the debate more effectively and left A's key mechanism (mandatory service converting into usable depth) more exposed than A left B's (targeted voluntary capability) exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's B4.3 exposing that A's cost comparison for conscription ignored bases, instructors, ammunition, and the opportunity cost of pulling professionals to train recruits — a gap A never closed in the closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): B won by forcing the real comparison: usable deployable capability under scarce instructors, bases, kit, and recurring exercises, not headcount. B also kept the motion’s general-Europe scope against special-case frontline models and exposed the selective-service fairness problem more cleanly than A’s jury/tax analogies repaired it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s system-cost replies (esp. B2.1–B2.4 and B4.3) neutralizing A’s “depth multiplies professionals / refresher drills cost a fraction” route by forcing full pipeline accounting and a per-euro comparison to voluntary reserves.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A wins by grounding the need for mass in the attritional reality of modern warfare (the "day 100" framing) and effectively weaponizing the 20-year failure of all-volunteer recruitment against Side B's "just fix retention" alternative. By pointing out that Europe has already spent two decades trying to recruit volunteers and failed to fill combat arms, Side A effectively neutralized Side B's core alternative, framing it as an "expensive illusion" and shifting the burden back to compulsion as the only proven mechanism for generating mass.
