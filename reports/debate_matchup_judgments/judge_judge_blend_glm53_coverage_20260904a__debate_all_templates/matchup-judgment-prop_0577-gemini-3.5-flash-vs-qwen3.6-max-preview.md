### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but Side A (CON) edges out the win on the central clash. The decisive question is whether legal pathways reduce or merely relocate irregular migration, and CON builds a more durable path here. PRO's core mechanism—that seasonal visas are "self-enforcing" because workers return to preserve future eligibility—depends critically on detection of violations, which PRO never adequately separated from interior enforcement. CON consistently exploited this: the employer self-policing claim [B4.1] is the weakest link in PRO's chain, and CON's point that seasonal sectors structurally prefer off-books flexible labor directly undermines it. PRO's "cage effect" is a real phenomenon and the best live argument on their side; CON's response (biometric tracking, expedited removal modernizing the picture) is plausible but incomplete. However, PRO never neutralized CON's overstay-conversion argument—the claim that expanded pathways shift irregular presence from the border to the interior, where detection is harder. CON's closing crystallization ("relocate vs. reduce") is the cleanest framing in the debate and captures the comparative question the motion asks. CON's pressure questions were also sharper, targeting the mechanism PRO relies on most, and PRO's A1 answer (employer certification self-policing) was the weakest answer of the debate. PRO's supply-and-demand framing is rhetorically strong throughout but ultimately the self-enforcement mechanism doesn't survive the scrutiny applied to it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 exchange on employer incentives [A2.1–A2.2]: PRO argued workers will return to preserve future visa eligibility, but CON showed that employer behavior in seasonal sectors actively undermines this—employers prefer flexible off-books retention and rarely face consequences. This directly dismantled PRO's self-enforcing compliance mechanism and was never squarely answered, remaining live through the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by keeping the comparison focused on substitution: legal, repeatable work channels redirect economically motivated migrants away from smugglers, while border enforcement leaves labor demand intact and can create a cage effect. Side A landed strong attacks on overstays, quotas, and employer incentives, but did not prove those effects would outweigh the large substitution effect of legal pathways. A's defense of enforcement also relied on idealized credible enforcement plus interior systems, which blurred the comparison and did not fully answer B's demand-side critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive rebuttal was that repeatable legal access gives workers a concrete reason to return, while enforcement makes crossings costlier and therefore encourages permanent irregular settlement; A challenged this well with employer and overstay incentives, but B preserved the stronger comparative mechanism.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A wins because its economic-incentive and cage-effect framing consistently neutralized B’s network/overstay claims across rebuttals, turning B’s own pressure questions into confirmation of self-enforcing compliance rather than evasion; A adapted cleanly while B repeated relocation arguments without closing the gap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1–A2.4 directly dismantled B1.1/B1.3 on networks and overstays by showing legal access removes cartel leverage and preserves return incentives, a clash A revisited effectively in Rebuttal 2 answers.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B built a more durable causal path throughout the exchange. The core PRO argument—that irregular migration is a labor-demand response and enforcement leaves that mismatch intact while raising smuggling premiums and converting circular migrants into permanent undocumented settlers—was introduced clearly in the opening and never squarely neutralized. Side B also made a crucial clarifying move in rebuttal: sector-tied visas are not meant to absorb all global migration demand, only verified shortage labor in specific industries, which directly deflated Side A's most potent "capped lottery" claim. The modern-program distinction (re-entry guarantees eliminate the overstay incentive that created bridgehead effects in mid-century programs) was a genuine and specific counter that Side A never adequately answered. Side A's "doorway without walls" framing was rhetorically strong and the enforcement-as-prerequisite logic is internally coherent, but it became somewhat circular—enforcement makes legal pathways work, yet enforcement alone fails to address the mismatch that drives irregular crossings. Side A's answers to pressure questions were competent but largely restated earlier positions rather than advancing the comparison. Side B's A1 response—that migration is sector-driven and network-dependent rather than a monolithic global tide—offered a meaningful reframe that kept the PRO case intact. Both sides avoided fake citations and grounded claims in plausible economic reasoning. The margin is clear but not dominant: Side A held real argumentative ground, particularly on the unavoidability of caps and the employer-incentive problem, but Side B answered those pressures more persuasively across the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 response to the "bridgehead" argument [B2.3]: distinguishing outdated single-use visa designs from modern frameworks with guaranteed re-entry was the most decisive single exchange. It turned Side A's strongest historical example against them by showing that the very mechanism producing bridgehead effects—risky return trips—is what enforcement worsens rather than solves, while modern pathways eliminate the overstay incentive structurally. Side A never recovered a coherent counter to this distinction.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the debate focused on the comparative mechanism: irregular migration is driven by labor demand, and legal pathways channel that demand more sustainably than border escalation. Side A had a strong caps-and-shadow-market objection, but leaned heavily on proving that pathways need some enforcement rather than proving increased border enforcement is more effective. A also relied on workplace verification and employer penalties, which did not cleanly defend the motion’s border-enforcement alternative. B more directly answered the cap argument by narrowing to verified sectoral demand and job signals, and A never fully neutralized B’s claim that tougher borders raise smuggling premiums, shift routes, and trap circular migrants into permanent irregularity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s rebuttal that enforcement leaves the labor mismatch intact while legal, renewable sectoral pathways change employer and migrant incentives was the decisive exchange, especially because Side A’s answer depended on internal workplace enforcement rather than border enforcement itself.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A built the clearer causal mechanism showing how scaled sectoral visas drain the economic motive for irregular crossing even under realistic caps, while directly answering pressure on employer and migrant incentives. Side B repeated the cap-and-surplus argument across rounds without neutralizing that structural alignment, leaving enforcement's comparative weakness on root causes unaddressed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 directly answered B's pressure questions on surplus and employer choice by reframing migration as sector-driven rather than monolithic, flipping the incentive comparison.
