### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's "dependency trap" line (pricing systems have a fiscal stake in cars persisting, with no city ever weaning off toll revenue) proved the most durable pressure point of the debate. A's Rebuttal 2 answer ("politicians cut taxes constantly") was generic and never named a concrete pricing system that scaled itself down, and A's own Closing oddly restated this unmet challenge without decisively countering it, effectively conceding the point rather than closing it out. B's reframing of exemptions as smaller, static, and need-based versus a toll that merely asks "can you pay" was also cleaner and less rebutted than A's "supplicant vs autonomy" counter, which was raised but not pressed home in the final round. A's false-binary and humanitarian-impact arguments were strong and never fully demolished, keeping this competitive, but A's closing was clipped mid-thought right as it pivoted back to its strongest material, costing it rhetorical closure. B's closing, by contrast, landed cleanly on the "manages cars vs. ends them" framing, giving the debate its clearest final throughline.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure question ("name the city that voluntarily weaned itself off toll revenue") and A's unconvincing, unspecific answer, later highlighted unanswered in B's closing, was the most decisive exchange.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won a close debate by making the decisive comparison easier: both sides conceded alternatives matter, then A showed pricing is structurally aimed at perpetual revenue while a core ban is aimed at the end state. A's pressure on the dependency trap (no city voluntarily weaning off tolls; fiscal stake in continued driving) was never fully neutralized—B's bureaucracy-cuts-both-ways and tax-cut analogies missed the unique purpose-behavior coupling. B was strong on sequencing, layered tools, and permit-as-supplicant dignity, and stayed competitive throughout, but tended to dissolve the motion into "mixes exist" rather than defeat ban-in-the-core on the proposition as written. A's clearer destination framing and sharper follow-through after pressure outweigh B's practical and equity counters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1 dependency trap (pricing needs drivers to fund alternatives, so never finishes the job) and the closing crystallize that pricing has no car-light end state; B never produced a weaning example or clean incentive reply.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won by proving that congestion pricing creates a structural fiscal dependency on the very car traffic it claims to reduce, whereas a ban aligns city incentives with a genuinely car-free end state. A neutralized B's equity objections by showing they argue for exemptions rather than pricing, and B never recovered from A's exposure of the missing sunset plan or the moral incoherence of indefinitely charging vulnerable users. While B's sequencing and false-binary frames were competent, they amounted to an evasion of the central-district choice the proposition posed, and A's closing crystallized the comparative clash more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The dependency-trap exchange in the later rebuttals, where A used NYC and London dynamics to show priced cities become fiscally coupled to continued car volume with no credible sunset plan, and B could only answer with the generic observation that politicians sometimes cut taxes—failing to name a single city that had actually weaned itself off toll revenue.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side A built a cleaner, more sustained line of attack: it forced Side B to concede that its "ban" still requires loading zones, delivery permits, and curb fees, effectively reframing the debate as "pricing alone vs. pricing-plus-prohibition" and demanding B justify the extra layer. B never cleanly met that burden—its funding answer (redirected road budgets, grants, bonds) was something a pricing city could access on top of toll revenue, and its equity answer (targeted delivery permits) reintroduced the very exemption bureaucracy it had criticized pricing for. Most decisively, A caught a direct contradiction: B called irreversibility a "feature" in Rebuttal 1 but then claimed the ban "reverses with a single council vote" in Rebuttal 2. B's closing pivoted to a philosophical reframe ("streets belong to people") instead of resolving this contradiction or answering the "pricing plus prohibition" collapse, which reads as evasion of live pressure rather than resolution. B's opening and framing were strong and its equity/public-space argument had real rhetorical force, but the later rounds show A doing the sharper comparative work and closing the debate on cleaner terms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 exposing B's contradiction between "irreversibility is a feature" (B2.7) and "a ban reverses with a single council vote" (B4.2), paired with the "pricing plus prohibition" reframe that B's closing never squarely answered.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B won by turning A's funding and access answers into a decisive comparative trap: once paid loading zones, delivery permits, and curb fees are admitted, the live choice is pricing alone versus pricing plus a blunt prohibition layer, and A never justified that extra cost on revenue, equity for genuine-need trips, spillover response, or adaptability. A's public-space hierarchy frame was sharper rhetorically, but B repeatedly made the clash easier to decide—especially by catching A's irreversibility contradiction (feature, then one-council-vote reverse) and showing pricing can differentiate leisure drivers from caregivers/trades while funding buses immediately. Format clips on A's opening and B's closing were minor and roughly offset.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pricing-plus-prohibition flip (B2.4/B4.1): A's own loading/permit/curb-fee funding tools concede pricing mechanics, so the ban must justify an extra irreversible layer it never clearly bought.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing that Side A's ban still relies on pricing mechanics (permits, curb fees, loading zones), so the real choice is "pricing alone" versus "pricing plus an extra prohibition layer." B demonstrated that this extra layer adds stranded trips, loses toll revenue, and sacrifices calibration without concrete compensating benefits. B also caught A in a damaging contradiction: A first called irreversibility a "feature," then claimed a ban could be undone with one council vote, undermining both their lock-in and flexibility arguments. A's philosophical "people-first" framing remained eloquent but could not overcome B's persistent comparative weighing on equity, funding, and adaptability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal exposed that A's ban requires the same pricing infrastructure A criticized (delivery permits, curb fees) and caught A contradicting themselves on irreversibility, then showed every funding source A proposed was also available to a pricing city—plus tolls—making the prohibition layer a pure cost with no marginal gain.
