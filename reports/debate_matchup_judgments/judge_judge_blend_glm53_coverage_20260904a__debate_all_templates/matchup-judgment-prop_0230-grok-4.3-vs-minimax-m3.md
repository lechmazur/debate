### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON built and sustained the decisive clash throughout: Finland's conscript model depends on specific preconditions—a live border threat, continuous refresher cycles, a funded NCO corps—that Western European countries such as Portugal, Ireland, and Spain do not possess. CON crystallized this as an admission of narrow preconditions rather than a general defense of the policy. PRO's best answer ([B4.3]) was that conscription "creates" those conditions rather than requiring them first, but CON's response—that turning mass into deployable reserve capacity takes at least fifteen years and diverts the very funds needed for immediate capability—was never squarely answered. PRO never escaped this trap. On modern warfare readiness, PRO's [B4.2] answer (conscripts handle support tasks freeing professionals) was a competent partial response, but CON's point that the opening fights in any realistic European scenario require combined-arms professionals, drone operators, and cyber specialists rather than fresh basic trainees remained largely live. On cohesion, PRO's claim that voluntary programs have "plateaued" was ungrounded while CON pointed to France's Service Civique and Germany's Bundesfreiwilligendienst as existing mixed-cohort alternatives; PRO dismissed these but never demonstrated they perform worse than conscript systems. PRO also made imprecise claims ("data from existing conscript systems show measurable drops") without any substantiation, while CON relied on plausible background examples (Russia 2022, Germany 2011, Sweden/Norway) with appropriate epistemic caution. The clipping of CON's later speeches is a real execution cost but did not obscure CON's arguments enough to change the outcome. The preconditions trap remained unresolved in PRO's favor throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [A2.2] crystallization—"conscription works where everything conscription needs already exists, which is not a defense of the policy but an admission of its narrow preconditions"—was the most decisive single exchange. PRO's entire case rested on Finland, yet PRO's own rebuttal had conceded that Russia's failure stemmed from absent infrastructure. CON turned that distinction back on PRO to show the argument was circular, and PRO never recovered from it.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): A won narrowly. Its best route was showing that Finland-style readiness depends on sustained refresher cycles, cadres, political threat perception, and funding, so mandatory service by itself risks producing low-readiness rosters while diverting money and young workers from more relevant professional and specialist capacity. B had a coherent answer that conscription is the scalable starting point and can cover support roles, but its cohesion claims leaned on under-grounded causal assertions, and it never fully overcame A's time-to-capability and opportunity-cost critique. A's repeated clipping was an execution cost, keeping the margin small.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal to B's Finland example was decisive: it reframed conscription as requiring the very infrastructure B claimed it would create, making B's "start building now" answer plausible but not enough to neutralize the delay and cost problem.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won because it delivered complete, unclipped speeches in every round and crystallized the comparative stakes in a tight closing, while Side B suffered three word-limit clips that truncated two rebuttals and its final speech. Substantively, A successfully defended a division-of-labor model in which conscripts handle territorial defense and logistics, freeing the professional core for cyber, drone, and intelligence roles. That blunted B’s strongest attack that modern warfare needs specialists rather than mass. On cohesion, A showed that the voluntary civic programs B cited have not demonstrated comparable population-level integration, keeping its second prong alive. B’s preconditions/inversion argument was sharp, but without a full closing or complete development in clipped speeches, it could not fully resolve A’s live path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The preconditions exchange: B’s pressure question forcing A to justify conscription in countries without Finland’s live threat or refresher infrastructure, A’s “create the conditions” answer that conscription builds the cohort and pipeline simultaneously, and B’s counter that this is a fifteen-year diversion rather than a shortcut. A’s closing reframed this as an unavoidable first step against permanent under-capacity, allowing the argument to survive B’s pressure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by building and sustaining a decisive comparative pressure that Side A never escaped: after two decades of market-based recruitment producing hollow force structures, what concrete mechanism delivers a callable mass reserve within five years? Side A never answered this. Its Rebuttal 2 response—"targeted financial incentives combined with NATO-integrated reserve frameworks"—was an assertion, not a mechanism, and it came after B's Q1 had explicitly demanded specificity. That unanswered burden is fatal because the voluntary alternative is not hypothetical; it is the extant policy whose failure is B's central premise, and A never challenged that premise directly. B also executed effective reframes throughout: the "straw goal" correction (conscription serves reserve generation, not active-unit padding), the "complements not competitors" answer on specialists, and the "refresher cycles are the logic, not a bug" answer to A2—each of which materially narrowed A's live lines of attack. On the social cohesion dimension, B pointed to Finland and Switzerland as concrete examples of universality working, while A relied on vague references to "several European states," "historical patterns," and "surveys from existing programs" without naming any. B's Q2 directly called this out, and A's Rebuttal 2 repeated the same vague formulation rather than providing specifics—a clear epistemic failure that reduces the weight of A's cohesion objections. A's strongest live argument was the budget competition between mass conscript infrastructure and specialist tracks, which B answered only partially. But that partial answer was enough given the larger structural asymmetry: A's preferred status quo is what produced the hollow armies B described, and A offered no concrete path out of that gap. B's closing crystallized the central choice cleanly; A's closing recycled earlier points without sharpening the comparison. The execution and comparative discipline were clearly stronger on B's side.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 on the "straw goal" [B2.2–B2.3]: by correctly identifying that A's training-capacity objection assumed the wrong goal (active-unit reinforcement rather than reserve generation), B dismantled the structural logic of A's defense case. This reframe held through the rest of the debate and was never effectively answered—A continued attacking the straw goal in subsequent rounds rather than engaging the reserve-generation comparator B had set up.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): B won by making the central comparison clearer: Europe needs mass reserves, voluntarism and NATO pooling are the failed status quo, and A never supplied a credible mechanism to produce comparable readiness on the needed timeline. A had plausible objections about training capacity, specialist shortages, and uneven enforcement, but leaned on vague unsupported claims about surveys and European examples, then failed to answer B's pressure for specifics. B's social cohesion case was less fully proven, but its defense readiness route and comparative weighing carried the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's decisive exchange was pressing A on how voluntary incentives would produce a mass callable reserve within five years; A's answer stayed generic, allowing B to frame A as offering implementation complaints against conscription but no workable alternative.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by establishing that mandatory service is the only viable path to mass reserve generation after two decades of failed voluntarism, and by turning B's implementation objections into arguments for genuine universality rather than abandonment. B failed to offer any concrete mechanism by which voluntarism could deliver callable reserves within five years, and conceded that the social frictions they cited stemmed from selective enforcement—the very half-measure A argued against. A's division-of-labor framing (active specialists + reserve mass) cleanly neutralized B's quality-vs-quantity objection, while A's consistent comparative weighing—measuring the proposal against the demonstrably failed status quo—made the decisive clash clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 (Turn 3) and Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where A demonstrated that B's active-unit proficiency objection attacked a straw goal by showing conscription serves reserve generation, turned the selective-enforcement complaint into a case for true universality, and exposed that B offered only implementation difficulties against a status quo that had already failed.
