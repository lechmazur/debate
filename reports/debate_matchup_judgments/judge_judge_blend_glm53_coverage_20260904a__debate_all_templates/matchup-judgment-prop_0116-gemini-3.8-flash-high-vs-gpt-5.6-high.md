CON’s ex ante cap vs ex post liability distinction held, and the execution hit from B’s clipped Rebuttal 2 did not erase that comparative edge.

B’s exclusion concern remained live but was weakened by A’s enrollment-volume and anti-discrimination counters, and B’s clipped Rebuttal 2 left the point underdeveloped. While CON distinguished ex ante caps from ex post liability ([B4.1]), PRO’s framing that structural underperformance is identifiable—and that CON merely wants universities immune—proved the more damaging and memorable move.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won by keeping the motion narrow (partial, program-level, repeated debt-to-earnings failure) and turning CON’s best harms back on CON’s preferred caps/eligibility cutoffs, which create the same screening pressure but more bluntly. CON landed real redlining and public-service cost concerns and exploited PRO’s imperfect-enforcement concession, but never justified zero institutional downside when schools control price and still collect tuition. Comparative weighing favored shared skin-in-the-game over socialized default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Q1/A1: same redlining incentive under CON’s strict eligibility thresholds, with partial multi-year liability as the less distortionary alternative.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a clear moral-hazard story and, decisively, reframing the comparative choice around institutional accountability. A showed the motion targets repeated program-level failure with only partial liability, blunting CON’s redlining and public-service objections: low pay alone is not punished if debt is proportionate, and cohort-based safeguards are no weaker than the binary eligibility cutoffs CON itself endorses. B repeatedly treated the plan as per-borrower underwriting despite PRO’s clear program-level design, and never resolved why hidden debt distress is fairer than shared responsibility. A’s closing crystallized the asymmetry—universities control price and promises, so they should bear part of the risk when programs repeatedly fail.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where A answered B’s pressure questions by demonstrating that strict eligibility thresholds create the same screening incentive in stronger, binary form, and that a program surviving only by shifting recurring losses onto graduates is not genuinely preserved by the status quo. This reframing made B’s alternative appear internally inconsistent and clarified the decisive clash.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) built the more resilient path: repeated, cohort-based, partial liability on debt-relative earnings directly answers moral hazard and control-of-price. CON's two live routes — redlining and public-service shuttering — were materially damaged when B showed (1) CON's own strict eligibility thresholds create the same or stronger screening incentive and are binary rather than graduated, and (2) the metric punishes unaffordable debt, not low pay, requiring repricing/subsidy rather than closure. CON repeated the redlining claim after admitting audits imperfect but never answered the comparative. Closing crystal of one-way bet vs shared responsibility gave cleaner decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1-B4.1 / B3.1 exchange: B turned CON's redlining objection into a comparison showing CON's preferred eligibility cutoff is more distortionary than partial, cohort-adjusted liability, and paired it with access-audit safeguards — a direct hit CON's R2 never resolved beyond asserting audits fail.
