### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides ran coherent, well-structured cases: PRO built a clean "expected value per total cost" framework and CON contested whether that metric alone should decide the motion, insisting that presence, field judgment, and infrastructure are irreducible parts of the science ledger. The debate turned on the Pressure Questions exchange: CON asked what infrastructure survives on residual funding, and PRO's honest answer (A2) conceded that crew-rated heavy lift and a Station-class lab would not be guaranteed, with "ambitious systems... deferred or retired." CON seized this admission in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, showing that PRO's own "focused" crewed remainder would be an orphaned capability without the shared architecture that makes crewed science possible at all—directly undercutting PRO's claim that selective human missions could still "win the comparison." PRO never repaired this hole (e.g., by pointing to cheaper commercial heavy-lift alternatives or a concrete way to preserve minimal shared infrastructure), instead retreating to the general principle that costly infrastructure shouldn't earn automatic priority. That principle is reasonable in the abstract, but it left the concession's practical bite unanswered. CON's closing crystallized this tension memorably ("fund the majority or lose the option... you cannot keep a classroom, retire the workplace"), giving the clearer, more resolved path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's own answer to the A2 pressure question (conceding that heavy lift and a Station-class lab would not be guaranteed under a minority crewed budget) into proof that PRO's "focused" crewed remainder is really an orphaned, non-functional capability.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's middle-ground position of keeping 'focused' human missions. By proving that space infrastructure is 'lumpy' and indivisible, A forced B to concede that heavy lift and space stations might be lost under B's budget. A then expertly used this concession to show that B's plan doesn't just reduce human spaceflight, but breaks its required industrial base and the political coalition that funds the agency overall.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal, where A seizes on B's concession regarding heavy lift to crystallize the 'lumpy' nature of space capabilities, proving that unbundling the architecture makes future human missions impossible.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's idealized budget reallocation. Side A's argument that human spaceflight requires a "lumpy" industrial base (heavy lift, life support, EVA) and a broad political coalition was highly persuasive and grounded in the practical realities of spaceflight. When Side B conceded in Rebuttal 2 that a minority budget would not guarantee crew-rated heavy lift or a Station-class laboratory, Side A rightly pointed out that this would destroy the very "focused" crewed missions Side B claimed to support. Side A's rhetoric was also sharper, memorably framing Side B's plan as merely photographing worlds we have dismantled the capacity to inhabit, while Side B struggled to defend a robotic portfolio that relied on uncosted political and infrastructural synergies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they capitalized on Side B's concession about deferring heavy lift and the Station to prove that Side B's "focused" crewed program would actually be an orphaned, non-functional capability lacking the infrastructure to fly.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): B built its case squarely on the motion's own stated metric (science per dollar) and used the Pressure Questions round decisively: A conceded outright that it could not name a single deep-space crewed mission that beats robotic alternatives on that metric [A4.3]. B then used that concession relentlessly in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, framing A's fallback appeals to "adaptive investigation," capability preservation, and rebuilding costs as an exemption invented after the concession rather than a genuine counter-metric. A's complement/capability arguments (Hubble servicing, indivisibility of programs, future option value) were thoughtful and correctly identified that PRO must justify "most" rather than merely showing robots win individual comparisons, but A never supplied a workable alternative threshold, a percentage, or a concrete example of crewed work outperforming robots — leaving its "most should stay crewed" position resting on unquantified hedges. B's closing crystallized this cleanly: complements can be preserved through minority funding (stations, physiology, hardware tests) without requiring majority allocation to a mode that loses on the metric the motion itself sets. A's rebuttals were competent and sharpened the burden question, but ultimately conceded the load-bearing factual claim and never recovered a concrete comparative win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question ("name the deep-space crewed missions that beat probes and telescopes on science per dollar—or admit you are defending presence") forced A's explicit concession in A4.3 that no such mission could be named; B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing then used that concession to reframe A's remaining arguments as post-hoc exemptions rather than a live comparative case.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B successfully leveraged the "science per dollar" metric to force a concession that deep-space crewed missions cannot compete on those terms. Side B then decisively neutralized Side A's "capability loss" argument by reframing it as a circular funding trap that inherently crowds out robotic fleets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4's response to the cadence argument, biting the bullet on ending viable crewed planetary exploration and arguing that a deferred generation of instruments outweighs an option preserved mainly by spending.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built a much stronger path to victory by tightly anchoring its case to the proposition's explicit metric ("science per dollar") and forcing Side A (CON) to concede that no deep-space crewed mission beats robotic probes on this measure. While CON offered a thoughtful defense of human adaptive capability and the risks of capability decay, CON failed to justify why this warrants retaining most of the crewed budget rather than making targeted cuts. PRO's rebuttals were exceptionally sharp, effectively neutralizing CON's examples (like Hubble) and cleanly handling the "rebuilding cost" argument by restricting it to retained capabilities. PRO's rhetoric was also more punchy and memorable, making the comparative stakes clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A2), where PRO directly answered CON's pressure question by biting the bullet on ending viable crewed planetary exploration and explaining why the rebuilding cost of a discarded capability shouldn't veto the budget shift, effectively dismantling CON's main defensive line.
