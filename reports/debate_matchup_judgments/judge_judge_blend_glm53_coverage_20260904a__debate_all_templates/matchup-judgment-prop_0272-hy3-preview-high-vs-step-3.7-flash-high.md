### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate. Side B had a plausible developmental case for sports, arts, and play, but leaned too heavily on the overbroad claim that these are necessary prerequisites for meaningful digital learning. Side A repeatedly and effectively distinguished casual home screen access from structured, career-aligned digital enrichment, and framed the motion as a real opportunity cost for underfunded schools. Side B never fully answered why digital programs with certifications, college prep, or design/coding training should be delayed for students who may already have collaboration and executive-function skills from other settings. Side A’s pressure questions were weak and partly misdirected, which narrows the margin, but its rebuttals did more decisive comparative work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest exchange was its answer to the prerequisite argument: it conceded that sports and arts can build useful skills, but argued that making them a mandatory sequencing condition for digital access is unjustified gatekeeping, especially for older low-income students needing career-linked digital training.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built the stronger case by maintaining that non-digital programs uniquely fill embodied skill and access gaps, directly answered pressure questions on equity and prerequisites, and showed why sequencing improves digital outcomes. Side B's counter that skills come from chores/jobs and digital is always the urgent gap remained less grounded and was never fully neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's direct A1/A2 answers plus rebuttal 2 comparison of hardware access vs real enrichment that damaged B's core equity claim.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by exposing PRO’s central prerequisite claim—that in-person sports, arts, and play are non-negotiable foundations for all digital learning—as an unsupported overgeneralization that functions as cruel gatekeeping. CON effectively showed that many low-income students already build executive function and collaboration through family responsibilities, part-time jobs, and rigorous academics, and that delaying career-critical, school-provided digital enrichment to force non-digital participation harms the very equity PRO claims to protect. CON’s equity reframing—that structured, credential-bearing digital training is the resource low-income students cannot access elsewhere, while non-digital programming is often available through community centers and local grants—survived PRO’s pressure and gave the cleaner comparative path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Reb2 dismantling of the prerequisite claim, arguing that delaying coding bootcamps until students complete intramural sports is baseless gatekeeping for students who already possess stronger executive function from supporting their families and taking AP classes.
