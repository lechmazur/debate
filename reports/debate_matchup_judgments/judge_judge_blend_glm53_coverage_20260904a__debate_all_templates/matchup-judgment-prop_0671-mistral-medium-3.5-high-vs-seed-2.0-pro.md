### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO built a coherent, plausible case for paper-based cognitive foundations (handwriting, attention, spatial memory) and maintained it consistently, but its equity rebuttal stayed largely conclusory: it repeated that "accommodations exist beyond devices" without engaging CON's sharper mechanism — that formal accommodations only reach children already identified, while tablets passively serve undiagnosed strugglers (dyslexia, slow processing) without requiring anyone to notice. CON's opening reframe (permanent home-advantage lock-in, "quiet six-year-olds" who never get formal support) was original and hit a real gap in PRO's case, and CON's pressure questions and answers stayed on that exact point, sharpening it each round ("habits built over thousands of small moments," the bike-riding analogy). PRO's answers to the pressure questions (A1/A2) asserted device navigation is "intuitive" and that alternative accommodations "exist," but never directly refuted why passive, unprompted tech support is inferior or replaceable for children nobody flags. CON's closing crystallized this unanswered asymmetry cleanly, while PRO's closing mostly recapped its foundational-skills thesis without closing the specific hole CON kept exploiting. That undressed vulnerability is why CON's route to victory remained live at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1/2 point that formal accommodations only help children already identified, while tablets offer automatic, no-diagnosis-needed support (read-aloud, adjustable text, immediate feedback) for the "quiet six-year-olds" no one notices — a mechanism PRO never squarely answered, only reasserting that non-digital accommodations "exist."
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A won by defending the actual device-light, paper-prioritizing policy and explaining why foundational literacy, attention, handwriting, and reasoning should come first. B offered compelling equity rhetoric but repeatedly treated device-light as three years of total exclusion, despite the motion not banning limited guided use or accessibility exceptions. B also leaned heavily on an unsupported exact claim about three undiagnosed children per accommodated child and overstated tablets as automatic intervention.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s first rebuttal most decisively exposed B’s false binary: later one-to-one access and a paper priority do not imply permanent digital exclusion, while accommodations can remain available. B never adequately repaired its assumption that the policy bans all early guided device practice.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON built the clearer path by showing the motion formalizes home advantage and abandons quiet undiagnosed kids; PRO’s foundational benefits and sequencing claims remained live but never neutralized the catch-up and silent-support pressure after full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 on digital habits as thousands of untaught low-stakes moments (bike analogy) that do not close in weeks of later tablet access.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): PRO built a clear, adaptive narrative: early-grade paper work builds irreversible executive-function/motor foundations, while digital operation skills can be acquired quickly later. Crucially, PRO turned CON's central equity argument by claiming low-income students benefit most from building foundational focus before receiving devices, and repeatedly challenged CON to name a single digital skill that must be learned at six rather than nine. CON never squarely answered that challenge in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, instead repeating the "digital divide" framing with only superficial variation. CON's fine-motor equivalence claim (tablet stylus = pencil) was asserted but not engaged with PRO's neural-development mechanism, and CON's closing failed to sharpen the clash further, largely recapping. PRO's admission under pressure ("no secret study") was an honest but weak evidentiary moment, yet CON did not exploit it decisively, spending its answer instead on restating equity concerns rather than pressing PRO's lack of grounding. Overall PRO progressed the debate more effectively, directly answered pressure, and left the decisive comparative question unresolved in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's inversion of the digital-divide argument in Rebuttal 1 (A2.1–A2.2), turning CON's equity claim into a reason to prioritize paper-first sequencing for disadvantaged students, followed by CON's failure to directly rebut that inversion in later rounds.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by framing the policy as sequencing rather than prohibition and by pressing Side B to explain why basic digital skills must be learned in the earliest grades. Side B never adequately established that later tablet access creates a permanent digital deficit and repeatedly treated a device-light policy as near-total denial. A's unsupported claims about exact learning times, universal district patterns, and irreversible neural windows limited the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest response was that later device availability preserves digital literacy while paper-first instruction builds attention and motor foundations, coupled with the challenge to identify an age-six digital skill that cannot be acquired later. Side B asserted compounding disadvantage but did not demonstrate why that delay would be permanent.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by locking onto one clean, live comparison that survived the full exchange: early paper builds non-retrofittable executive/motor pathways while every core digital skill can be mastered later; A never named a unique early-tablet skill that resists catch-up and never dislodged B’s sequencing answer to the digital-divide claim. A kept equity pressure live but undercut it with selective-test dismissal and overclaims about stylus equivalence; B’s weighing stayed sharper and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s R2/closing crystallizing that A identified zero early-only digital skills while foundations cannot be installed later
