### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): B won by showing that serious digital enrichment requires mentors, equipment, and school infrastructure rather than merely a phone, while A never established a sufficiently concrete stopping point for its priority rule. A preserved a meaningful health and safety case, but repeatedly portrayed optional digital programs as forced extra screen time and relied on the weak premise that digital opportunity is already broadly accessible. B also better explained inclusion, scalability, and the opportunity cost for low-income students. A's nonexistent C-reference IDs were an additional execution flaw.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal most decisively distinguished routine daytime Chromebook use from collaborative, mentored digital creation, then used A's own concession that digital programs scale cheaply to pressure the vague and potentially open-ended embodied baseline.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by making the live comparison easier to decide: what only school uniquely supplies after 3pm, and at what opportunity cost. PRO’s embodied-development and “you can’t download a field” equity story was strong early, but CON repeatedly forced the “before” sequencing into a practical trap—expensive per-child physical baselines that never cleanly finish, while credentialed digital access (mentors, licenses, portfolios) does not scale from asphalt or a cracked phone. PRO admitted digital scales cheaply yet still deferred it; CON’s inclusivity and day-vs-after-school distinctions stayed live and unanswered. PRO never fully neutralized the indefinite-block risk or the affluent-buy-both market point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.5–A4.7 / A5: PRO admits cheap-scaling digital tools yet would block labs until costly staffed fields/instruments are “more available,” leaving low-income kids with neither league nor lab.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively dismantling Side A's core equity premise. Side A argued that digital enrichment is easily accessible outside school while physical programs are not. Side B exposed the flaw in this by pointing out that affluent families dominate the private market for high-quality digital enrichment (coding camps, robotics), proving that mentored digital creation is also expensive and privatized. Side B also effectively weaponized the "before" condition of the prompt, demonstrating that Side A's physical baseline (staffing, insurance, facilities) is an ongoing, expensive commitment that could indefinitely block cheaper, highly inclusive digital labs. Side B's rhetorical framing and sharp rebuttals made the comparative choice much clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they expose the trap in Side A's "basic baseline" answer, showing that physical staffing and insurance costs scale per child and never finish, thus indefinitely blocking the cheap, inclusive digital lab for low-income students.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by establishing that movement, physical creation, and supervised social play provide scarce benefits that even excellent digital creation cannot substitute for. A also answered B’s sequencing objection by showing that making these activities more available can use existing gyms, blacktops, cafeterias, and adaptive formats rather than requiring stadium-scale investment. B compellingly distinguished creative technology from passive screen use, but exaggerated both the physical-program bottleneck and digital scalability while leaving A’s health, fatigue, and belonging comparison insufficiently answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s response to the scalability attack was decisive: the motion requires greater availability, not expensive varsity infrastructure, and unstructured play itself is one of the proposed goods. A then exposed tension between B’s claim of serving hundreds through personalized pacing and its reliance on mentored robotics teams for deep collaboration. B’s modular-pod answer reduced but did not eliminate that cost and staffing tension.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) won by locking the clash on scarcity after 3pm versus abundant screens, keeping sequencing as foundation rather than delay, and repeatedly exposing CON’s unresolved scale-vs-belonging contradiction. CON’s mobility and inclusion points stayed live but never cleanly neutralized the equity flip or the cheap existing-space framing of “more available.”
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Q1 plus B4/B5 follow-through: CON cannot simultaneously claim a lab for “hundreds with personalized pacing” and deep small-team robotics belonging at low cost like sports.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully exposing the logistical contradiction in Side B's scaling argument, pointing out that Side B could not simultaneously claim digital programs serve hundreds via personalized pacing and provide deep, small-team collaborative mentorship without high costs. Side A also won the equity clash by effectively reframing it around the daily reality of low-income students: screens are already abundant during the school day, while safe, supervised physical spaces and adult mentorship at 3 PM are scarce. Side B's macro-economic arguments were strong, but Side A's grounded focus on the immediate developmental and supervisory needs of the after-school window was more persuasive and better defended against pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side B's dual claim of mass scalability and deep collaborative belonging, pointing out that high-quality tech mentorship requires the same low ratios and resources that Side B claimed physical sports could not afford.
