### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases and executed the pressure-question format cleanly with proper A1/A2 responses. B's opening market-failure story and "stop undercutting at the source" framing was strong and consistently pressed, and its flip of the calibration trade-off ("a blunt national minimum misfires blindly, while a sectoral board can see thin margins") was a clever rhetorical move. However, A's "error control/blast radius" framing proved more resilient under exchange: A repeatedly forced B to concede that bands still require centralized political choices, and in Rebuttal 2 A landed a sharp, largely unanswered point that B's annual-cycle-plus-escalator model locks in raises during downturns rather than offering the agility B claimed. A also pressed an entry-barrier argument (compliance/classification costs favor incumbents) that B only partially answered ("scale no longer helps with wage-cutting") without fully engaging the compliance-cost asymmetry, and this thread was never revisited in B's closing. A's closing crystallized the clash cleanly around calibration error and "blast radius," while B's closing largely reasserted its framing without directly closing the escalator/downturn gap. The debate was close and both sides argued with real substance, but A's rebuttals did slightly more decisive damage to B's live routes to victory, giving A the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 point that B's "annual council with inflation escalator" is "automatic cost increases plus scheduled bargaining, regardless of demand shocks, bankruptcies, or regional downturns" directly undercut B's claim that sectoral boards offer superior real-time flexibility, and B's closing never squarely answered this critique.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): PRO won because its undercutting/race-to-the-bottom mechanism stayed live through the full exchange and gave the cleaner comparative reason to adopt the motion. CON pressed hard on calibration risk, fragile firms, and complexity, and was strong on error-control framing, but never fully neutralized the claim that a safe national floor plus after-the-fact credits leave wage-cutting rivals free to win work. PRO answered the pressure on updateability and simplicity with a coherent board model and repeatedly flipped CON’s small-business warnings: a known common floor protects decent low-margin firms more than open undercutting does. CON’s alternative package was real, but weaker as a source-level fix, so the decisive clash favored sectoral bargaining on balance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s sustained undercutting reply (esp. B2.5/B4.4 and B5.2–B5.4): credits and enforcement clean up after low-road competition; a common floor removes wage-cutting as the winning business model.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won by sustaining a live calibration-and-error-control critique throughout the debate. PRO's sectoral model requires centralized boards to price heterogeneous labor markets accurately across regions, skills, and seasons, but CON showed that banding multiplies classification burdens rather than eliminating them, and that annual council reviews with automatic escalators are too rigid for demand shocks and regional downturns. PRO's race-to-the-bottom narrative remained coherent, yet never squarely answered why concentrating wage-setting authority avoids the capture, delay, and error costs CON identified. CON's alternative—raising floors through a broad indexed minimum, enforcement, and targeted credits—accepts the same uncertainty without freezing entire labor-market architectures, giving the cleaner comparative reason to reject the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated demonstration that PRO's "floors with bands" multiply rather than solve calibration complexity, and that moving administration upstream into sector-wide rulemaking favors incumbents and invites lobbying, which PRO's institutional defense of annual councils and escalators failed to fully neutralize.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases and executed the format cleanly (proper A1/A2 answers, focused rebuttals). CON's decisive move was the "bind-and-hurt or don't-bind-and-redundant" dilemma, which forced PRO into either conceding that sectoral floors must be complex and differentiated (undermining the "just a floor" framing) or admitting they collapse into the same blunt instrument as a minimum wage. CON then converted PRO's own rescue plan—phase-ins, aligned reimbursement, training subsidies—into a "hidden tax" turn: PRO's design fixes for vulnerable firms amount to admitting sectoral bargaining doesn't create productivity, it just redistributes costs to taxpayers or forces closures, which PRO never fully answered. PRO's strongest surviving point was that a general minimum wage doesn't reach scheduling, overtime, and classification standards the motion explicitly covers, landed cleanly in its closing, but CON's closing chose to press the subsidy/capture line rather than engage it, leaving a live but narrower point for PRO. On balance, CON's rebuttals were sharper, more consistently exploited across rounds, and better withstood pressure, giving it a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's dilemma framing in Rebuttal 1 (A2.2)—"either sectoral boards set rates high enough to matter... or they set them so low and differentiated that they replicate the statutory minimum while adding bureaucracy"—combined with its Rebuttal 2 turn converting PRO's own subsidy/phase-in fixes into a "hidden tax" admission (A4.3), was the most damaging and sustained exchange of the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): A (CON) won a competitive debate by locking the clash on the bind-or-redundant dilemma and then showing B’s successive rescue tools—differentiation, phase-ins, reimbursement alignment, training/procurement support, transparent boards—concede job/price harm while multiplying complexity and incumbent capture. B kept a live advantage on fissuring and subcontracting, but never fully outweighed entry, poor-consumer price, and representation burdens under the motion’s on-balance test. A’s simpler baseline-plus-joint-employer alternative survived pressure more cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s bind-or-redundant dilemma (esp. A2.2/A4.2–A4.3 and closing): if sectoral floors bind they destroy marginal firms; if not they are redundant—and B’s design fixes read as admission of damage socialized onto taxpayers and shoppers.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON won by exposing a persistent workability trap: sectoral frameworks either bind and crush marginal firms, or they are so differentiated and low that they replicate a statutory minimum while adding crippling complexity. PRO’s answers required an ever-growing list of mitigations—differentiated minima, phase-ins, aligned reimbursements, training support, procurement rewrites—which effectively conceded the damage. CON crystallized this into a memorable “insider protection” frame, showing that one clear democratic floor protects workers without choosing winners, whereas sectoral boards decide who is allowed to exist. PRO kept the fissured-workplace problem alive, but never fully neutralized the capture and complexity costs, or the force of CON’s bind-or-redundant dilemma.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s bind-or-redundant dilemma and its application to PRO’s mitigation strategy: by forcing PRO to defend sectoral floors with an escalating list of carve-outs and subsidies, CON showed that the policy’s own fixes prove its harms, making the route to victory for PRO collapse under its own weight.
