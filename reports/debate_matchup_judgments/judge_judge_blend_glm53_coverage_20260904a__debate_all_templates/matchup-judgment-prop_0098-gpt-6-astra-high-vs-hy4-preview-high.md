### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that PRO’s safeguards grant deployment permission, not reliable detection or independent remediation of concealed algorithmic manipulation. CON’s “poisoned baseline” critique demonstrated that rollback to a compromised checkpoint restores rather than fixes the vulnerability, and that differential testing cannot by itself validate the incumbent model. CON also effectively neutralized PRO’s repeated divestment frame by distinguishing foreign ownership from enforceable inspection and modification rights. The decisive clash—whether the JV closes the realistic manipulation pathway—resolved in CON’s favor because PRO never established that its controls could reliably identify and remedy subtly distorted recommendations before harm occurs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s rebuttal 2 and closing, which exposed that PRO’s rollback and shadow-testing remedies assume a trustworthy baseline that PRO never establishes, and which cleanly separated technical accountability from ownership to defuse PRO’s divestment label.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): CON wins the adequacy half. PRO relies on deployment veto + rollback, but CON shows veto is permission not detection, and rollback assumes a clean baseline. PRO's shadow-testing answer measures a delta between current and prior model but cannot validate the baseline is clean or that benchmarks cover future manipulation, and guardrails only patch known symptoms. B's demand for provenance and usable remediation rights is not divestment, so PRO's "divestment relabeled" frame fails.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's permission ≠ detection distinction and poisoned-baseline attack on rollback, which PRO never fully answers despite detailing shadow testing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively dismantling Side A's core security mechanisms. Side A relied on deployment vetoes, differential shadow testing, and rollbacks to argue that algorithmic manipulation could be caught and reversed without ending Chinese ownership. Side B effectively countered that a veto grants permission, not detection, and that shadow testing only measures the delta between updates. If the baseline model is already subtly biased (the "poisoned baseline" problem), shadow testing will not catch it, and rolling back merely restores the vulnerability. Side A's attempt to dismiss this in the closing by claiming no licensing deal could ever meet Side B's standard effectively conceded the debate on the specific JV structure. Meanwhile, Side B successfully distinguished between retaining IP ownership (influence) and requiring technical inspection and remediation rights (security), perfectly threading the needle of the proposition. Side B's technical grounding and precise rebuttals made it the clear winner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of Side A's "differential shadow testing" mechanism in Rebuttal 2, pointing out that comparing an update against an already distorted baseline yields reassuring deltas while leaving the underlying manipulation intact.
