### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): Side B won on the decisive clash. CON's core argument—that conditional approvals with enforceable clawback, escrow, and offtake provisions actually preserve more leverage than a default block—survived the exchange in a way PRO's core argument did not. PRO's claim that state-directed firms override commercial logic to make conditional tools unenforceable was turned back against it effectively: if commercial incentives are truly irrelevant, then the presumptive block also fails to stop embargo or nationalization of refined output, which rivals can exercise regardless of mine ownership. PRO's answer (blocking prevents new footholds from forming) is a genuine partial response but leaves two unresolved burdens: CON's sequencing argument (blocking mines starves domestic refineries of feedstock certainty needed to finance construction) and the embargo-regardless-of-ownership point. PRO never offered a specific mechanism showing how allied capital would fill the gap at the required pace, while CON's conditional-approval framework was grounded in specific tools (escrow, mandatory third-party offtake, operational veto). PRO's closing was notably short and mostly repetitive, leaving CON's strongest material unanswered on the page. CON's closing crystallized all three pillars cleanly. Both sides maintained epistemic discipline without fake citations, but CON's causal reasoning was more specific and more systematically applied to the live clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1 and its follow-through in Rebuttal 2 constituted the most decisive exchange. CON argued that blocking acquisition does not reduce a rival state's embargo capacity—rival states can withhold refined exports regardless of mine ownership—so the presumption strips us of contractual hooks and audit access while leaving the embargo threat fully intact. PRO's answer (the block prevents new footholds, narrowing seizure targets) was a partial response, but it left CON's embargo-regardless-of-ownership point and the refinery-sequencing problem standing, and PRO never explained why conditional approvals could not deliver the same foothold-limiting effect through enforceable governance conditions.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A established a plausible national-security rationale: rival-state-tied ownership creates hard-to-reverse leverage and contractual safeguards may fail in crisis. But Side B made the stronger comparative case that a presumptive block can worsen the very dependency it targets by delaying mine development, starving domestic refining of feedstock, and forfeiting conditional governance tools. A mostly answered with assertions that allied capital would replace rival capital and that delays would be temporary, but did not sufficiently prove that transition mechanism. B’s sequencing argument and less-restrictive-alternative framing were more concrete and better weighed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s strongest exchange was the response that if rival states can override commercial contracts, ownership bans alone do not solve embargo or processing leverage, while if commercial incentives still matter, conditional approvals with offtake, audit, and clawback rights are preferable to a blunt presumption.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): Side B wins by successfully turning Side A’s core premise into a double-bind and exposing a critical sequencing flaw in the decoupling strategy. When A argued that rival states override commercial contracts during crises, B countered that if contracts are useless, a presumptive block also fails to stop embargoes while sacrificing visibility; if contracts work, conditional approvals are superior. More decisively, B’s sequencing argument—that domestic refineries cannot secure financing without guaranteed upstream feedstock, which a presumptive block would starve—materially undercut A’s claim that blocking rival capital naturally spurs allied redundancy. A responded competently but relied on optimistic assumptions about allied capital redirection that never fully answered B’s timeline and bankability pressure. B’s framing of isolation vs. enforceable integration crystallized the clash more persuasively for a third-party reader.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over contractual governance vs. state override (A3.1/A4.1 vs B2.3/B4.1). B effectively trapped A’s premise: if geopolitical directives render contracts unenforceable, they also render ownership blocks irrelevant to embargo risks, making the presumption costly but ineffective. B’s pivot to retained clawbacks, escrow, and mandatory offtake as conditional tools offered a more nuanced, workable mechanism that directly answered A’s security concern without conceding the supply chain.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by building and sustaining a coherent structural argument that Side B never squarely neutralized. The civil-military fusion/sovereign immunity claim—that commercial conditions, audit rights, and board majorities evaporate the moment a rival state invokes emergency powers—was introduced early, sharpened through pressure questions, and remained live through the closing. Side B's most serious attempt to answer it (pre-arranged stockpiling and neutral-party redundancies operating independently of board control) was plausible but vague, and never explained how those mechanisms survive a sovereign emergency override any better than ordinary contract terms do. Side A's pressure question exposed this directly: B's Q1 conceded the relevant moment is a "formal emergency," which is precisely when A argues the safeguards collapse. B's A1 in Rebuttal 2 responded with structural redundancies "activated by neutral parties" but offered no concrete mechanism for why those parties could resist a state command that by B's own framing had already overridden board authority. The "negative capacity" framing—that rival-tied capacity appears as supply but functions as a chokehold—was effective and B never developed a clean counter-weighing. B's strongest ground was capacity reduction and regulatory chill, and these were real tensions, but A's response that redirecting sponsors to non-rival jurisdictions purifies the capital pool, and abandoning projects reveals hidden state subsidies, held up under scrutiny. B's retaliation argument was partially effective but somewhat repetitive across rounds. A's closing crystallized the fatal asymmetry cleanly: leverage is permanent, safeguards are conditional. B's closing was shorter and less sharp, restating the diversification point without advancing the weighing. Execution costs were minor on both sides; both used A1/A2 and Q1/Q2 formatting correctly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.4) directly answering B's Q1: the argument that audit rights and alternative sourcing plans are meaningless precisely at the crisis moment when civil-military fusion statutes activate, combined with sovereign immunity preventing enforcement, cleanly collapsed the central premise of B's "targeted review with safeguards" alternative. B never recovered from this in the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by making the central risk comparison cleaner: critical-mineral capacity controlled by firms closely tied to rival states is not secure capacity but latent coercive leverage. Side B had a real and competitive diversification argument, but it often leaned on overbroad versions of the motion and never fully answered why contractual safeguards would survive a crisis in which the rival state commands the firm. A better tied the presumption to asymmetric information, irreversible dependence, and the limits of ex post enforcement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was against B's targeted-safeguards argument: A argued that board controls, audits, and supply clauses fail precisely when sovereign crisis commands matter most, turning B's monitored-acquisition model into procedural comfort rather than real security.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side B wins by successfully adapting to Side A's core pressure regarding sovereign override. While A powerfully argued that contractual safeguards evaporate during crises, B pivoted in Rebuttal 2 to structural redundancies (third-party stockpiling, independent supply routes) that function regardless of corporate control. B also won the macro-weighing on supply chain resilience, demonstrating that a presumptive block entrenches existing monopolies and slows diversification, whereas monitored acquisitions dilute rival market share and create additive security. A's dismissal of capital flight as merely "purifying the pool" failed to address the resulting domestic supply gap, leaving B's comparative framework more complete and actionable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 answer to Q1, shifting from contractual board control to physical/structural redundancies to neutralize A's sovereign override critique, combined with the "additive security" weighing that reframed diversification as the decisive metric.
