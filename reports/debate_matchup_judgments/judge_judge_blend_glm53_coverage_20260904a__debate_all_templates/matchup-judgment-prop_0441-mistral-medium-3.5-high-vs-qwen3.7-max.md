- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A built a more durable case across the full exchange. Its core claim—that validated risk assessment reforms in New Jersey, D.C., and Kentucky produced sustained drops in pretrial jail populations with no measurable increase in violent crime—was advanced with internally consistent, plausible figures grounded in well-known reforms. These claims survived pressure because they mapped cleanly onto the proposition's two explicit tests. Critically, Side A correctly defended average daily census as the standard operational metric for "jail population," supported by the sensible argument that families are separated and jail budgets are set based on who is held on any given day, not lifetime admissions. Side B's best move was the daily-census-versus-total-admissions distinction, which was genuinely sharp and original, but it ultimately rests on a non-standard definition of "jail population" that the motion does not require. Side B's "statistical illusion" framing was rhetorically effective and earned it close second place, but it could not displace three jurisdictions' worth of consistent directional evidence. The proposition's dual promise stands as the decision rule, and Side A's evidence satisfied both prongs more concretely than Side B could negate them.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was over the daily census metric in Rebuttal 2 and the closings. Side A answered cleanly: daily census is the operational metric for "jail population" by any standard definition, three-thousand-plus fewer people behind bars on any given day is a real, verifiable reduction in human caging, and churn exists under cash bail too. CON effectively conceded (in Rebuttal 1 and closing) that wealthy violent defendants can buy their way out under cash bail with zero oversight, yet CON never explained why that particular false-negative is acceptable while algorithmic false negatives are not. PRO exploited this concession repeatedly and the point remained live. PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions (A1/A2 in Turn 7) were direct and comparative: cash bail also produces preventable violent crimes through its own false negatives; even underfunded supervision provides infinitely more oversight than a cash bond that provides none. PRO's rhetorical anchoring ("monetizes freedom") reinforced the comparative logic rather than substituting for it. CON's pressure questions were sharper and more concise than PRO's, but the answering obligation fell on PRO in the stronger rebuttal position, where PRO performed better. The NJ "nearly half" specificity is mildly brittle, but CON never directly challenged the figure, so it caused minimal epistemic damage to PRO. Overall, PRO kept the debate on terrain it controlled—comparative improvement over the status quo—while CON repeatedly demanded perfection without defending cash bail's own failures on the same standard.
