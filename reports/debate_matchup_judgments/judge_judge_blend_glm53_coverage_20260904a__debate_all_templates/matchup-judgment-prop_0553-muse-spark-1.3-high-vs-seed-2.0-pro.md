### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A anchored the debate to the actual text of the motion — access to parts, tools, documentation, with no ban on compatible parts or repairer licensing — and used that anchor effectively to dismantle B's opening "criminalisation" framing. When B pivoted to a subtler "weaponisation via marketing/legitimacy" theory, A's strongest counter (price, speed, and proximity beat legal branding for a farmer who cannot part with his lifeline phone for weeks) went largely unanswered in substance; B's Rebuttal 2 mostly reasserted the same claim ("he will lose customers") and, when pressed on why safeguards couldn't cure the pricing/marketing concern, retreated to an unfalsifiable claim that regulatory capture "always happens," which is more assertion than grounded argument. B's car-mechanic analogy was a good original move, but A's distinction (capital/tooling/immobilisers vs. a withheld PDF and paired parts) blunted it without a real rejoinder from B. B's grey-market "four in ten" figure also verges on brittle fake precision. A's closing crystallized the clash cleanly; B's closing largely repeated its thesis rather than resolving the live economic-incentive counter.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question ("if your fear is pricing/marketing, why kill the whole right instead of adding safeguards?") combined with A4's farmer example (price/speed/proximity beat legal branding) was the decisive exchange; B's answer that safeguards "will never survive the drafting process" conceded the theoretical case while offering only a speculative political-capture claim in its place.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A won a competitive debate by keeping the clash on the motion as written: access without bans or licensing. A forced B to rest the case almost entirely on speculative weaponization and worst-case drafting, then answered pressure directly with price/proximity incentives and the phones-vs-cars barrier distinction. B was sharper and more original on political-economy risks, but never fully showed why an added option must erase the bazaar rather than coexist with it; that left A with the cleaner live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 and later A2 on cars: the motion invents no ban/licence, and phone barriers are withheld manuals/pairing rather than capital-scale car repair lockout—so access arms the informal tech instead of killing him.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by grounding the debate deeply in the Indian context (UPI, local bazaars, extreme price sensitivity) and decisively neutralizing Side B's main offensive. Side A's rebuttal of the car analogy—pointing out that modern auto repair requires heavy physical and financial infrastructure while phone repair merely needs digital access (PDFs, software unlocks)—was the turning point. Side A's weighing mechanism that "price and proximity beat paperwork" effectively countered Side B's cynical implementation and regulatory capture arguments, making the comparative choice much clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's car analogy, distinguishing between the heavy physical/financial infrastructure needed for modern auto repair versus the simple digital access (PDFs, software unlocks) needed for phones, which completely deflated Side B's strongest comparative example.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON's rebuttal work was sharper and more comparative. CON directly turned PRO's "status quo is already bad" framing by arguing the law changes the distribution of risk rather than just its magnitude—especially on counterfeit quality (disclosure lets clones close the quality gap while keeping the price advantage) and on liability (a mandated manual doesn't transfer competence, fire suppression, or insurance). PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer that "liability remains exactly where it stands right now: with the person performing the work" was a costly concession that CON seized on in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing, reframing the debate as "state-encouraged risk-shifting" and "abandonment." PRO's closing reasserted the general "already broken, can only improve" theme but never directly neutralized this liability-shift argument or the counterfeit-quality inversion, leaving CON's strongest line of attack live and unresolved. CON also exposed a real tension in PRO's IP carveout answer (it either exempts domestic firms who rely on licensed foreign chipsets or leaves foreign importers unpoliced), which PRO's "standard provision" answer did not fully resolve. PRO's environmental/e-waste and livelihoods arguments remained comparatively under-pressured, but CON's more decisive rebuttals on safety, counterfeits, and liability carried more weight because they attacked the mechanism of PRO's central causal story rather than just asserting bland safety fears.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over A4.2's concession that "liability remains exactly where it stands right now: with the person performing the work" and CON's Closing quotation of it as proof of "state-encouraged risk-shifting" was the most decisive moment—PRO never adequately answered why a mandated manual without mandated competence or insurance improves outcomes rather than merely multiplying risky attempts.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): CON won by converting PRO’s cleanest concessions into a decisive comparison: a mandate that multiplies DIY/attempt risk while leaving liability on households, plus better counterfeits and uneven Make-in-India burdens. PRO’s status-quo-is-already-bad case was strong and kept the debate live, but never fully proved net improvement once confidence-without-competence and the IP/enforcement carveout problems were pressed. CON’s liability-split frame made the vote easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s use of PRO’s A1/A4 answer that manufacturers don’t pay and families keep full fire liability, reframed as risk-shifting rather than safer repair.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B built a much stronger causal case, particularly on how right-to-repair mandates improve counterfeit quality and shift financial liability to consumers. Side A fell into a fatal trap during the pressure questions by bluntly conceding that manufacturers bear no liability for home repair fires. Side B ruthlessly and eloquently exploited this concession, framing the law as state-encouraged risk-shifting that provides "confidence without competence." Side B's superior grasp of the Indian market context and devastating closing rhetoric made the decision clear.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's exploitation of Side A's liability concession in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, crystallizing the argument that a mandated PDF transfers confidence but not a fire cabinet or insurance, effectively neutralizing Side A's safety claims.
