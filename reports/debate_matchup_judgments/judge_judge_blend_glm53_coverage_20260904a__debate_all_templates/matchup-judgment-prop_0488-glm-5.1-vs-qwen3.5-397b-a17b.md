### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): Side A won by building a more coherent, better-defended path to the motion's core question. The decisive clash was definitional—PRO correctly identified that resilience means absorbing diverse shocks, while CON's definition (delivering one solution at scale) is actually a description of efficiency, not resilience. CON conflated scale with extreme concentration throughout the debate and never adequately answered PRO's central challenge: why must exactly three firms control the market rather than ten well-capitalized competitors? The "scale PLUS redundancy vs. scale MINUS backup" framing in PRO's Rebuttal 2 crystallized this gap clearly and CON never closed it. CON's strongest move—the asymmetric risk argument in the closing—was blunted by the fact that the current concentrated system already creates catastrophic genetic monoculture risk, a live point from PRO's opening that CON never fully neutralized. CON's own concession in Rebuttal 2 that portfolio narrowing is "risk management" inadvertently validated PRO's fragility narrative. The AT&T divestiture analogy directly answered CON's R&D pipeline concern; CON's counter ("we're debating biological systems, not telecom") acknowledged the distinction but did not show why the capital dispersal logic fails in agriculture specifically. Both sides met the format requirements including A1/A2 labeling. PRO's pressure questions were sharper and more targeted than CON's; PRO's answers in Rebuttal 2 were more direct. CON's shorter closing left real burdens unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to its own Q1 (the AT&T divestiture analogy): divested units retain germplasm, breeding teams, and patents, so competitive dispersal does not destroy R&D capacity—it redirects it away from rent-seeking toward differentiation. CON's response that "we aren't debating telecom" was a legitimate distinction but did not explain why the capital-dispersal logic fails specifically in agriculture, leaving the core challenge unanswered.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B won by making the central distinction clearer: scale may be useful, but extreme concentration creates single points of failure, genetic narrowing, bundled dependency, and weak redundancy. Side A had a serious capacity argument about research, logistics, and transition risk, but it mostly asserted that only the current handful can sustain those functions and never convincingly answered why several large competitors plus public breeding could not preserve scale while adding resilience. Side B also turned Side A's defense of portfolio narrowing into evidence of fragility.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's repeated challenge that Side A conflated necessary scale with extreme concentration was decisive, especially the unanswered question of why three dominant firms are uniquely viable while ten well-capitalized competitors would not provide both innovation and redundancy.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B won by successfully decoupling "scale" from "extreme concentration" and brilliantly turning Side A's defense of "portfolio narrowing" into proof of structural fragility.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Closing weaponized Side A's Rebuttal 2 argument, showing that what Side A calls "risk management" (narrowing genetic deployment) is precisely the single point of failure that destroys resilience.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): CON won on the two most live clashes by the end of the exchange. First, the capacity argument: PRO repeatedly asserted that fragmentation creates "parallel capacity" but never identified where that capacity comes from mechanistically. CON's point that seed production requires months of lead time and dedicated infrastructure went materially unanswered—PRO's "A2" in Rebuttal 2 just restated the assertion. Second, the incentive-identity argument: CON's pressure question pointing out that smaller firms face identical farmer-demand incentives was PRO's weakest moment; PRO responded with "monopolies manufacture demand," a bare assertion with no mechanism. CON's follow-up that if herbicide tolerance pays, any firm—large or small—chases it remained live. CON also handled PRO's Q1 better, directly rebutting the "only producer" premise by noting major firms maintain multiple production facilities across continents and regional players still operate alongside them. PRO's strongest living argument—genetic synchronization risk from narrow IP-protected germplasm—was plausible but CON's counter (IP covers trait inserts, not full genetic bases; firms make independent germplasm decisions) was at minimum a draw. PRO's efficiency-vs.-survival framing and the fleet analogy were rhetorically effective, but CON's "distributed vulnerability" counter and the "more letterheads, not more resilience" closing crystallized the clash more sharply. The truncation in CON's opening was a minor execution cost but didn't damage any substantive argument. CON consistently made the debate easier to decide by showing that "more firms" does not automatically mean "more resilience" when the firms lack surplus capacity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question Q1 (B3.1)—pointing out that smaller firms face identical farmer-demand incentives for herbicide tolerance, so corporate structure alone cannot redirect innovation—was the most decisive exchange. PRO's answer invoked "monopolies manufacture demand" without a supporting mechanism, and CON's Rebuttal 2 follow-up (B4.4) closed the loop, leaving PRO's core innovation-incentive argument materially damaged and unanswered.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): Side A won by making the central mechanism clearer: resilience depends on deep R&D pipelines, seed multiplication lead times, surplus production, and logistics capacity, not merely the number of firms. Side B had an intuitive single-point-of-failure case, but repeatedly asserted that fragmentation creates parallel capacity without explaining where that capacity comes from or how smaller firms handle the capital-intensive breeding and crisis-response burdens. Side A also effectively neutralized the lock-in argument by arguing farmer demand and scale-driven innovation would persist after breakup.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to the “competitors can ramp up” claim was decisive: seed production requires seasons, infrastructure, and regional adaptation, so fragmented firms cannot simply replace a failed supplier mid-crisis. That directly undercut Side B’s main redundancy route.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): Side A systematically dismantled B's causal links by grounding their arguments in the physical and economic realities of agriculture. A's explanation of seed multiplication timelines proved that shock absorption requires surplus capacity, which relies on scale. A also expertly exposed the contradictions in B's proposed solutions, noting that smaller firms cannot license traits if the large R&D operations creating them are broken up.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal dismantled B's innovation argument by pointing out that smaller firms licensing traits relies entirely on the massive R&D scale B is trying to break up, completely trapping PRO.
