- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): PRO won primarily by anchoring the debate in a compelling systemic frame—the race to the bottom and the corporate welfare subsidy—that CON never fully neutralized. PRO's core claim that large corporations currently weaponize poverty wages against both workers and smaller competitors remained live throughout, while CON's responses focused on distributional edge cases rather than the aggregate harm. PRO's second pressure question—asking what structural mechanism prevents employers from extracting pay discounts while still imposing rigid scheduling—was the most effective blow of the debate, exposing that the individual pay-for-flexibility bargain CON defended is structurally unenforceable without collective leverage. CON's Rebuttal 2 answer acknowledged the power imbalance but then argued that sectoral bargaining "bans tailored deviations," which sidesteps the core problem: those deviations are already worthless without enforcement power. CON did carry real live arguments—the insider/outsider distributional concern about union negotiators pricing out part-time and gig workers is genuine and PRO's "consultation mechanisms" answer was vague—and the rural marginal-business example was concrete and effective. But CON's alternative solutions (targeted minimum wages, portable benefits, stronger enforcement) were repeatedly invoked without sufficient development, leaving PRO's corporate welfare framing largely uncontested in the closing rounds. On balance, PRO's arguments covered more systemic terrain, PRO's pressure question did more damage, and the key flexibility clash resolved in PRO's favor after the full exchange.

CON's Rebuttal 2 response admitted the power imbalance but pivoted to arguing that sectoral rules "ban the very tailored deviations" workers use to negotiate—which doesn't answer PRO's challenge that those deviations are already illusory. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but CON edged PRO through the full exchange for three reasons. First, the Hartz reforms rebuttal [A2.3]—that Germany survived only by adding opening clauses and decentralizing, i.e., reintroducing the enterprise-level flexibility CON defends—was the single most damaging specific counter in the debate and went unanswered across PRO's remaining speeches. PRO's Germany/Nordics pillar was never fully restored. Second, CON's insider/outsider framing ran consistently from opening through closing and provided a coherent comparative lens: sectoral standards protect the insulated worker at the cost of pricing out the most vulnerable. PRO acknowledged the equity problem but never neutralized the specific mechanism CON identified. Third, PRO's grounding weakened in Rebuttal 2: the Dutch supermajority claim and the "roughly 1 in 8" informal-worker figure both carry suspicious specificity for a closed-book debate, diluting PRO's credibility precisely when it needed to answer CON's sharp shadow-employment question cleanly. PRO also suffered two clipped speeches (Rebuttal 1 and Closing), which are real execution costs and prevented full crystallization of the closing narrative. CON's alternative—a regularly updated statutory minimum indexed to regional costs, combined with enterprise bargaining—remained a cleaner, less contested path throughout. The margin is real but not wide: PRO's narrative about enterprise bargaining failing low-wage workers is compelling and well-grounded, and CON's own answer to PRO's Q2 on retraining was assertive without much support. This is a competitive debate decided primarily by the Hartz rebuttal and PRO's format penalties.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's [A2.3] point that Germany required the Hartz reforms and widespread "opening clauses" precisely because rigid sectoral bargaining caused structural unemployment—meaning Germany survived by reintroducing the enterprise-level flexibility CON defends—directly undercut PRO's central real-world example and was never directly answered in any subsequent PRO speech.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by more effectively neutralizing PRO's Germany/Nordics examples via Hartz reforms, exposing admin burdens on small firms, and maintaining focus on vulnerable outsiders being priced out, while answering pressure questions directly and weighing dynamism vs cartelization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal 1 on absolute (vs relative) automation costs and Hartz decentralization counter to sectoral examples.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A built the stronger comparative case by grounding sectoral bargaining in concrete, observable failures of the current enterprise-only system—wage suppression in fragmented industries, exploitation of low-leverage workers, and endemic off-books labor—while offering specific, plausible mechanisms (Dutch supermajority bargaining tables, carve-outs, standardized enforcement) to answer CON’s theoretical concerns. PRO’s direct, causal answers to CON’s pressure questions on small-business capture and shadow employment contrasted sharply with CON’s less grounded replies to PRO’s pressure, where CON largely reasserted a wish-cast of “robust statutory minima” rather than explaining how enterprise bargaining itself solves living-wage gaps. While CON landed strong theoretical blows on automation and the Hartz reforms, these were not decisive enough to overcome PRO’s real-world referents, clearer empirical path, and superior handling of the decisive institutional clash. Execution costs from PRO’s clipped speeches narrow the margin but do not flip the result.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s reframing of shadow employment in Rebuttal 2, arguing that informal labor is a status-quo pathology driven by unregulated competition and that standardized sector-wide costs eliminate the competitive incentive to hire off-books, directly neutralized CON’s pressure question and became a live route to victory that CON never fully recovered.
