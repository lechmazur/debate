### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A (CON) built and sustained the sharper path to victory. Its medical-tourism turn — a domestic ban restricts only those who cannot afford to travel, so it worsens the very inequality PRO invoked — was the debate's decisive argument. PRO's best answer (keeping the practice marginal prevents normalization) was partially responsive but never showed how stratification is actually arrested when affluent embryos are still selected abroad and the resulting children compete domestically. That gap was open from A1 through the closing and was never closed. CON also successfully sustained the unstable-regulatory-line argument: PRO answered with "clinical consensus and democratic oversight," but this is exactly the shifting administrative discretion CON warned about, and the rejoinder never resolved the core concern. PRO's "dialogical vs. monological" distinction was rhetorically vivid and generated the debate's most interesting exchange, but CON's counter — pre-birth selection overrides no existing person's will, so the consent framing actually favors selection over post-birth imposition — materially damaged the distinction rather than just trading slogans. PRO's policy kept being framed as a regressive tax on reproductive freedom that fails its own stated goal, and the CON closing crystallized that framing cleanly without relying on new material. The clipping of A4 carries a small execution cost but the core arguments had been made, and the closing compensated effectively. PRO ran a competent, structured case with genuine rhetorical moments, but was repeatedly reactive and never neutralized CON's central turnback argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's sustained medical-tourism turn: PRO claimed restriction prevents biological stratification, but CON showed across R1, R2, and the closing that affluent parents will select embryos abroad regardless, their children will compete domestically, and a domestic ban therefore merely strips access from middle- and working-class families — worsening the exact harm PRO cited. PRO never produced a clean answer showing how the stratification is actually prevented rather than relocated.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by making the policy test sharper: legal restriction may not stop wealthy access, may worsen unequal access through medical tourism, and requires an unstable disease-versus-enhancement line over intimate reproductive choices. Side A had strong dignity and normalization arguments, but relied more on asserted inevitability of caste and arms race, while B more directly challenged whether the proposed restriction actually solves those harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's turn on medical tourism was the decisive exchange: A answered that restriction prevents normalization, but B persuasively pressed that the stratification harm persists for wealthy users abroad while the ban mainly burdens those without travel access.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON successfully turned PRO's central inequality argument by showing that a domestic ban would be circumvented via medical tourism, restricting only non-wealthy families and worsening the very biological stratification PRO sought to prevent. CON also demonstrated that the disease-enhancement boundary PRO needed was unstable and would invite arbitrary state power over intimate choices, and that PRO's "coercive arms race" argument proved too much. PRO's commodification concerns remained vivid but could not overcome the policy's self-defeating structure and speculative downstream harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's medical tourism turn, demonstrating that the ban acts as a regressive tax on reproductive freedom while failing to prevent the genetic stratification it claims to solve.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B built a cleaner and more decisive path to victory on three fronts that A never neutralized. First, the non-identity argument in B's first rebuttal — selecting which child exists versus optimizing an existing one — was philosophically sharp and A's answer ("the child retains full agency") simply talked past it. Second, B's positional good argument in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive move in the debate: tall or high-scoring embryos are valuable only because other embryos are shorter or lower-scoring, so equitable distribution is a logical impossibility. A responded in closing only by asserting "these are not zero-sum positional goods" with no supporting reasoning, leaving B's framing standing. Third, B directly and repeatedly engaged the motion's distinctive clause — "even if the technology becomes more reliable" — arguing that reliability makes the sorting cleaner and therefore makes the caste objection stronger, not weaker. A largely argued past this clause. A did score some genuine points: the line-drawing problem between disease and non-disease traits, and the fertility tourism concern, were real pressures. B's response to fertility tourism (coordinated refusal rather than domestic surrender) is contestable but adequate. The disease/non-disease boundary argument was answered by B with the observation that sex-selection bans and germline-editing restrictions already draw similar lines without collapsing into totalitarianism. B's Q1 trap was particularly effective, exposing an internal contradiction in A's open-future argument, and A's answer ("parents pay for probabilities") resolved neither horn of the dilemma. B's Rebuttal 2 being clipped is a mild execution cost, but the core arguments were preserved before the clip. Overall, B's arguments were more original, more directly tied to the motion, and left the more important clashes in their favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal attacking the tutoring analogy via the non-identity point: "A tutor helps an existing child develop … embryo screening does the opposite: it selects which child exists." Combined with B's Q1 — "if the future is truly unaffected, why would any parent pay for it?" — this trapped A in a contradiction it never escaped. A's response that the child "retains full agency" simply fails to engage which child is selected, leaving B's distinction between optimizing an existing person and choosing which person to bring into existence as the live and unanswered philosophical divide in the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate. B’s autonomy and fertility-tourism arguments were plausible, but A more directly separated embryo selection from post-birth optimization, answered line-drawing by noting that fuzzy boundaries do not erase clear non-disease cases, and crystallized the decisive inequality issue: a regulated market in ranked embryos would legitimize and scale positional genetic hierarchy rather than make it equitable. B repeated offshore access and subsidies but underanswered why reliable sorting for height, appearance, and cognition should be socially permitted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest exchange was against B’s regulated-equity answer: A argued that distributing access to ranked embryos merely administers the caste system because the good is positional, while B’s reply shifted toward health and baseline benefits without fully answering appearance, height, and comparative cognition.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B won because it established that screening for non-disease traits creates a positional, hereditary caste system that regulation cannot render equitable, a structural claim Side A never squarely answered despite repeated pressure. Side B also landed a decisive early rebuttal against the tutoring analogy by distinguishing selection of embryos from shaping existing children, and it answered pressure questions more directly. While Side B’s second rebuttal was clipped for exceeding the word limit—a real execution cost—its core framework survived intact and was crystallized effectively in the closing, whereas Side A’s closing merely restated assertions and mischaracterized the opposition as requiring a global ban.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s first rebuttal, which demonstrated that tutoring shapes an existing child who can still rebel and forge an unexpected future, while embryo screening selects which child exists, thereby collapsing Side A’s central analogy and moral equivalence.
