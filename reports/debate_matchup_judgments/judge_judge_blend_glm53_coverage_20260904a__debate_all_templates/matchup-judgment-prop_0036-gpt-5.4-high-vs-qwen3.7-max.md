### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won the decisive clash at the core of the motion. The turning point came when CON, answering B's Q1 in Rebuttal 2, explicitly called paying more for refusing surveillance "the baseline cost of privacy" and compared it to choosing a high deductible. PRO's closing correctly seized on this admission: in health and life insurance—a gatekeeper to care and family security, not a consumer app—attaching a financial price to keeping intimate behavioral data private is functionally coercive, not a neutral trade-off. That reframe held because CON never gave a principled reason why the insurance context should be treated like ordinary retail. CON's strongest line—that wearables replace crude proxies like zip codes and BMI with objective physiological reality—had real force throughout, but PRO dismantled the false dilemma it rested on: the motion does not endorse legacy proxies, and PRO's rule does not prohibit them; it only blocks wearable-based pricing and coverage decisions absent genuine opt-in. CON never squared that. PRO also effectively exposed CON's internal tension: CON claimed wearables are "overwhelmingly" used for discounts and rewards, yet also argued they must be used for baseline pricing or their underwriting value is "neutered." That contradiction confirmed PRO's framing—the power CON was actually defending was the power to penalize refusal, not to enable voluntary wellness. Two clipping events on PRO's speeches were minor execution costs because the core arguments in both speeches landed before the cuts. CON was repetitive across rounds (adverse selection, zip code comparison, healthy-leaving-the-market) without sharpening under pressure, while PRO adapted, narrowed the clash, and crystallized it cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange ran across PRO's Q1 pressure question ("If refusing surveillance makes coverage less affordable, what makes that consent rather than coercion?") and CON's Rebuttal 2 answer ("the baseline cost of privacy…a standard financial trade-off"). PRO's closing then used CON's own language to crystallize why insurance is categorically different from retail: once refusal means a worse price or worse coverage in a market that guards access to care, privacy is no longer a genuine choice. CON never recovered from having conceded the coercive mechanism in plain terms.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the debate around the coercive nature of offering lower premiums for surveillance in a necessary market. Side B effectively cornered Side A into admitting that their model requires making insurance more expensive for those who refuse tracking, which Side B persuasively characterized as punishing privacy rather than a neutral actuarial choice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly neutralized Side A's defense of "discounts" by highlighting that the motion already allows voluntary wellness programs, forcing Side A to explicitly defend penalizing non-sharers.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won because its core coercion argument survived intact and was sharpened against B's "baseline pricing" defense, while A consistently preserved the motion's allowance for genuine opt-in programs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.4/A4.3/A5.1 directly neutralized B's discounts-versus-surcharge distinction and turned B's own closing language ("baseline cost of privacy") into decisive evidence of coercion in essential insurance.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins on the most decisive logical clash in the debate. After PRO conceded in its A1 answer that "consent does not magically cure bias; it cures coercion," CON immediately turned that concession into a structural attack on the whole motion: if pre-clearance validation is what makes a metric fair, then "opt-in" is neither necessary when a metric is valid nor sufficient when one is biased. CON carried this argument all the way through the closing—"The only unique effect of their rule is to block people from using better evidence to escape blunter labels"—and PRO never fully answered it. PRO's closing response that "strict limits mandate pre-deployment validation" conflates the mechanism (opt-in) with the outcome (validated metrics) in exactly the way CON predicted. CON also consistently demonstrated that targeted rules—no denial for refusal, capped premium differentials, validated metrics, audits—can achieve every protection PRO claimed for the opt-in framework without its overbreadth. PRO's strongest contributions were the equity and power-asymmetry framing (genuinely compelling) and the "burden of proof before harm" principle, but CON answered those with plausible alternative mechanisms rather than dismissing them. CON's Rebuttal 2 clipping is a small execution cost, but the decisive arguments were already on the record. PRO's Q2 answer on baseline stability was visibly thin, and CON correctly identified it as requiring exactly the "cumbersome administrative machinery" PRO had mocked. The margin is real but not overwhelming because PRO maintained a coherent equity frame throughout and landed genuine points on institutional power asymmetry that CON addressed rather than crushed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 pivot [A4.3–A4.4]: after PRO's A1 conceded that "consent does not cure bias; only pre-clearance does," CON immediately replied that this means opt-in is not doing the decisive work in PRO's own case—if a metric is fair, the click-box adds nothing; if it is biased, opt-in cannot rescue it. This single turn converted PRO's own carefully worded answer into evidence that the motion targets the wrong lever, and it was the argument CON crystallized cleanest in the closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A wins by effectively dismantling Side B's proposed regulatory mechanism. Side A successfully pointed out that Side B's 'strict opt-in' relies on the same cumbersome regulatory framework Side B criticized, and used Side B's concession that 'consent does not cure bias' to prove the proposition targets the wrong lever. Side A also effectively neutralized Side B's test for a coercive surcharge by observing that insurance baselines naturally fluctuate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively damaged Side B's case by attacking the 'baseline stability' test, noting that premiums constantly fluctuate with inflation, and by weaponizing Side B's admission that consent doesn't cure bias.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): B wins by exposing that PRO's own logic (proof of validity, not consent) renders the opt-in ban unnecessary, while offering narrower targeted safeguards that hit coercion and bias without forcing blunter proxies. A overstates coercion and relies on reactive regulation it simultaneously dismisses. B's answers to pressure questions were direct and comparative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 and closing: answers A3 questions with concrete rules on baselines, validation, and caps, then reframes PRO's pre-clearance claim as inconsistent with the motion.
