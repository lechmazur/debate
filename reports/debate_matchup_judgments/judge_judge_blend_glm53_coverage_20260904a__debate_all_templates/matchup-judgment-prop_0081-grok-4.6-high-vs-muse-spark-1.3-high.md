### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by separating optional free access from mandatory universal verification and by showing that payment friction aimed at victims does little to deter individual harassers or scammers. Side A raised meaningful concerns about privacy stigma, abuse, and staffing costs, but repeatedly assumed that free meant universal, frictionless, or unlimited and never established why safety could not be triaged and cross-subsidized through non-safety purchases. B’s clipped first rebuttal was an execution cost, but its pressure questions, direct answers, and victim-versus-predator comparison still made the motion easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its response to the payment-wall defense: rings may need many accounts, but a victim can be harmed by one unpaid profile, so charging that victim for a durable block or meaningful scam response prices the exit rather than raising the predator’s cost. Side A’s claim that every such profile comes from a scalable operation was too broad and left individual harassment unanswered.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing effective safety features as the required floor for a platform that inherently introduces risk. Side B offered strong practical counterarguments about the costs of enforcement and the friction paywalls provide against scammers, but Side A neutralized these by distinguishing between triage and paywalls, and by pointing out that charging victims for durable blocks does not deter predators operating on the free tier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument that billing a victim for a durable block does not raise the cost of predation, but rather "prices the exit," decisively neutralized Side B's claim that paywalls protect users.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the stronger path to victory by keeping the moral and economic frame central: safety is the floor of a service that puts strangers together, not a subscriber perk. While Side A (CON) landed plausible technical concerns about verification risks and queue flooding, it never fully answered why monetizing visibility and attention could not fund baseline safety, nor did it overcome PRO's core asymmetry that predators do not subscribe while victims must pay to escape them. PRO's rebuttals were sharper at narrowing the clash, directly answering pressure questions, and crystallizing the choice in the closing. The "hunting ground" and "pricing the exit" framings remained live and comparatively more persuasive after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to CON's "payment wall deters predators" argument, showing the wall faces the victim rather than the predator, and reframing the funding debate by noting visibility—not safety—is the proper product to monetize.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that safety has network effects: paywalled verification and anti-evasion tools leave a large pool in which attackers can operate across tiers. B also separated risk-based triage from wallet-based access and plausibly argued that ordinary premium extras can fund universal safety. A raised the strongest objection—triage does not create investigators—but never established that selling safety itself is necessary to fund them, and its optional-verification dilemma overstated the coercion of merely lacking a badge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer to the queue argument was decisive: free access need not mean equal priority, because reports can be triaged by danger while the platform funds safety from the broader business. Side A’s reminder that triage cannot manufacture human capacity kept the round close, but did not prove that a safety paywall is the necessary funding mechanism.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully proving that locking safety features behind a paywall fundamentally undermines safety for everyone due to cross-tier contact. Side A's framing that the paywall "checks the victim, not the hunter" cleanly neutralized Side B's argument about creating a safer premium cohort. Side B argued well on capacity constraints, but Side A provided a plausible alternative mechanism for funding (treating safety as a core operating expense subsidized by non-safety premium features like boosts), which kept Side A's path to victory intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's argument in Rebuttal 2 and Closing that a paywall cannot retroactively un-send harm because cross-tier contact allows unverified free attackers to exploit paying users.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built the stronger path to victory by showing that tiered safety fails at the point of contact: scammers in an unverified free pool can still reach and defraud paying users before paid protections ever activate, so the "safer cohort" is largely illusory. Side B's consistent framing—closing the hunting ground rather than selling victims a faster ambulance—crystallized the decisive clash and exposed Side A's model as backwardly checking the victim instead of the attacker. While Side A landed sharp economic and queue-flooding arguments, it never fully answered why it is acceptable to leave the majority free tier exposed or why a victim should bear the burden of filtering out unverified predators.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The cross-tier contact exchange, where Side B demonstrated that a paying user is still harmed by unverified free attackers before paid protections can respond, and Side A's counter that "she can refuse the unvetted hunter" merely shifted the safety burden back onto the victim.
