### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's central strategic frame. Side B relied heavily on the claim that Side A had conceded an "equivalent outcome" where regulation achieves the same goal as a ban but more cheaply. Side A successfully rebutted this by demonstrating that retaining a corporate entitlement inherently carries risks that a buyout eliminates, specifically pointing out that regulatory "friction" during a drought directly threatens the water reserve.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing effectively neutralized Side B's cost-equivalence frame by arguing that when restrictions must precede depletion, regulatory "friction" is not just an administrative nuisance, but a direct danger to the water supply.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) kept the decisive clash on whether reserves should carry avoidable commercial claims and answered CON’s strongest hits: compensation is a one-time exit cost vs indefinite management, monitoring remains but commercial incentive to resist caps disappears, and emergency bottles don’t require private ownership of the source. CON’s cost and continuity arguments are real but left the tension that drought caps also face resistance/compensation unless all permits contain ideal conditions, undermining the claim regulation achieves the same protection cheaper.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.6-B2.7 / B4.4 distinction that emergency logistics (bottles, trucks, packaging) can be contracted while extraction rights remain public, neutralizing CON’s continuity and disaster-gap argument.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A wins by successfully defending the structural advantage of a ban over regulation. While Side B mounted a strong offensive on the financial costs of takings and compensation, Side A effectively absorbed this as a one-time transition cost that purchases a permanent exit from the conflict. Side A's most decisive move was cleanly separating the extraction right from packaging logistics, which neutralized Side B's emergency supply chain argument. In the final clash, Side A decisively rejected Side B's "same destination" framing by clarifying that regulation leaves a perpetual, legally protected commercial claim on the aquifer that the government must continually fight to constrain, whereas a ban removes the claim entirely. Side A's rebuttals were sharper, particularly in exposing Side B's unproven assumption that all existing concessions contain drought-cap conditions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's emergency readiness argument by distinguishing between physical logistics (which can be contracted) and extraction rights (which the ban targets), while simultaneously turning Side B's permit-condition argument by noting Side B failed to prove existing concessions actually contain those clauses.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully linking the structural incentives of corporations to the inevitable failure of the regulatory regime Side B proposed. Side A's answers to the pressure questions cleanly dismantled Side B's attempt to separate physical extraction from corporate motive, proving that the motive inherently compromises the regulatory cap. Side B's inability to provide an empirical example of their regime working further cemented Side A's structural critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1 and A2 in Rebuttal 2 brilliantly explained why corporate extraction is uniquely harmful, showing that a corporation's fiduciary duty turns every regulated limit into a continuous negotiation, whereas a ban collapses enforcement into a resilient binary fact.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): CON maintained a live, narrower route: public allocation can deny harmful pumping without banning safe pumping, corporate identity doesn't change volume, and a no-pumping order is also binary.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 [A2.2] + Rebuttal 2 [A4.1] - pointing out that "unregulated aquifer withdrawals" cannot prove failure of conservative, metered, curtailment-ready permits, and refusing to invent a jurisdiction while demanding same evidentiary standard from PRO.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a clearer, more decisive comparative mechanism on enforcement. A's argument that a categorical ban reduces enforcement to a binary fact—eliminating the continuous negotiation and leverage that corporations exploit in permit regimes—directly answered B's regulatory design and neutralized the "volume is volume" defense. While B showed excellent epistemic discipline by refusing to invent a jurisdictional example, A effectively capitalized on that empirical gap in the closing. A's rhetoric was also sharper and more memorable, crystallizing the stakes of regulatory capture.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's explanation in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing of why a binary ban removes corporate leverage and simplifies enforcement, directly defeating Side B's claim that targeted no-pumping orders are equally effective.
