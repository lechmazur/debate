### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but Side A's rebuttals cut deeper into the live clash. A's "bait-and-switch" framing of retroactively dissolving confidentiality promises, its irreversibility argument on disclosure ("no court order can stuff the secret back into the envelope"), and its concrete intermediary-submission mechanism for medical updates directly answered B's strongest pressure question (dynamic mutation updates) with a workable alternative, which B never fully neutralized—its closing simply restated the same claim about needing "a named individual" without engaging A's intermediary rebuttal. B's equality/autonomy framing and "second-class citizen" argument were strong and rhetorically memorable, but the case leaned more on assertion (records are the adoptee's alone) than on defeating A's granular counters about third-party spillover and reliance harms. A's Rebuttal 2 was clipped for length, a real execution cost that slightly tempers its dominance, but the substantive content that survived was largely complete and decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1) answering B's pressure question about later-discovered mutations by proposing intermediary-authorized updates without identity exposure—paired with the unanswered "envelope" irreversibility point from Rebuttal 1—did the most damage to B's medical-necessity pillar, which B's closing failed to directly re-engage.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that access to identifying information creates an irreversible privacy breach, not merely optional contact. Side B established genuine autonomy and identity interests, but its medical case remained vulnerable because a name neither supplies clinical facts nor compels biological parents to provide updates. Side A also better handled reliance on permanent confidentiality, while Side B largely asserted that such promises were illegitimate rather than resolving the retroactive breach. The clipped rebuttal modestly hurt A but did not erase its comparative advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to B’s medical-link argument was decisive: anonymized intermediaries can transmit willing updates, while releasing a name does not make an unwilling parent cooperate. A then exposed that B’s prospective time-limit proposal did not answer the harm of retroactively dissolving existing confidentiality guarantees.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won the core analytical clashes, particularly regarding the nature of the anonymity promise and the mechanics of medical privacy. B successfully framed the confidentiality guarantee as a binding state obligation that induced relinquishment, making retroactive dissolution a breach of trust rather than a simple expiration of temporary secrecy. Furthermore, B effectively dismantled A's medical necessity argument by distinguishing between the clinical facts required for health and the legal identity demanded for contact, noting that willing parents can already update registries. Side A's pivot to applying temporal limits to "future placements" effectively conceded the retroactivity problem for existing records, and its reliance on protective orders to manage spillover risks was less persuasive than B's point about the irreversibility of a leaked name.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to the medical necessity claim, which sharply distinguished between the clinical data oncologists actually need and the legal identity A was demanding, while highlighting that intermediaries already allow willing parents to submit dynamic updates without exposing their identities.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but PRO landed the more damaging structural hit: by pointing out that record-sealing was typically a unilateral statutory default rather than an individually bargained-for promise (B2.1), PRO undercut CON's central "broken promise/reliance" pillar, and CON's response ("whether she signed or not, she acted under assurance") never fully repaired that foundation. PRO also successfully pressed that non-identifying medical summaries are practically inadequate, weakening CON's proposed alternative, and PRO's equality framing ("no one else owns your birth story") was never squarely answered beyond restating the privacy claim. CON's strongest surviving point was the sequencing argument (identity disclosure itself, not just contact, causes irreversible harm, sharpened by pressure question A3.2), and PRO's answer ("knowledge itself is not violence," "proves too much") was serviceable but not airtight — yet CON also failed to press this advantage further in its shorter, more repetitive closing speeches. On balance PRO progressed the debate more effectively, answered pressure with clearer structure, and left CON's core premise more exposed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 point that sealed records were imposed by statute rather than requested by birth parents (B2.1), which undercut CON's "broken promise" framing and was never adequately repaired in CON's later speeches.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side B won by distinguishing access from contact and framing permanent sealing as an exceptional denial of adult autonomy. Side A presented real privacy and reliance concerns, but generalized disputed confidentiality expectations into a blanket veto and relied on speculative disruption and adoption effects. Its proposed medical summaries and mutual-consent registries did not fully answer outdated records or the independent identity interest.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest response was that no-contact rules can regulate actual intrusion without enforcing permanent ignorance. Side A repeatedly treated private disclosure as equivalent to publication or family disruption, but never established that access itself necessarily causes those harms.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built a more philosophically coherent and legally grounded case, successfully framing the debate around equality and the limits of privacy. A's rebuttal that B's standard—protecting birth parents from the disruption of "mere knowledge"—"proves too much" by logically justifying the concealment of paternity in all contexts was a decisive blow to B's core harm argument. A also effectively neutralized B's reliance argument by distinguishing between a unilateral statutory default and a negotiated contract. A's rhetoric was sharper, more memorable, and better adapted to the evolving clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 response to B's pressure question, where A dismantled B's claim that "mere knowledge causes irreversible disruption" by showing it would absurdly justify concealing paternity from any adult just to protect a parent's settled life, exposing the overreach in B's privacy framework.
