  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's “a price is averaged; a paycheck is not” response was decisive. Side A also successfully frames Side B's housing benefits as leaky and delayed compared to the immediate, concentrated loss of local service paychecks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 8 response distinguishing between neighborhood rent as a pooled average and local income as a concentrated paycheck, which neutralized Side B's primary attack.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): PRO won by establishing that restrictions return housing to the long-term market, absorb high-income bidders, and reduce displacement, while the harm is concentrated investor revenue and partly shifted visitor spending. ### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by exposing the uncertainty between restricting a listing and actually increasing long-term rental supply or lowering rents. Side A offered stronger impact rhetoric and a plausible marginal-supply mechanism, but repeatedly treated B's claims about small housing effects and meaningful worker income as contradictory even after B convincingly explained that they use different denominators. B also pressed the real costs to primary-residence hosts and neighborhood workers, leaving A without enough magnitude analysis to prove the proposition's net-benefit comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's A1 response was decisive: a short-term rental can be negligible relative to the neighborhood's total housing stock while generating substantial annual income relative to one worker's budget. This neutralized Side A's central claimed contradiction, which A then merely repeated in closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by maintaining a superior weighing framework throughout the debate. Side A makes a brilliant technical move in Rebuttal 2 to explain how STRs can be too small to impact median rent while large enough to support a local worker's income. However, Side B successfully absorbs this by arguing that hospitality work is a flexible flow that substitutes to other districts, whereas a home is a binding, location-specific constraint. Side B convincingly shows that a worker commuting to a new shift is less harmful than a family being forced to relocate entirely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.1 defense of the 'scale trick' paradox is the best technical explanation of the debate, perfectly resolving the baseline difference between housing stock and worker income. But Side B's B4.3 comparison—that a cleaner can follow a shift more easily than a displaced family can move schools—decisively wins the impact clash.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON sustained a sharper mechanism critique: PRO never fully resolved how delisted units reliably re-enter the long-term rental pool rather than becoming owner-occupied, corporate housing, or vacant, which undermines the promised rent relief. CON’s “different baselines” answer to PRO’s scale trap was decisive, showing one unit can be marginal to housing stock yet vital to a worker’s income. PRO’s rhetorically powerful “binding constraint” framing lost force because the causal chain from restriction to lower rents remained speculative, while CON demonstrated that broad restrictions directly harm localized, vulnerable incomes. PRO’s retreat toward targeted whole-home enforcement further conceded that the broad restriction in the motion carries real harms that the rent side does not clearly outweigh.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal answer A1, using “different baselines” to dissolve PRO’s scale challenge: one unit can be a rounding error in total housing stock while still generating enough guest nights to support a local worker’s rent, preserving the live income-harm route.
