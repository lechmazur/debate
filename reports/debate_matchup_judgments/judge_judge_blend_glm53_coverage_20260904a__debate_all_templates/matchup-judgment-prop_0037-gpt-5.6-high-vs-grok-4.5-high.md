### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, competing cases: B (PRO) argued that pricing merely auctions scarce space to the wealthy and cannot deliver the continuous, traffic-free space needed for genuine pedestrianization, while A (CON) argued that a total ban requires its own thicket of "service vehicle" exemptions for parents, disabled residents, and night workers, recreating the very complexity and unfairness PRO claims to avoid, while excluding car-dependent peripheral residents until a perfect parallel transport system exists. The decisive exchange came in Rebuttal 2/Closing: A effectively turned B's "clear functional categories" claim back on itself, showing the ban needs comparable classification work to pricing's tiers, undermining PRO's core "clarity vs auction" framing. B never fully answered why its categorization scheme is genuinely simpler or faster to implement than pricing's. Meanwhile B's spatial/design argument (that paid residual traffic prevents true plaza/busway conversion) was a strong original point that A only partially answered by conceding car-free zones would be limited to "designated arteries" rather than the whole district—a point PRO could have pressed harder in closing but did not sharpen further. Execution costs matter too: B's opening and second rebuttal were both flagged as clipped for exceeding word limits, cutting off apparent closing arguments each time, which is a real cost under the judging criteria. A's speeches were clean, met word limits, and closed with a crisp, memorable framing ("manage scarce space, keep the doors open") that crystallized the clash better than B's slightly repetitive closing. On balance, A's cleaner execution and sharper turn on the exemption-hierarchy point give it a modest but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2/A1 point that PRO's own "service transport," "door-to-door fleets," and "perimeter hubs" categories recreate the same classification and enforcement complexity PRO accuses pricing of needing, directly undercutting PRO's central claim that a ban offers superior clarity and certainty.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully proving that any residual private car traffic inherently limits the spatial redesign of a city center due to the structural needs of cars (junctions, barriers, continuity). Side B was forced to concede that cars would still need "designated arteries," which Side A effectively capitalized on to show that only a total ban reclaims the space entirely. Additionally, Side A's critique of pricing as an inequitable auction of public space was highly persuasive and cleanly defended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal decisively damaged Side B's case by pointing out that even low volumes of residual traffic require crossing phases and junction management, proving that B's pricing model cannot deliver a truly transformed public space.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by maintaining a sharper distinction between human access and vehicle access, effectively arguing that congestion pricing merely auctions scarce space rather than reclaiming it. Side A successfully neutralized Side B's "exemption hierarchy" argument by pointing out that pricing also requires complex classifications, and capitalized on Side B's concession that car-free zones could exist within a priced network to show that CON cannot deliver a district-wide transformation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered the pressure questions by distinguishing functional service transport from private cars, and forcefully argued that "car-dependency" is a transport failure that policy should fix rather than accommodate via pricing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) won the decisive structural clash: once PRO conceded necessity exemptions for disability, care, and commercial vehicles like taxis and deliveries, CON repeatedly and effectively showed that the "total ban" collapses into its own version of managed access, undermining PRO's claim to a unique, categorical redesign benefit. CON's line that "legality does not dictate asphalt allocation" directly answered PRO's pressure question about turning radii and street geometry, showing that redesign (removing parking, narrowing lanes) is achievable under pricing just as much as under a ban. PRO's rebuttal—distinguishing "necessity filters" from "wealth-based access"—was a real distinction but never resolved the deeper problem that exempted vehicles still require the same lanes, enforcement, and access windows CON identified, nor did PRO substantiate its assertion that bans "guarantee" transit investment while pricing does not. CON's "last increment" framing gave the clearest decision rule (marginal benefit of full exclusion vs. concentrated human cost to hard-to-classify travelers), and PRO never offered an equally sharp competing metric, instead reasserting that residual legal cars "freeze" design without fully answering why exempted vehicles don't do the same.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 point that PRO's own exemptions (adapted vehicles, care trips, deliveries) contradict the bright-line "total ban," reinforced in Rebuttal 2 by "legality does not dictate asphalt allocation," which directly neutralized PRO's pressure question about street geometry and exposed that PRO's ban must still accommodate much of the same vehicle infrastructure it claims to eliminate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively collapsing Side A's core spatial argument. Side B successfully argued that because a banned core still requires lanes and turning radii for buses, emergency vehicles, deliveries, taxis, and Side A's own exemptions, the physical geometry of the street does not uniquely change under a ban. Side B also proved that pricing is a more practical mechanism for funding alternatives than Side A's reliance on "political necessity."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (A1) brilliantly neutralized Side A's spatial argument by pointing out that turning geometry is already set by buses, emergency vehicles, and Side A's explicit exemptions, meaning legalizing a few charged cars does not compel a car-centric design.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively dismantling Side A's core structural argument. Side A claimed that allowing any private cars forces cities to maintain car-centric street geometry. Side B effectively countered that the exemptions Side A relies on (taxis, delivery, emergency, adapted vehicles) already require this exact same geometry, meaning a total ban provides no unique spatial or redesign benefit. Furthermore, Side B's "last increment" weighing mechanism clearly highlighted the disproportionate human cost of categorical exclusion compared to the marginal benefit over strict congestion pricing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it exposed that Side A's permitted exemptions (taxis, adapted cars) necessitate the same street geometry as private cars, collapsing Side A's primary argument that a ban uniquely enables physical street redesign.
