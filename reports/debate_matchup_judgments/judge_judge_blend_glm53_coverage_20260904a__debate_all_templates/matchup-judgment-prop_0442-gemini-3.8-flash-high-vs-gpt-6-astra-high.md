### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO built a clear, intuitive case around asset lock-in and coordination failure, and its opening was rhetorically strong. But CON consistently pressed the hardest question—whether a "binding" mandate actually guarantees the fuel supply and infrastructure it promises—and PRO's own compliance mechanism (fleet pooling plus "pay compliance penalties and keep sailing") ultimately conceded the very flexibility it had attacked in CON's carbon-pricing alternative. CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3) exploited this contradiction directly and cleanly, and PRO's closing never squarely answered it, instead reasserting the lock-in framing without resolving why its escape valve doesn't collapse the "guarantee vs incentive" distinction it built its whole case on. CON also correctly called out PRO's mischaracterization of its carbon-pricing plan as "modest" when CON had specified an escalating, credible price, and PRO did not fully recover from that correction. Both sides argued the motion as written and used plausible, ungrounded-but-reasonable general knowledge without fake precision, but CON's tighter internal consistency and sharper exploitation of pressure-question answers gave it the more resolved, decision-easing case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.3, catching PRO's own admission that carriers can "pay compliance penalties and keep sailing" as functionally identical to the pay-instead-of-switch flexibility PRO condemned in carbon pricing—undermining PRO's core "guarantee vs incentive" distinction that the whole case rested on.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the fight on the instrument itself, not generic climate urgency. A’s lock-in/coordination case was strong and consistent, but under pressure A added penalties, pooling, and phased targets that turned the “binding fuel timetable” into a flexible pay-or-comply scheme. B cleanly showed that this collapses A’s uniqueness claim versus escalating lifecycle carbon pricing, while also holding the delivery-certainty gap open. A never re-established a reliable comparative climate benefit from the extra rigidity after those escape valves appeared.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3/B5.2: PRO’s compliance-penalty answer protects service by letting operators pay instead of switch—the exact option PRO said fatally weakens carbon pricing—so the mandate’s “guarantee” becomes just another incentive under scarcity.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively exposing a fatal contradiction in Side B's case. When pressed on how a mandate handles fuel shortages, Side B introduced "compliance penalties" and "fleet pooling" as escape valves. Side A brilliantly pointed out that this mechanism functions exactly like the carbon pricing Side B had previously dismissed as ineffective. If the penalty is affordable, it acts merely as a financial incentive that can be passed on; if it is prohibitive, it causes the exact supply shocks and freight spikes Side B claimed a mandate would avoid. Side A also effectively defended its alternative by explaining how a credible, escalating carbon price factors into the 30-year lifetime cost of a vessel at the ordering stage, neutralizing Side B's "asset lock-in" argument. Side A's rebuttals were sharper, more logically consistent, and dismantled Side B's core comparative advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's "compliance penalty" escape valve, demonstrating that it collapsed Side B's distinction between a binding mandate and a carbon price.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases—A on coordination/investment logic and externality pricing, B on physical fuel scarcity, renewable-input competition, and distributive food-security harm. B's "binding-vs-exempt" dilemma (rebuttal 1/2, closing) was the debate's sharpest pressure point, forcing A to concede "no absolute guarantee" on financing and to introduce narrow emergency relief. A's answer—distinguishing ordinary cost rises (still binding) from catastrophic humanitarian triggers (narrowly excepted, with defined trigger/expiry)—was a defensible, non-evasive refinement rather than a reframe of the motion, and it preserved the core "even if costs rise" commitment for the bulk of the timetable. Crucially, A's closing turned the dilemma back on B's alternative (performance standards face the identical choice between eventually forcing fuel substitution or leaving decarbonization unfinished), and B's closing did not engage this counter-turn, instead recapping earlier points. That unanswered tu quoque, combined with A's cleaner handling of the Q&A round (clear A1/A2 structure directly engaging both questions), gives A a modest edge in resolving the debate's central clash, even though B's scarcity and equity arguments remained forceful and vivid throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 1 exposing the tension between "binding even if costs rise" and A's later "costs should determine pacing"/emergency relief was the most damaging single exchange; A's closing counter-turn showing B's performance-standard alternative faces the same substitution dilemma was the most consequential unanswered response.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): B won by locking A into a live dilemma on the motion’s core wording: a truly binding fuel timetable either forces scarce clean-fuel bidding that hits food/fertilizer freight for import-dependent countries, or else needs cost-triggered relief that undercuts the demand certainty A needed to justify the mandate. A’s coordination case and lead-time phasing were coherent and answered the strawman of overnight conversion, and A’s pressure on deep performance standards was sharp, but A never restored a clean “even if costs rise” route once emergency carve-outs and soft government-funding protection were admitted. B made the decisive comparison easier: flexible goal-based standards cut emissions without manufacturing an artificial fuel squeeze or relying on phantom safety nets.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 dilemma: binding enforcement immiserates bulk importers; waivers recreate the investment stalemate A’s case depends on.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully trapping Side B in a dilemma regarding the binding nature of the mandate and the physical reality of green fuel supply. Side A's argument that mandating non-existent fuels would trigger a bidding war with terrestrial grids was highly persuasive, and its attack on Side B's reliance on hypothetical government subsidies to offset regressive freight costs was decisive. Side B's defense that narrow emergency relief would not destroy the overall demand signal was plausible but failed to fully overcome Side A's sharp critiques of the mandate's real-world economic and physical constraints.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it crystallized the inescapable dilemma: enforcing the timetable regardless of freight spikes immiserates vulnerable nations, while carving out exemptions for essential cargoes destroys the guaranteed demand that Side B claimed was necessary to solve the investment stalemate.
