### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Both sides built a genuine clash, but PRO's analogy to already-tolerated intrusions (schools, fire stations, restaurants, bars/taquerias) did more work than CON's "sacred home baseline," because PRO could show the baseline is not actually how neighborhoods function, while CON never explained what happens to the isolated resident when negotiation predictably fails — the exact scenario PRO's Q1 exposed. CON's textual point ("even when residents worry" means override, not negotiation) was sharp and never fully neutralized, but PRO's closing exploited CON's own pressure-round concession that both harms are equally serious, showing CON's rule gives one harm 100% protection and the other 0% — an asymmetry CON's answers ("you get to make your case," "home is sacred") never resolved. CON's emotionally vivid examples (night nurse, grandfather) were effective but PRO absorbed them into its own framework by noting these same people also need late-hour spaces. PRO's clipped opening is a real execution cost, but the core framework survived intact and was reinforced in later rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Questions (A3.1–A3.2) forcing CON to admit both harms are comparably serious, followed by PRO's Rebuttal 2/Closing use of that concession to show CON's rule gives sleep 100% protection and loneliness 0% — a asymmetry CON's "sacred baseline" answer never actually resolved.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO won the central weighing clash. A reframed "even when residents worry" as worry ≠ veto plus enforceable conditions, showed low-cost venues can mitigate noise/parking like existing bars/taquerias, and pressed the asymmetry concession that both sleep disruption and loneliness are health harms. B kept asserting an absolute baseline right to rest and an override reading of the motion, never answering why schools/bars are allowed, and its pressure round asked questions that supported PRO's incumbency critique. A managed both harms vs B protecting one at 100% cost to the other became the live, decisive comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1/R2 combo - the "proves too much" response to the night nurse/grandfather examples plus the 25-seat café walkability point, and A4 flipping B's own Q1 to expose that CON defends a 100%-0% incumbency rule after conceding both harms are serious.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A built a much stronger comparative case, successfully framing "allow" as removing an absolute NIMBY veto rather than an unconditional override. Side A's pressure questions were sharp and exposed Side B's asymmetrical weighing of harms. Side B made a critical unforced error in Pressure Questions Q1, accidentally making Side A's point, and failed to effectively distinguish low-cost venues from the existing bars and restaurants they conceded were acceptable. Side A's rebuttals were direct, well-weighed, and decisively won the clash over how to manage competing public health harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they brilliantly capitalized on Side B's confused Q1 to highlight the asymmetry of giving sleep 100% protection and loneliness 0%, while cleanly answering Q2 by pointing to existing conditional permits for schools and clinics.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A built a vivid, emotionally resonant case around the hidden cost of isolation, but that framing rested on unsupported generalizations ("general community research confirms") and, critically, on a specific statistic ("permits denied half the time") smuggled into the Pressure Questions round rather than argued with support—exactly the kind of brittle precision the rules warn against, and B exploited this cleanly. B's case was more structurally rigorous: it repeatedly forced A to confront who bears irreversible cost versus who can walk away, pressed the unanswered question of "verified by whom" for enforcement, and introduced (in the opening, legitimately) the strong point that commercial corridors and mixed-use zones already accommodate these venues—meaning the motion's real bite is overriding residents specifically in areas they chose for quiet. A never squarely answered this alternative-zoning argument, leaving a live, undamaged route for B. B also executed the Pressure Questions and answer format cleanly and used the exchange to sharpen rather than repeat its case, while A's answers partly relied on unverified numbers. B's closing tied these threads together more precisely, while A's closing largely restated its opening frame without resolving the mixed-use and verification gaps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's [B4.1] response exposing A's unsupported "denied half the time" statistic as asserted rather than shown, combined with the unanswered "verified by whom" question about enforcement, most damaged A's practical case for overriding resident objections.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won the decisive clash on the motion's "even when" language. B showed worry is the only evidence available before licensing, that A's reversible-harm fixes (on-call contact, three complaints) recreate the resident-burden gauntlet, and that commercial corridors already allow venues so the motion only adds override power. A never proved imposed late-hour commercial spaces create connection rather than resentment, and leaned on an unsupported "denied half the time" figure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B R2: turning A's enforcement answer into proof of B's burden argument — "Verified by whom?" — and reframing a contested café as a battleground not a hub, collapsing A's loneliness-cure and reversible-harm claims together.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's practical mechanisms and conceptual framing. While Side A relied on the emotional weight of loneliness and isolation, Side B effectively demonstrated that forcing commercial venues into residential areas over local objections—and relying on adversarial complaint hotlines for enforcement—produces resentment rather than community. Side B's pressure questions exposed critical flaws in Side A's enforcement and burden-of-proof assumptions, and Side B's closing perfectly crystallized why a contested, heavily policed venue fails to deliver the social fabric Side A promised.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side A's proposed enforcement mechanism (a posted phone number and suspension after three complaints) by pointing out that "verified by whom?" forces exhausted residents into an adversarial gauntlet against a commercial operator, proving the safeguard is a burden, not a shield.
