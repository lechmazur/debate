- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Both sides ran credible cases, but PRO (Side B) maintained a cleaner live route to victory throughout. PRO's structural argument — that every technocratic institution derives its right to govern from democratic consent, making legitimacy the ultimate ordering principle rather than a subordinate tool — was never squarely defeated. The Singapore pressure question (Q2) was PRO's sharpest move: CON's own favored example uses regular elections, which PRO correctly crystallized as a concession that democratic consent is always necessary, even in purportedly technocratic systems. CON's answer (elections "validate" rather than dictate technocratic policy) was adequate but did not neutralize the force of the point. CON's strongest counter was the physical-reality hierarchy — you cannot vote against a virus or a flood — and the "fatal contradiction" catch (PRO says preventable death is never acceptable, yet defends communities' power to reject expert mandates). PRO's response to the contradiction [B4.1] was reasonably grounded: exclusion breeds the distrust that makes life-saving mandates fail anyway, and PRO consistently framed their position as democratically accountable competence rather than uninformed populism. This left CON's "competence as absolute prerequisite" framing as a claim about necessity, not about comparative priority — it showed competence is important but did not show it matters more than legitimacy in the comparative sense the motion requires. CON's repetition of the physical-reality argument across rounds without deepening it cost them some progression credit. PRO's concession management was tighter: they never claimed to oppose expert input, only to insist on democratic accountability for it, which was a more defensible and nuanced position across the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 pressure question — asking why Singapore maintains democratic elections if competence alone suffices — was the most decisive exchange. CON's answer conceded that elections play a validating role, which PRO's closing correctly crystallized as proof that even the strongest technocratic systems rely on democratic consent as the ultimate source of authority.
