### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): CON built a cleaner, more durable structural case: consenting-adult autonomy plus a proportionality burden, arguing that every harm PRO named (coercion, inequality, medical risk) has a narrower remedy (staged safety approval, labor-law anti-compulsion rules, taxation) than outright criminalization. PRO's caste-system and coercion narrative was vivid but rested on largely unproven leaps ("mathematically superior" workers, "no safe framework ever") that were never substantiated beyond assertion, and PRO's key concession that prohibition merely pushes access offshore was never squarely reconciled with the claim that banning prevents stratification — CON exploited this directly and repeatedly, and PRO's closing answer (friction just keeps enhancement "rare") conceded the caste dynamic persists rather than refuting it. CON also answered the pressure questions with clear A1/A2 structure and substantive content, while PRO's rebuttals in later rounds largely reasserted prior claims rather than closing the proportionality gap CON kept pressing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 A2, explaining regulators can deny approval for unsafe techniques without banning the category forever, directly undercut PRO's "no safe framework can ever exist" claim and exposed that PRO's own offshore-friction concession undermines rather than supports the anti-caste argument.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): A won by consistently meeting the high burden for strict prohibition: showing narrower tools (staged safety approval, labor/anti-discrimination rules, liability) address risk, coercion, and inequality without criminalizing adult bodily choice, while B's ban is self-defeating because it concedes offshore access for the wealthy yet strips oversight. B pressed hard on biological uniqueness and obsolescence but never neutralized the comparative claim that regulation preserves management tools the ban destroys.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2/A4 reply that regulators can refuse unsafe edits while still rejecting permanent prohibition, paired with the offshore concession that a ban hides rather than prevents caste harms.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON showed that each of PRO’s harms—medical risk, coercion, and inequality—has a narrower regulatory remedy, making strict prohibition disproportionate and counterproductive by driving enhancement offshore. PRO’s speculative claims of biological obsolescence were deflated by analogy to other regulated risky choices, and CON’s closing crystallized that PRO never justified criminalizing consensual adult choice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal and closing, which turned PRO’s inequality and medical-risk arguments by demonstrating that prohibition creates a hidden caste system and removes safety tools, while regulation can target each harm without surrendering bodily autonomy.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides built coherent, competently argued cases, but B landed the sharper exchanges. A's core theory—that positional externalities turn consent into "adaptation"—was compelling in the abstract, but B's pressure question (Q1, Turn 6) exposed that A never satisfactorily distinguished genetic edits from already-tolerated baseline-shifting practices like orthodontics and cosmetic surgery beyond asserting "genetic is different," which reads as conclusory rather than demonstrated. B also landed a concrete, unrebutted practical point: that genetic-editing enforcement differs meaningfully from organ-sale bans because the underlying technology is more decentralized, meaning prohibition is more likely to simply export risk to unsafe black markets while the wealthy access safe options abroad—worsening rather than preventing the safety-based hierarchy A warns about. A's closing reasserted "smaller stigmatized market reduces harm" without directly re-engaging this distinguishing mechanism. Conversely, B's own weak point—that anti-discrimination law can handle dating/hiring pressure—was somewhat hand-waved and never fully answered A's dating-market coercion concern, but this gap was less central to B's overall throughline than A's unresolved line-drawing problem was to A's. On balance, B made the practical case-for-inaction (regulation beats a porous, harm-shifting ban) more concretely and answered pressure more directly, giving it a clear but not overwhelming edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question Q1 (Turn 6, B3.1) forcing A to explain why genetic editing is categorically different from already-accepted baseline-shifting practices like orthodontics and cosmetic surgery, combined with B's Rebuttal 2 point (B4.2) that CRISPR's decentralized nature undermines A's organ-sale-ban analogy for suppressing black markets—a point A's Closing never directly answered.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the decisive clash on social coercion and baseline change clearer across rounds: a licensed market turns non-enhancement into a marked disadvantage more than a smaller stigmatized one does, and ordinary autonomy analogies do not dissolve that. Side A pressed a strong black-market/offshore and bodily-sovereignty case, but never fully neutralized how regulation would normalize pressure or why genetic status goods differ from revisable external upgrades; B’s consistent weighing of reduced coercion over perfect enforcement carried the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 on why regulation generalizes risk/pressure while a stigmatized market keeps non-participation freer, answering A's safety and access line directly.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully turning Side B's equality and safety arguments against them. CON demonstrated that strict prohibition would push genetic enhancement into dangerous black markets and offshore medical tourism, creating the exact biological hierarchy PRO fears while violating bodily autonomy. PRO's abstract coercion framework never fully overcame CON's practical critique that criminalizing consensual bodily modification to manage social pressure sets a paternalistic precedent that fails on its own egalitarian terms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal exposing that PRO's ban guarantees a two-tier system where the wealthy access safe enhancements abroad while ordinary citizens face dangerous underground procedures, thereby undermining PRO's core equality argument.
