- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): B built a fuller causal case (job lock, fragmented risk pools, monopsony pricing, coverage collapse at diagnosis) and A never substantively rebutted the job-lock or pricing-power lines, letting them stand as unanswered structural advantages for single-payer. The pivotal exchange was B's tu quoque that A's proposed "fix" (subsidized public continuation for the unemployed/sick) is itself single-payer for the costliest population, which exposed real tension in CON's repair-not-replace strategy. A's best answer—distinguishing a backstop from exclusive purchasing—was coherent but largely assertive rather than mechanistically grounded, and A's closing repeated this distinction without fully dissolving B's "that's a concession, not a principle" framing. A did land one genuinely sharp rebuttal (that "campaignability" doesn't get a patient today's appointment), which meaningfully dented B's concentration-risk answer, but this single hit wasn't enough to offset the broader unanswered ground B held on efficiency and labor-market distortion. B's closing framing ("residence beats payroll") was also more memorable and cleanly crystallized the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal in [B2.3]/[B2.4]/[B4.3]—turning A's "subsidized continuation" fix into an admission that public financing is needed for exactly the population employer coverage fails—was the most damaging unresolved pressure point in the debate, though A's [A4.4] counter that "visibility does not provide the appointment" was the sharpest single rebuttal landed by either side.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won by effectively neutralizing Side B's core defense of "institutional alternatives." By pointing out that the choice belongs to the employer (whose incentive is cost-control) rather than the patient, Side A dismantled the supposed safeguard of pluralism. Furthermore, Side A masterfully framed Side B's "subsidized continuation" concession as proof that public financing is necessary for the vulnerable, making the retention of private administration a redundant and wasteful "second roof."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's first rebuttal, which pointed out that B's proposed "fallback" creates a single-payer system for the unprofitable while maintaining duplicate administrative costs for the rest.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the decisive clash on the motion’s enrollment rule and forcing B to defend a work-status tie that B never principled. After full exchange A left the cleaner reason to prefer residence-based financing over patched payroll financing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 conversion of B’s subsidized fallback into “single-payer for everyone private financing abandons, plus duplicate overhead,” which neutralized B’s repair-without-replacement route.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-engaged cases, but CON's structural argument about funding mechanisms proved decisive. CON's distinction between affirmative repeal (a visible political act needed to cut statutory protections) versus affirmative appropriation (where inaction quietly starves a single-payer budget) reframed the fiscal-fragility debate in a memorable, hard-to-rebut way. PRO's response—that single payer "need not" rely on annual appropriations and could be built on standing law—was exposed in CON's closing as describing no system that actually exists, while PRO's own admission ("I cannot promise the next legislature never cuts funding") handed CON a clean concession to exploit. PRO landed real points too: the job-lock framing tracks the motion's own language, the Switzerland citation correction damaged CON's evidentiary grounding, and B2.1's observation that CON's patches concede the need for coverage independent of employment was underexploited by CON but still a live wound. However, CON's tighter rhetorical control, sharper use of PRO's own phrasing ("sound governance, not employment-linked eligibility"), and the last-word closing that directly dismantled PRO's final defense gave CON the cleaner path to victory, despite the real cost of a clipped opening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 distinction between statutory subsidies requiring affirmative repeal versus single-payer budgets requiring affirmative appropriation (and the follow-up "that is the whole concession" jab at PRO's admission that legislatures might cut funding) was the pivotal exchange, reframing political fragility in a way PRO never fully neutralized.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully exposing a double standard in Side B's framework. Side B argued that single-payer systems are doomed by poor governance and annual budget cuts, while defending employer-based systems by pointing to highly regulated statutory patches (subsidies, guaranteed issue). Side A cleanly dismantled this in Rebuttal 2 and Closing by showing that Side B's "affordable escape routes" require the exact same political reliability and funding that Side B claimed was impossible for single payer, neutralizing Side B's structural differentiation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4.5) decisively shifted the debate by pointing out that Side B's market patches require the very same sound governance and reliable funding that Side B treated as disqualifying for a single payer.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by making structural failure under stress the decisive comparison: monopoly queues and simultaneous system-wide cuts versus patchable job-lock with standing multi-payer exits. A strongly forced governance symmetry and showed employer “choice” is often one plan, but could not neutralize B’s capacity/exit and appropriations-asymmetry weighing, especially after B’s concrete A1/A2 answers. B’s clipped opening was a real cost, yet later rounds adapted more cleanly to the live clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 2 A1/A2: individual-market exit plus statutory-vs-annual funding asymmetry, and tribunals cannot create OR capacity.
