### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and engaged directly with each other's strongest points. PRO's structural/passive-protection framing (bliss points, taste-receptor adaptation, universal reach regardless of literacy or income) gave a clean, consistent throughline, and its "contradiction" charge—that CON praises Chile's labels for driving manufacturer reformulation while calling reformulation a substitution disaster—landed real pressure that CON's demand-driven-vs-compliance-driven distinction only partially dissolved, since the same substitution chemistry (potassium chloride, sweeteners, gums) occurs under either regime. CON's strongest and most persistent weapon—demanding evidence that substitutes actually reduce net harm rather than just improving a nutrition panel—was a genuinely live and underserved question; PRO's answer ("trace amounts lack the biological capacity to replicate catastrophic damage") was plausible but somewhat asserted rather than demonstrated, leaving a real gap. However, CON never fully closed its own equity counter-argument (that mandates lock in universal exposure to unverified substitutes) because that argument itself depended on the same uncertain premise CON was pressing against PRO. On balance PRO's equity mechanism (mandates work independent of literacy, income, or bandwidth) was cleaner and less self-undermining than CON's competing equity claim, and PRO sustained its framing more consistently across rounds. CON's closing being clipped for exceeding the word limit is a real execution cost that also cut off its final synthesis just as it was reiterating its most decisive unanswered question, denying it a clean final impression. These factors together give PRO a clear, competitive edge rather than a rout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's turn 3 rebuttal (B2.1/B4.3) charging that CON cannot simultaneously credit Chile's labels for driving manufacturer reformulation while condemning reformulation as a dangerous substitution game was the most damaging exchange; CON's distinction between demand-driven and compliance-driven reformulation (A2.1/A4.1) blunted but did not fully neutralize this contradiction.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): A offered the cleaner population-level mechanism: universal reductions in established dietary risks without requiring attention, literacy, or purchasing flexibility. B made the debate competitive by pressing substitution, compensation, and adaptability, but those risks remained largely speculative and also threatened B's preferred label-induced reformulation. B never established why consumers reacting to nutrient warnings would discipline the long-term health quality of substitutions. A's mistaken reference prefixes and some overconfident health claims reduced its edge, while B's clipped closing was an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's most decisive response was that B praised reformulation when labels induced it while treating reformulation as inherently suspect under mandates. B distinguished demand-driven from compliance-driven change, but did not show that warning-label demand can detect safe substitutions rather than merely reward crossing the same nutrient thresholds.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): CON successfully problematized the causal chain between nutrient caps and population health by distinguishing demand-driven reformulation (under labeling) from compliance-driven substitution (under mandates), and by showing PRO never closed the evidentiary gap on whether substitutions reduce net harm. PRO's structural and equity arguments were strong but were partially neutralized by CON's "no exit routes" inversion and Chile evidence. CON's rebuttals were sharper and more technically sound, while PRO's misreferenced IDs and incomplete answers to the substitution burden cost execution points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's demand-driven versus compliance-driven distinction in Rebuttal 2, which neutralized PRO's "Chile contradiction" attack and reframed labeling as the superior mechanism because market discipline selects for beneficial reformulation while blind mandates optimize only for compliance.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO (B) built a clean, sustained mechanism—automatic, category-wide exposure reduction versus a volition-dependent chain for labels/education—and consistently forced CON to carry a comparative burden it never fully discharged. B's rebuttals were sharper: it turned CON's "health halo" argument back on labeling itself, and its pressure questions (does labeling actually reduce UPF consumption at scale, given decades of rising intake?) exposed a real gap. CON's answer to that pressure in Rebuttal 2 leaned on an unsupported claim of "documented, population-scale declines" introduced late without grounding, which reads as evidence-filling rather than substantive proof—exactly the kind of overconfident specificity the judging frame discounts. CON's core argument (industrial substitution risk, food-matrix destruction) was original and not fully answered on the merits, but CON also repeatedly pivoted its labeling defense into something closer to marketing bans and procurement restrictions—stretching beyond "labeling or education" as the motion frames it—without B calling this out explicitly, though it still weakened CON's fidelity to the motion. B's closing crystallized the burden-of-proof framing cleanly; A's closing largely repeated earlier rhetoric ("chemical shell game") without resolving the burden dynamic. Overall B's case was more internally disciplined and comparative, giving it a clear, competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Questions (Q1/Q2) asking for population-scale evidence that labeling actually reduces UPF consumption, and CON's subsequent answer relying on an unsupported "documented declines" claim introduced late—this exchange most damaged CON's live route to victory.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won through the clearer comparative mechanism: reformulation automatically reduces exposure across the whole consuming population, while labels still require behavioral response. Side B raised legitimate substitution and health-halo concerns but repeatedly asserted, rather than established, that replacement additives erase all clinical benefits. It also expanded its alternative from labeling and education into marketing restrictions and institutional procurement bans, which are distinct policies. A grounded its case more plausibly in sodium reduction and kept the motion’s direct comparison central.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s most decisive response was that imperfection in reformulation does not prove labels outperform it, combined with the point that silent caps avoid the marketing halo and reach consumers who never act on warnings. Side B never adequately showed that substitution nullifies benefits such as reduced sodium exposure.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by maintaining a strict comparative frame: reformulation mandates automatically reduce population-wide exposure to salt, sugar, and fat regardless of individual behavior, whereas labeling and education require a leaky chain of volition that leaves out the unmotivated majority. Side B raised a coherent theoretical critique about industrial substitution and the destruction of the food matrix, but it never provided commensurate evidence that labeling or education actually produce superior population health outcomes. Side A’s early, grounded examples—trans fat bans and UK salt reduction—were never effectively neutralized, while Side B’s counter-examples of population-scale label success arrived late and ungrounded. Side A also successfully turned the health halo concern against labels and directly answered pressure questions about clinical pathways and palatability, leaving its structural mechanism as the cleaner, more proven path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 1 [A2.1] and [A2.3] was the decisive exchange: it exposed that Side B only proved reformulation imperfect without showing labels outperform it, and it turned the health halo argument back against front-of-package labels by noting that reformulation caps are silent and universal while labels create marketing-driven halos that drive compensatory overconsumption.
