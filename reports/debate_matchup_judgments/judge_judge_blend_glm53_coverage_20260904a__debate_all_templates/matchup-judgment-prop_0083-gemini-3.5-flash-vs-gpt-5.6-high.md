### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON built a clearer decision rule from the outset: deception should track the false belief created in the recipient, not the production method of the content. This let CON generate a repeated, damaging counterexample PRO never neutralized—an accurate AI photo can be truthful while an unaltered old or staged photo can be genuinely misleading, so PRO's process-based test both over- and under-inclusive. PRO's answers to pressure questions largely reasserted the "synthetic vs. record" distinction (A4.1) without engaging why that distinction tracks harm rather than mere method, and its attempt to carve out "standard translation/formatting" as non-deceptive (A4.2) let CON land a sharp rebuttal (B4.3) showing PRO had quietly narrowed the motion's own broad category of "AI-written conversation assistance," undermining the proposition as stated. CON's closing crystallized this cleanly: deception should track the lie, not the software. PRO's closing was rhetorically vivid but mostly restated its trust-based intuition without resolving the boundary and old-photo problems raised against it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's repeated old-photo/staged-photo counterexample (B2.1–B2.2, reinforced in B4.4) against PRO's process-based deception test, culminating in B4.3's catch that PRO's own answer retreats from the motion's stated scope of "AI-written conversation assistance."
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by keeping the decisive standard clear: deception requires material false belief about the person, not the production tool. B consistently showed that PRO’s blanket rule condemns accurate AI depictions and endorsed assistance while excusing conventional misleading photos or ghostwriting, and exposed PRO’s inability to bound “AI-written conversation assistance” without collapsing into vague distinctions between formatting and generation. Side A delivered passionate authenticity rhetoric and strong trust framing but never neutralized the overbreadth or accuracy-vs-pedigree clash, leaving its route dependent on treating synthetic origin itself as inherently material. B’s answers, boundary pressure, and closings made the voting decision cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4: deception tracks false belief created by the representation, not whether pixels or prose were statistically generated; this directly undercut A’s “record vs simulation” claim while catching real catfishing without tool-based overbreadth.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A built a much stronger, more practical framework for deception based on material falsehood rather than production method. Side A effectively exposed the unworkable boundaries in Side B's definition of AI conversation assistance and highlighted the absurdity of condemning accurate AI representations while failing to address misleading conventional photos. Side B's philosophical distinction between captured photons and synthesized pixels was rhetorically polished but practically irrelevant to user expectations, leaving Side B's case vulnerable to Side A's sharp rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, which dismantled Side B's attempted boundary between "technical formatting" and "semantic generation" by pointing out that translation and paraphrasing inherently require semantic choices, rendering Side B's disclosure rule unworkable and overbroad.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): A built a cleaner, more adaptable case: it conceded early that disclosure ≠ ban, distinguished accessibility use from concealment, and in Rebuttal 2/Closing turned B's "stigma" objection back on itself — arguing that a negative reaction to a disclosure label is evidence the information is material, not evidence it should be hidden. A's pressure questions also forced B to confront the hardest cases (fully fabricated images, machine-supplied intimacy), and B's answers ("already covered by existing fraud rules," "adopted by sender's genuine intent") sidestepped rather than resolved those hypotheticals. B's strongest point — that disclosure rules will be unenforceable and will burden honest/vulnerable users while bad actors evade them — was real and never fully neutralized, but B failed to press or develop this asymmetry in the closing, instead mostly repeating the stigma/enforcement framing from earlier rounds. A's closing crystallized the clash more sharply around "whose consent controls," giving the judge a cleaner decision rule, while B's closing was largely recap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 turn on stigma ("if some recipients respond negatively to a label, that proves the information affects their choice—not that they should be deprived of it") most damaged B's central accessibility/stigma objection, and B never adequately answered this reframing in the following speeches.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): A built the cleaner path by centering recipient consent on material synthetic face/voice and treating disclosure as the modest default rather than a ban. B raised real accessibility and enforcement frictions but repeatedly collapsed labels into stigma and failed to confront the clearest cases A kept live. Across pressure and later weighing A adapted more tightly while B recycled the same cost-of-honest-users claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1–A4.5: partial deterrence plus recourse beats institutional permission, and hard-core photorealistic/outourced flirtation cases remain unanswered by curation analogies.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B built a significantly stronger case centered on the recipient's right to informed consent, effectively distinguishing between minor digital curation (like lighting or spellcheck) and material AI substitution (like generating non-existent physical features or composing synthetic wit). Side B dismantled Side A's enforcement and stigma arguments by demonstrating that imperfectly enforced norms still provide vital deterrence and recourse, and that transparency does not equate to a ban. Side A relied too heavily on false equivalencies and the "bad actors will ignore it" trope, while failing to adequately answer Side B's sharp pressure questions regarding wholesale fabrication and the recipient's agency.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered the pressure questions, exposed Side A's evasion of the most extreme cases of AI fabrication, and crystallized the decisive asymmetry between the modest burden of disclosure and the severe risks of concealed synthetic presentation.
