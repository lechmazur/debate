### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): The decisive clash was administrability and categorical fit. A's central move—that high-risk social-product law exists for public networks, strangers, and amplification, none of which a private one-to-one chat produces—was never dislodged. A also landed a clean, mechanistic rebuttal to B's slot-machine metaphor (no wallet, no public score, no network) and, crucially, exposed that B's proposed distinguishing test ("optimized for task completion" vs. "optimized for retention") fails to separate companion apps from ordinary tutoring, language-streak, and journaling apps that also optimize for continued engagement [A4.4]. B never repaired this in the closing, leaving a live, unanswered gap in its core administrable line. B's strongest point—that relational/attachment harm can occur without any toxic output, making content-based regulation blind to it—was real and forced A to make some concessions, but A absorbed this by conceding that dark patterns and notification abuse can be banned under ordinary consumer/chatbot rules, without needing the full high-risk apparatus (identity gates, duty-of-care litigation exposure, "behavioral reinforcement" audits). That partial concession blunted B's strongest attack while B's own framework remained vulnerable to overreach. Both sides followed the Q&A format correctly, but A used the pressure round more effectively to generate a rebuttal that went unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's tutor/streak/journal counterexample in Rebuttal 2 [A4.4], directly undercutting B's "design objective" line as the auditable distinction—left unaddressed in B's closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by locating the distinctive risk in engineered relational dependency rather than isolated toxic outputs. Side A raised strong overbreadth and administrability objections, but its proposed session limits, notification caps, and dark-pattern bans effectively conceded that system-level engagement design matters. B then offered a workable distinction between incidental warmth and products optimized around simulated reciprocity and retention.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: In the decisive exchange, B answered A’s boundary challenge by focusing audits on optimization objectives, retention mechanics, and guilt-based re-engagement rather than kindness itself. A showed that tutors also seek retention, but did not fully erase the difference between task-oriented continuity and monetized synthetic attachment.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A built a more compelling and structurally sound case by correctly identifying that the primary harm of AI companion apps is relational and architectural (emotional dependency, behavioral capture) rather than purely content-based. Side A effectively dismantled Side B's reliance on ordinary chatbot regulation (content filters) by showing it misses the systemic manipulation inherent in companion apps. While Side B raised a valid overbreadth concern regarding useful chatbots like tutors, Side A provided a workable distinction based on design objectives (task completion vs. relational retention). Side A's analogies and rhetorical framing were sharper and more decisive in proving why the high-risk social product paradigm is the correct regulatory fit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they clearly distinguished between task-optimizing tutors and relation-optimizing companions, directly answering Side B's overbreadth pressure and exposing the flaw in treating algorithmic emotional dependency as a mere "screen time" issue for parents to manage.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO built a consistent, vivid distinction between "regulating replies" (chatbot-level filters) and "regulating the relationship" (purpose-built attachment engineering), and used it to expose a genuine weakness in CON's core definitional move: that high-risk classification only applies to multi-user network harms. PRO's rebuttal that "any engagement product could escape social rules by removing other children from the screen" was a sharp, unrebutted point—CON's Rebuttal 2 essentially restated the network-externality claim rather than answering it. PRO's pressure questions (what extra feature is needed before a duty of care applies; which specific duty requires facial ID) forced CON to defend its biometric-surveillance claim, and CON's answer was a bare reassertion ("high-risk platform regimes routinely enforce strict age-assurance mandates") without engaging PRO's counter that age-appropriate design/duty-of-care needn't entail ID scans. CON's opening and R1 were solid and the privacy/collateral-harm argument was a real concern, but it was never tightly linked to the specific duties PRO proposed, leaving that objection more asserted than proven. PRO's closing crystallized the clash cleanly; CON's closing mostly recapped rather than resolving the live tension. Overall PRO progressed the debate more effectively and left fewer of its key claims unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 point that CON's "no network, no risk" framing would let any single-user engagement product escape social-product rules simply by removing other users from the screen—a structural loophole CON never squarely closed in Rebuttal 2.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by showing that persistent, profit-driven companionship creates attachment and developmental risks even without a public network or individually prohibited scripts. Side A offered credible targeted safeguards and benefits, but never resolved displacement, dependency, and intimacy-shaping inherent to the product model. Its claimed inevitability of biometric surveillance was asserted rather than established, while its migration argument did not justify weaker regulation of commercial products.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s pressure on age assurance was decisive: it asked which proposed duty actually required facial identification, and Side A answered only that firms would inevitably adopt it. B thereby weakened A’s largest claimed regulatory cost while preserving the distinction between filtering outputs and imposing a duty of care over designed attachment.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully distinguishing between regulating software outputs (ordinary chatbots) and regulating the purpose and relationship engine (high-risk social products). Side A effectively dismantled Side B's primary practical objection—that high-risk regulation inherently mandates biometric age verification—by clarifying that a duty of care and age-appropriate design do not require face scans. Side A's rhetorical framing ("regulate the relationship, not the wrapper") crystallized the clash perfectly, while Side B remained trapped in a definitional dispute about network externalities that Side A had already neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered the pressure questions by dismantling the assumption that high-risk social duties inherently require biometric surveillance, effectively neutralizing Side B's main collateral harm argument.
