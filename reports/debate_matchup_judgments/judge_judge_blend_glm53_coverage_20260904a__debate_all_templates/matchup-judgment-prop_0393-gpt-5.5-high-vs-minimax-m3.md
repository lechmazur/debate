### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO (Side A) enters the debate with a durable core argument: design-stage accessibility is cheapest, buildings lock in barriers for decades, and universal design benefits a wide population—not a narrow disability category. Those three pillars mostly survived the exchange. CON's best pressure was the "stronger vs. modest" tension, exploited effectively in pressure questions and Rebuttal 2, forcing PRO to name a breaking point (major floor-area losses, project-killing structures). CON then used that concession to argue PRO's case only works if "stronger" drifts cheaper each round. That is a genuine live wound on PRO. However, PRO's responses hold up comparatively well: the design-stage cost advantage was never refuted on its own terms; the retrofit argument was effectively rebutted (CON only defended easy handle/ramp swaps, never the bathroom footprint and corridor-width problem PRO raised); and the "cheap inaccessible unit is not affordable to someone who cannot enter it" point was never cleanly neutralized by CON's supply concern—both can be true simultaneously. CON's targeted-set-aside alternative left meaningful gaps PRO identified (visitability, common spaces, ordinary-unit adaptability) and CON never adequately answered these. CON's supply argument was compelling in assertion but unproven in magnitude. Two clipped turns for PRO are a real execution cost but do not change the outcome because the visible portions still carry the load. The weighing favors PRO: the lock-in effect of building barriers into durable stock is certain and compounding; the supply-kill risk of modest baseline standards remains asserted rather than demonstrated.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange followed CON's pressure question about the voucher-holding wheelchair user. PRO's response—that a cheap inaccessible unit is not affordable to someone who cannot enter or bathe safely in it, so the supply gain is illusory for that renter—correctly framed the asymmetry: CON's supply argument assumes any new unit helps equally, but inaccessible units do not solve the housing problem for the very people CON invokes. CON's comeback ("you can't visit a friend in an apartment that was never built") is rhetorically sharp but does not escape the trap, because PRO can accept that both problems are real and still argue that building accessible stock from the start reduces both simultaneously.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A wins by successfully grounding their case in the motion's stipulation of 'modest' costs and proving that universal design prevents decades of expensive, often impossible retrofits. Side B makes a strong economic argument about marginal supply, but Side A effectively counters that an inaccessible unit provides no value to a disabled renter anyway.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 point that building codes already mandate fire and plumbing safety regardless of marginal supply impacts, successfully framing accessibility as a basic functional requirement rather than a private taste.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins by leveraging the motion's explicit "modest cost" concession to neutralize CON's supply-side offense. CON's case hinged on arguing that stronger standards would kill marginal projects, but PRO correctly noted this fights the premise and lacked proof that baseline universal design routinely destroys feasibility. PRO also won the alternative comparison: CON's targeted set-aside model failed to address visitability, common-area access, and aging-in-place, which PRO pressed effectively. CON's rhetoric was sharper and the supply tradeoff was plausible, but PRO's lock-in framing and broader demographic benefits provided a cleaner, more motion-consistent path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 response to the supply pressure, which correctly identified that CON was inflating "modest" into "project-killing" without evidence, while contrasting a speculative unbuilt unit against the certain, compounding exclusion of hardwiring barriers into decades-long housing stock.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Its core cost-asymmetry argument (one-percent construction premium versus ten-times-more retrofit costs) was never credibly neutralized; Side B's best answer—that not every unit ever needs the feature—acknowledges but does not defeat the asymmetry for the substantial share of occupants who will need it across a building's lifetime. Side A's critique of targeted approaches' track record (accessible units sitting empty, stigmatized, badly placed) was a live argument throughout, and Side B's proposed fix—better registries, priority matching, regulated placement—was speculative compared to observed behavior. Crucially, Side B honestly admitted in A2 of its Rebuttal 2 that it could not cite a jurisdiction where accessibility standards measurably reduced housing starts. That admission materially weakened its supply-suppression claim, leaving it theoretical. Side A also answered the scope question (Q1 from B) with a productive narrowing: the mandate covers step-free entries, wider doorways, reinforced walls, reachable controls, and basic bathroom maneuvering—not full customization. Side B's response that even this core changes layouts in small apartments was fair but insufficient to overcome the foundational cost logic. Side B did land a real point—PRO's own case still requires targeted programs for individualized needs, so the mandate layers a universal premium on top of targeting rather than replacing it—but Side A's "cheap structural foundation makes later adaptations simple rather than catastrophic" reframe partially answered this. Side A's rhetorical framing ("architect's drawings versus the emergency room") was memorable and advanced the substantive argument rather than substituting for it. Both sides were epistemically disciplined; neither deployed fake precision. Side A's arguments progressed and narrowed the debate more cleanly across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's attack on the targeted approach's track record in Rebuttal 1 [A2.4–A2.5]—accessible units sitting empty while demand is oversubscribed, stigmatized, awkwardly placed, allocated through systems users describe as adversarial—was the most decisive exchange. Side B's response across later turns was to propose fixing the targeting system, but that answer was speculative and implicitly conceded the current system fails. This kept "targeted policy has a demonstrable failure pattern" as a live argument all the way to the closing, substantially undermining Side B's precision alternative.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully defending the cost-benefit asymmetry of universal design. Side A proved that building a baseline foundation prevents catastrophic structural retrofit costs later. Side B effectively argued that housing margins are cumulative and that a baseline still requires some individualized retrofitting, but Side A cleanly resolved this by explaining that the baseline makes those later retrofits cosmetic rather than structural. Side B's defense of a 'targeted' alternative also faltered against Side A's critique of its allocation failures.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A4.1 and A4.4 successfully neutralizing Side B's claim that universal design is redundant because individualized retrofits are still needed, clarifying that the universal baseline transforms those later retrofits from catastrophic structural changes into simple, cheap additions.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) wins by establishing a clearer structural advantage: the severe cost asymmetry between building accessible foundations during construction versus crisis retrofits later. When Side A pressed on scope and supply impacts, Side B effectively narrowed the mandate to a core package that defuses the space and cost inflation argument while preserving the long-term savings claim. Side B also successfully pressured Side A’s targeted alternative, highlighting the documented failures of segregated accessible units (stigma, misallocation, empty inventory) that administrative registries do not easily fix. Side A’s cumulative feasibility argument was theoretically sound but conceded empirical ground under pressure, and its reliance on fixing allocation systems felt less decisive than Side B’s structural baseline. Side B’s closing crystallized the clash memorably without introducing new claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered the scope question by distinguishing a universal foundation from full customization, neutralizing Side A’s space and cost inflation, and then weighed the targeted alternative’s real-world allocation failures against the structural simplicity of building the baseline once.
