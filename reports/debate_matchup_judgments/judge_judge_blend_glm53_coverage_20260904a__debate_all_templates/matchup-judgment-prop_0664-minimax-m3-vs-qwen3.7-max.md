- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B built and defended the cleaner path to victory. Its core timing argument—that economic tools arrive after the decisive 72-hour window while forward-deployed hardware operates inside it—was never neutralized. Side A's most important strategic move, claiming economic deterrence is "more credible precisely because it doesn't require sacrificing American lives," was turned sharply against it by B's Q1. B correctly identified this as advertising American non-intervention, which reduces deterrent value rather than enhancing it. A's Rebuttal 2 response ("avoiding nuclear war isn't weakness") sidestepped the logical contradiction rather than resolving it. A's security-dilemma argument had genuine force but was only partly answered; B's rejoinder—that absent forward presence the easy window stays open, so deterrence has already conceded the dilemma—was persuasive even if not airtight. The Crimea analogy was plausible and A's "false equivalence" counter was underdeveloped. B's clipped opening was a real execution cost, but B rebuilt its key points effectively across later rounds and progressively narrowed the clash around the timing and credibility clashes it owned. A's closing repeated rather than sharpened, while B's closing crystallized the decisive exchange cleanly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 pressure question ("if economic deterrence is more credible precisely because the U.S. won't risk American lives, doesn't that advertise Washington will stand down?") was the most decisive exchange. A had handed B the opening by emphasizing non-escalatory credibility; B exploited it to show that signal is an invitation, not a deterrent. A never fully closed this gap in Rebuttal 2 or the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): A won because it made the cleaner comparative case that deterrence over Taiwan depends on the immediate military window, while economic deterrence alone is slower, less credible, and already priced into Beijing's decision.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's most decisive rebuttal was turning B's claim that economic deterrence is more credible because it avoids risking lives and escalation: A argued that this signals Washington will sanction rather than fight, making invasion less costly than a military denial posture would.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the timeline of a Taiwan scenario (hours/days vs. months) and brilliantly turning Side A's core argument against it. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the cleaner and more consistently pressed path to victory through the fait accompli/timeline argument: if an amphibious assault takes days and economic sanctions take months, the economic deterrence tool operates on the wrong timescale to physically stop the invasion decision. CON's best counter—that entanglement works upstream of the invasion clock by making the aftermath intolerable—is plausible but abstract. PRO's denial mechanism has a more concrete causal story. PRO also landed an effective rebuttal with the Ukraine analogy in Rebuttal 2: removing tripwires and relying on economic deterrence did not stop Putin, which directly damaged CON's core case for "flexibility." CON's responses—arguing Ukraine gave Europe weeks to mobilize—were adequate but did not fully neutralize the point. CON had genuine live arguments: the security spiral/counter-investment concern (PLA building hypersonics to saturate distributed defenses) was never fully answered by PRO, and the "simultaneously invisible yet dense enough" contradiction in CON's closing was a creative late pressure. CON's Cuban Missile Crisis example and "worst failure modes" framing were rhetorically strong and historically grounded. However, CON's escalation arguments felt somewhat theoretical against PRO's repeated concrete mechanism: you cannot cross the strait if the fleet is sunk before it arrives. PRO answered the A1/A2 format requirement correctly and maintained focus throughout. The margin is real but not dominant—CON ran a strong, disciplined case that kept multiple clashes live to the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Ukraine analogy in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange. PRO argued that Ukraine demonstrated removing tripwires and relying on economic threats invites aggression rather than preventing it—directly targeting CON's "flexibility" as a virtue. CON's response (that Europe gained weeks to coordinate and arm defenders) acknowledged the analogy's force but reframed rather than defeated it, leaving PRO's version of the lesson more persuasive on balance.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate. B built a strong security-spiral and preemption-pressure case, especially the claim that denial networks must be both dense enough to work and visible enough to provoke countermeasures. But A kept the comparison closer to the motion: economic deterrence alone is slow punishment against a possible rapid fait accompli, while forward denial changes the military feasibility of invasion before it begins. B never fully answered why economic entanglement would deter a leadership it described as driven by national rejuvenation, and its late Cuban Missile Crisis example was a new closing add-on that deserved limited weight.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's strongest exchange was turning B's tripwire argument: if Beijing is willing to attack U.S. and allied bases merely to stop missile deployments, it is hard to believe slower economic penalties would be the stronger deterrent. That sharpened the central physical denial versus economic punishment comparison.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B wins by exposing critical contradictions in Side A's case. B effectively demonstrated that economic deterrence operates too slowly to prevent a rapid military fait accompli, and cleanly turned A's own Ukraine analogy by noting that the absence of tripwires and reliance on economic threats failed to stop Putin. A's defense—that lacking tripwires in Ukraine at least bought time to impose costs—functionally conceded that the territory would still be lost, cementing B's victory on the core metric of conflict prevention.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first response in Rebuttal 2 (B4.2) turning A's pressure question about Ukraine, brilliantly pointing out that avoiding tripwires there resulted in an invasion, which destroyed A's core premise.
