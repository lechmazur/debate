### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built the more rigorous case by exposing the motion's core administrability problem: the same truthful, mixed-purpose timer can both manage real scarcity and increase conversion, making "mainly designed to pressure spending" an unworkable, motive-based test. A's "remedy mismatch" argument—that hiding the countdown leaves the underlying deadline intact while making it less legible—went largely unanswered and was reinforced in A4.4, which showed PRO's proposed "authentic constraint vs. arbitrary platform-created hold" distinction collapses because virtually all scarcity mechanisms (seat holds, courier windows, promo periods) are platform-designed yet manage genuine capacity. PRO (B) offered an intuitively appealing manipulation narrative and pressed a good Q1 on obstructive timers, but its Rebuttal 2 answer (B4.1) reasserted a distinction A had already shown collapses, and it never squarely engaged A's alternative remedy (targeting deception, resetting clocks, hidden fees directly) despite A raising it repeatedly and querying it directly in Pressure Questions. B's closing mostly restated its framing rather than resolving this live tension. Both sides suffered clipped speeches, but the substantive content lost was minor tail material, not core arguments, so this only marginally affects the comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4), showing that PRO's "authentic external constraint vs. arbitrary platform-created hold" test collapses because restaurant hours, seat-hold lengths, and courier windows are all platform choices that still manage real scarcity—directly neutralizing PRO's answer to the Q1 pressure question and exposing the remedy mismatch at the heart of the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by making the decisive clash legible: the motion’s motive-based default ban is vague under mixed purposes, and muting truthful timers is a remedy mismatch that leaves abusive deadlines intact while hiding when they strike. PRO’s dark-pattern intuition was strong, but its “external vs platform-created” / “neutral vs alarm” test never became workable, and it underweighted real perishability costs. A’s clipped open/close cost polish but not the comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R2 reply that a truthful countdown can report an illegitimate rule, so regulate the obstructive deadline rather than conceal expiry (A4.1/A4.6), undercutting PRO’s pressure framing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing a persistent remedy mismatch in PRO's proposal: removing visible countdowns does not remove the underlying arbitrary deadlines; it merely makes them invisible, leaving consumers surprised and less informed. CON also successfully undermined PRO's "mainly designed" standard as subjective and unadministrable, showing that mixed motives are the norm and that platform-designed constraints (holds, promotions, hours) still reflect genuine scarcity. PRO's "external vs. arbitrary" distinction collapsed under CON's attack, and CON's alternative—targeting false scarcity, deceptive resets, and unreasonable deadlines directly—provided a cleaner, more enforceable path to consumer protection without suppressing truthful, decision-relevant urgency.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4) introduced the decisive line that "the timer is not the deadline," showing that suppressing countdown cues by default leaves abusive cart-expiry rules intact while simply making them less legible to users, undermining PRO's claim that default removal effectively protects consumer autonomy.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO built a more precise, defensible framework: preserve truthful, deadline-tracking cues while excluding presentation designed mainly to provoke impulse (resetting timers, out-of-context "X people viewing" style scarcity). CON's central objection—that this distinction is "economically unworkable"—was tested directly in the pressure round, and CON's own answer (Q2) conceded that resetting/irrelevant cues should not be default, effectively accepting PRO's line while trying to relabel it as ordinary fraud law. PRO seized on this in Rebuttal 2, sharpening the dilemma CON never resolved in its closing, which mostly repeated the "fabricated vs. truthful-and-effective" binary without addressing the truthful-but-misleading category PRO kept pressing. CON's strongest surviving point—that static timestamps impose more cognitive friction than live countdowns on mobile users—was never fully neutralized by PRO, keeping CON's win path partially alive and preventing a larger margin. PRO's clipped closing is a real execution cost that also limits the margin, but the substantive clash was resolved more clearly in PRO's favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Questions (B3.1/B3.2) forced CON to admit resetting or platform-wide irrelevant scarcity cues should not be default, which PRO then leveraged in Rebuttal 2 to show CON had implicitly conceded the very information/pressure distinction it called "unworkable," and CON's closing never resolved that bind.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the motion narrow and decisive: preserve real operational urgency, reject pressure-first defaults. Its functional test (tracks the user's option, persists only while real, proportionate salience) repeatedly answered A's "unworkable intent" attack without banning discounts, holds, or batching. A landed real force on mobile salience, cognitive friction, and adult agency, but too often collapsed the case into defending any effective timer and overclaimed that neutral timestamps destroy coordination. B's risk comparison—platforms redesign cheaply; users audit mid-purchase expensively—was the cleaner voting issue. Mild cost to B for the clipped closing; not enough to flip.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 / PQ answers: objective functional test plus offer-vs-presentation split, forcing A either to permit resetting/irrelevant cues or concede B's distinction.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO successfully defended a workable distinction between truthful operational urgency and manufactured pressure theatre, repeatedly showing that CON's core examples of dynamic discounts, fare holds, and courier batching were preserved under the motion. CON never proved that these operational benefits require pressure-first design as the default, retreating instead to the claim that the distinction collapses—a claim PRO rebutted with a concrete functional test and by noting that mixed motives do not erase dominant purpose. CON's reliance on existing fraud law also failed to engage the proposition's target of truthful but manipulative cues, while PRO's risk comparison and framework held up through the pressure questions and closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 absorption of CON's examples, where PRO demonstrated that fare holds, batching windows, and genuine scarcity are explicitly preserved by the motion, forcing CON to defend the stronger claim that dynamic commerce requires pressure-first presentation by default rather than merely accurate, salient operational information.
