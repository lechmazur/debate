### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side B won primarily through three durable arguments that A never fully neutralized. First, the "third-party to contract" point: A's best response was that the agreement shields the donor's own identity rather than extinguishing the child's rights, but this sidesteps B's core claim that a private deal between clinic and donor cannot generate binding authority over a separate person who never existed at signing. A never gave a clean principled account of how that authority works. Second, A's acceptance of prospective-only reform was repeatedly exploited by B and never escaped the trap. A's prospective/retroactive distinction is legally coherent, but B's counter—"reliance on an overbroad promise is not the same as reliance on a legitimate one"—was sharper and directly addressed the distinction rather than talking past it. Third, the motion's "even if" structure correctly foreclosed A's supply argument as the decisive factor; A spent real effort on supply consequences but never reframed why those costs should override the right as stated in the proposition. A's strongest contributions were the non-identifying registries alternative (concrete and specific), the "no pre-existing positive entitlement to a stranger's name" framing (a clean liberal counter), and the bodily-consent-retroactivity argument. These were good but B answered each: agency over curated narratives, registries as static and infantilizing, and the overbroad-promise counter. B maintained a consistent framework across all rounds, its pressure questions exposed real burdens, and its closing crystallized the existing clash cleanly rather than introducing new material. A's closing was rhetorically polished but largely recapped without resolving the live vulnerabilities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's exploitation of A's prospective-only concession was the most decisive exchange. When A accepted that future donor-conceived adults would receive identifying information, B correctly argued this concedes the interest is legitimate—and then B's closing line that "reliance on an overbroad promise is not the same as reliance on a legitimate one" directly answered A's retroactivity distinction rather than ignoring it. A never recovered a clean principled basis for why the right exists for one cohort but not another.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Both sides debated competently, but PRO held the stronger path to victory after the full exchange. PRO's core moral claim — that the state cannot legitimately seal a citizen's own biological origins by pre-birth contract, and that reliance built on such an arrangement is reliance on an illegitimate arrangement — survived the exchange without being squarely neutralized. CON's best responses were the adoption-records turn (showing even sealed adoptions were opened prospectively) and the "what a name uniquely adds" argument (the ability to locate and legally claim against someone who never consented), both of which were sharp. But CON's retroactive-vs.-prospective-reform distinction, while clean, implicitly concedes the right for future cases while denying it for existing donor-conceived adults — a gap CON never adequately justified. PRO's response that "transition protections for donors are possible; permanent concealment is not" was underdeveloped but still pointed at this asymmetry. CON's weakest moment was the "everyone is born into circumstances they didn't choose" reply to the consent argument, which is a non-answer to PRO's point that no third-party contract can seal the biological record of the person it creates. PRO's rhetorical framing was consistently stronger — "a contract that binds only those who never signed it is not a contract; it is a restraint" — and the closing crystallized the existing clash cleanly rather than widening it. The margin is real but not dominant; CON's rebuttal work was genuinely competitive, especially the adoption-records turn.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's adoption-records turn in Rebuttal 2 [A4.3–A4.4] was the most technically precise single exchange: CON took PRO's own analogy and showed that jurisdictions which opened adoption records typically did so prospectively with contact-preference mechanisms, not by retroactively publishing identities — directly undermining PRO's use of adoption as support for retroactive disclosure and reinforcing CON's prospective-reform argument. PRO never answered this turn, which is the closest either side came to a decisive blow, though it was not enough to overcome PRO's stronger fundamental-rights framing throughout the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. A made strong points about donor reliance, privacy, reduced supply, and prospective reform, and later effectively pressed B on whether identifying information enables unwanted contact or legal claims. But B kept the central clash clearer: donor anonymity is not just a contract between adults, because it is a state-backed arrangement that withholds a person's own origins from the only party who never consented. B also answered the supply point with better weighing, arguing that family-formation interests cannot justify legally manufactured ignorance. A's alternatives such as genetic testing and registries reduced some practical harms but did not fully answer B's identity and equal-access claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive rebuttal was that donor contracts cannot permanently bind the resulting adult who never signed them, and that genetic databases or voluntary registries are patchwork substitutes rather than a real legal right to origins.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): CON wins by successfully narrowing the clash to retroactive breach versus prospective reform and effectively turning PRO’s claim about “legal parentage recognition” into a liability. While PRO mounted a principled rights-based case, CON demonstrated that practical needs are increasingly met by genetic testing and registries, leaving identifying disclosure primarily as a tool for contact and legal claims against donors who relied on anonymity guarantees. CON’s sharper distinction between donor anonymity and sealed adoption records, combined with cleaner weighing of supply harms against a narrowing practical gap, provided the more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2, which directly answered PRO’s pressure questions by distinguishing knowledge from contact, then turned PRO’s own “legal parentage recognition” point to show that identifying information imposes unagreed obligations, not just knowledge. This effectively collapsed PRO’s rights claim into a retroactive privacy breach and clarified the prospective/retroactive split.
