### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides built coherent, competently argued cases, but CON's rebuttal chain was more surgical and left PRO's weakest claim exposed. PRO's asymmetry argument (irreversible neurotoxicity vs. amortizable debt) was rhetorically strong but rested on an unsupported claim that capital debt is funded through "separate accounting" and doesn't compete with other health spending. CON directly punctured this in Rebuttal 2 ("debt service is paid from the same ratepayer revenue"), and PRO's closing never specifically answered that point, instead simply re-asserting the asymmetry framing. CON also landed a sharper structural point that the motion's own "replace rather than rely on chemical treatment" dichotomy is porous, since PRO's own plan still requires treatment during the years-long construction window and cannot eliminate interior solder/brass sources — a point PRO only partially answered by minimizing it as "brief." CON's pressure questions and answers (A1/A2 correctly formatted) were used effectively, including a clever reductio turning PRO's "monitoring is post-hoc" critique back against replacement's own reliance on inspection and sampling. PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions were serviceable but leaned on the same unproven fiscal claim that CON had already begun dismantling. Overall CON made the decisive clash (fiscal opportunity cost and the motion's internal tension) clearer and left the debate easier to decide in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that "debt service is paid from the same ratepayer revenue," directly refuting PRO's "separate accounting" claim, which PRO's closing never squarely answered.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO won by keeping the live clash on irreversible neurotoxicity versus amortizable debt and by forcing CON to defend an indefinite chemical barrier whose failures are discovered only after exposure. Rhetoric and weighing made PRO’s route easier to decide.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s first rebuttal turn treating monitoring as post-hoc bio-monitoring ([B2.1]/[B2.2]), then crystallizing it in R2/closing against CON’s “intensify monitoring” safety net.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): CON wins a close clash by keeping two decisive points live: replacement is not "zero forever" – it leaves solder/brass, creates excavation spikes, and still requires treatment, testing and filters during years of construction – and the proposition's "even when significant rate increases or new debt" burden forces the same revenue that funds pathogen control, PFAS response and affordability to be diverted. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but PRO's rebuttals did more real work. PRO's key move—distinguishing a bounded, auditable, finite construction process from a perpetual, invisible chemical process that must be re-proven every day—directly answered CON's "competence paradox" and turned it back on CON's own model (a city too weak to dose chemicals has no safety floor under the status quo either). PRO also neutralized the Flint example and the lead-paint "opportunity cost" argument, the latter of which CON never revisited after PRO's dismissal. CON's strongest and most persistent point—that significant rate increases can cause shutoffs that erase any benefit of replacement—was real and pressed hard (including via the "zero benefit" concession CON repeated in later rounds), but CON largely reasserted this point rather than engaging with PRO's specific financing-design counters (lifeline rates, amortized debt, assistance) or explaining why those tools categorically fail. By the closing, PRO's framing—permanent poison versus a manageable, redesigned bill—was sharper and better resolved the live clash than CON's repeated equity assertions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.2) answering CON's competence-paradox pressure question: distinguishing perpetual, hidden chemical dosing from bounded, inspectable construction, and showing that CON's own paradox undermines the status quo it defends, was the most decisive exchange, since CON never fully rebutted this distinction in the following round.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner decisive path: chemical control is perpetual, fragile operational risk management of a remaining poison source, while replacement is a bounded capital asset that ends the hazard. CON's strongest live weapons (shutoffs/affordability and the competence paradox) were real but ultimately answered as design failures and as distinctions between invisible daily dosing versus auditable block-by-block construction with temporary mitigations; CON never squared why residual fragility plus decades of dual reliance is preferable once the proposition already permits phased debt and assistance. PRO kept the motion's "even when costly" clause intact without swapping the question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers distinguishing perpetual chemical competence from bounded construction and reframing shutoffs as tariff failure rather than reason to keep lead.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won the permanence clash. Chemical protection requires perfect perpetual dosing and collapses when it lapses - Flint illustrates fragility, not stability - while replacement is a bounded, inspectable capital asset whose cost can be amortized. CON's strongest affordability/shutoff pressure was met with lifeline rates and phased debt, and CON conceded incremental replacement is possible and that resilient scale must be continuously maintained during a decades-long transition, leaving its own model reliant on the competence it attacks. PRO's auditability vs daily-invisibility distinction and temporary filter/flush mitigations left the source-removal imperative live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.1/A4.2 turn on the competence paradox - framing corrosion control as invisible perpetual and failure-prone systemwide versus replacement as block-by-block auditable construction - plus A2.5's concrete spike mitigations directly neutralized CON's core construction-risk attack.
