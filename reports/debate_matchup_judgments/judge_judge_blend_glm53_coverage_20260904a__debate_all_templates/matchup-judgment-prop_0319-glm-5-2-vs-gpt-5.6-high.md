A's museum/library analogy was strong but B successfully distinguished those institutions by explicit public mission and accountable governance, a disanalogy A never fully closed. A's best point—that B's own subsidy tools rest on the same underlying cultural judgment—was clever but answered adequately by B's public-budget-versus-externalized-cost framework. B's rebuttal 1 was clipped for length, a real execution cost, but the argument was fully recovered and sharpened in later rounds, so the overall path to victory remained live and was better resolved by B by the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's distinction between transparent, publicly-funded cultural subsidies and regulatory favoritism that externalizes costs onto neighbors/patrons (B4.3, reiterated in B5.3-B5.5) was the most decisive exchange, since A's closing attempt to blur this line never actually answered why the cost-bearing location is irrelevant.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and the central dilemma—"if the designation changes nothing it's empty, if it changes outcomes it overrides competing interests"—was pressed hard by B and never fully escaped by A. But A's answer (a rebuttable duty to prefer less-destructive-but-equally-effective options, with safety/nuisance thresholds untouched) is a coherent middle position that B's rebuttal only partially dented by relabeling it "weighting with softer language." A also more directly answered the line-drawing objection with a testable, non-merit-based criterion (recurring programming, scale, access) and appeal rights, whereas B's "proportionality already does this" answer struggled to explain why cumulative, cross-agency erosion (zoning + licensing + policing each individually proportionate but collectively fatal) is caught by existing proportionality review — a gap A identified early and B's closing conceded is available only via a separate "cumulative impact assessment" tool, which is itself close to what A was arguing for. B's reversibility rebuttal ("bookstores and cinemas are irreversible too, so category expands to everything") is a decent generalization point, but A's closing didn't fully need to answer it since B never showed why extending some protection to other high-diffuse-value low-margin cultural spaces would be bad — B treated slippery-slope as self-evidently disqualifying without much argument. On balance A's framework survived the sharpest pressure (Q1/Q2 exchange) with a more precise, administrable answer, while B's strongest structural argument (weighting-in-disguise) was blunted but not fully defeated. This gives A a clear, non-dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over A3.1/B3.1 (does "considering cultural value" either add nothing or covertly override competing interests) and A's A4.1 answer distinguishing "duty to avoid needless destruction among equally effective options" from "priority over safety/nuisance" was the pivotal clash; B's B4.1 relabeling this as "weighting with softer language" was a strong comeback but didn't fully close the gap A opened on cumulative cross-agency effects.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A built the clearer route by treating the motion as a duty to count avoidable cultural loss and cumulative effects across agencies, not immunity, while preserving safety and nuisance tools; B's dilemma and favoritism attacks landed but overread "weight" as automatic override and never fully answered why siloed proportionality alone fixes the undercounting PRO identified. A answered pressures more cleanly with remedy-shifting vs standard-lowering, making the vote easier for the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A4.1-A4.2 reply dissolving B's override dilemma: weight changes remedy allocation and less-destructive options among lawful choices without waiving thresholds.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully distinguishing between lowering safety standards and altering the remedy to avoid cumulative cultural destruction. Side A's argument about siloed agencies and cumulative impact effectively answered Side B's claim that existing proportionality tests are sufficient. Side B's core "dilemma" (that the designation either does nothing or overrides legitimate safety/nuisance concerns) was persuasively dismantled by Side A's explanation that forcing officials to choose less destructive, equally safe alternatives is the exact purpose of the infrastructure designation. Side B became somewhat repetitive in later speeches, failing to fully neutralize Side A's "remedy vs. standard" distinction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answers the pressure questions by explaining how a rebuttable duty changes the remedy (e.g., tailored hours instead of blanket curfews) without waiving structural safety standards, effectively collapsing Side B's central dilemma.
