### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON built a coherent, escalating case: Gulf states already run vast legal corridors, and those corridors leak precisely because enforcement (wage protection, blacklisting, removal threat) is weak — so adding another corridor without fixing enforcement just widens the leak. CON also drove a sharp wedge into PRO's mechanism: PRO's own "penalty" for overstay (losing the next season's slot) only bites if removal/bans are actually enforced, which means PRO's corridor-first plan quietly depends on the very deportation credibility it claims to deprioritize. PRO never squarely resolved this contradiction; its closing pivoted to a strong rhetorical line ("vacancy is the dam breaking again," turning CON's "price of credibility" into a "confession") but this was more framing than a rebuttal of the underlying logical point. PRO's answers to pressure questions were also vaguer than CON's — "enough contracts to serve the labor-seeking share" and reusing CON's own enforcement tools without explaining why they'd suddenly work sidestepped the concrete numeric and mechanistic challenge CON posed. CON's sunk-cost mobilization argument (advertised slots pulling more people into Agadez than the lottery can absorb) was a plausible, well-integrated response to PRO's sequencing claim and went largely unanswered in substance. The debate was close and well-matched, but CON's tighter logical chain and more direct handling of pressure gave it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question (A3.1) asking why a worker wouldn't overstay to bank tenfold wages and still return legally next season, combined with CON's Rebuttal 2 point that PRO's "penalty" (losing the next season) presupposes exactly the deportation/enforcement credibility PRO wants to deprioritize — PRO's answer (A1 in B4.1) never resolved this circularity.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): B won the sequencing clash. A strongly showed that existing Gulf corridors leak and that North African transit is not wholly labor-driven, but much of A's solution relied on employer sanctions, blacklisting, and re-entry bans rather than proving mass deportation should be the priority. B could retain those enforcement tools while making a legal slot conditional on timely return. A repeatedly assumed deprioritizing mass deportation meant overstayers could return legally without penalty, despite B explicitly denying that. B therefore offered the cleaner comparative mechanism: legal access creates something workers and employers can lose, while deportation-first leaves persistent labor demand to refill informal jobs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer that overstaying forfeits the next legal season was decisive. A argued that this penalty requires prioritized deportation, but never established why recorded overstay and denial of re-entry could not enforce it without mass removals. This weakened A's central claim that corridors cannot become valuable until deportations come first.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by grounding the debate in the practical economic realities of the Gulf labor market. While Side B makes a strong theoretical case for enforcement-first to create deterrence, Side A effectively dismantles this by pointing out that Gulf states and employers will not tolerate the stalled projects and vacancies that Side B admits are the 'price of credibility.' Side A's argument that you cannot audit or enforce compliance in a market you refuse to adequately supply with legal labor is highly persuasive and survives Side B's pressure. Side A also maintains excellent rhetorical framing throughout, making the structural clash clear and decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing rebuttal targeting Side B's admission that raids would cause short-term vacancies. Side A sharply points out that Gulf economies cannot afford empty scaffolds, meaning political and economic pressure would immediately collapse Side B's enforcement-first sequence, rendering the deportation strategy unworkable in practice.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-reasoned cases, but the decisive late clash favored CON (Side A). A's "shadow bench"/invisibility argument—that biometric permits, payroll blocks, and exit-scan systems can only bind workers who are already inside the formal system, while the cheaper undocumented hire the market actually wants never appears in that system—was pressed hard in Rebuttal 2 and Closing and was never squarely resolved by PRO. B's closing simply reasserted the "price flip" mechanism (fines, blacklists, site stoppage) without explaining how those consequences get triggered absent the very detection/enforcement (raids) that corridors are supposed to deprioritize, leaving a live, unrebutted structural gap in PRO's mechanism. PRO's parallel attack on CON—that mass deportation is already routine yet refill happens, exposing deterrence-first as empirically hollow and prohibitively costly—was real and sharp, but A's reframe (deportation is currently too uncertain/cheap rather than too small in volume, so intensify certainty rather than abandon it) was a plausible, if underdeveloped, answer that blunted the force of that critique. On balance, A's final exchange landed the cleaner, more damaging and less-answered blow, giving it a modest but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.5–A4.8), pressing that payroll blocks, exit scans, and blacklists "only bind people already visible" and that "detection is the raid you want to demote" — a point B's closing never directly neutralized.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by locating the durable cause of repeated irregular migration in employer demand and pricing rather than merely the removable worker stock. Side A persuasively warned that legal and illegal channels can coexist, but repeatedly treated prioritizing corridors as abandoning detection or targeted enforcement. The proposition only contrasts corridors with mass deportations, and B showed how permits, employer sanctions, workplace controls, and targeted returns could coexist while addressing refilling. A never adequately explained why worker removal would change employer incentives or prevent rapid replacement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.5 was decisive: B directly answered both pressure questions, distinguished targeted enforcement from mass deportation, and turned A's concession that irregular hiring is cheaper than penalties into a reason to regulate employers. A's database critique exposed an implementation burden but did not establish that detection requires mass deportation.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully reframing the core driver of irregular migration as an employer pricing problem rather than a mere worker supply problem. By demonstrating that mass deportations fail to alter the employer's calculus and simply recycle the labor pool, A neutralized B's "removal first" doctrine. While B landed a strong analytical blow regarding the limits of digital enforcement on the invisible "shadow bench," A's comprehensive mechanism—fast legal corridors combined with strict employer liability and targeted digital enforcement—offered a more plausible and sustainable path to reducing irregularity than B's reliance on blunt, repetitive mass raids. A's closing effectively crystallized this clash, turning B's own concession about jobs refilling into a fatal flaw for the CON case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it answered B's pressure questions by explaining how digital enforcement and employer liability (site stoppage, payroll blocks) rewrite the risk calculus, effectively neutralizing B's "stacking" claim by showing how a well-designed legal corridor substitutes for the shadow bench.
