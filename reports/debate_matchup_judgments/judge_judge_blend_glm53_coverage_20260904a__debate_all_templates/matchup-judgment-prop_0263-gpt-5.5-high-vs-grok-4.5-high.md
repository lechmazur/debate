- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by effectively reframing the debate around consumer reliance and market failure.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's attack on the tax write-down defense, pointing out that generating private profit by intentionally destroying public access is exactly the market failure the motion resolves, effectively countering Side A's "capital reallocation" argument.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): PRO won by isolating the decisive flaw in CON's case: tax write-downs and accounting removals often require non-exploitation, which directly guts CON's assurance that strong titles simply migrate elsewhere. PRO successfully reframed originals as public-facing releases branded to attract subscribers and creators, not mere warehouse inventory, and showed that market discipline fails for obscure works because viewers learn of removal too late and affected stakeholders are not all subscription decision-makers. CON's refusal to accept even a moderate floor, and its concession that some works would be buried to free capital, made its position seem extreme and prioritized corporate accounting over concrete cultural access. PRO's weighing—protecting existing works and creator reliance against speculative future production losses—proved cleaner after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer on tax write-downs, which exposed that CON's "strong titles find new homes" defense collapses when accounting incentives explicitly require preventing any exploitation, forcing CON to defend destroying public access for private tax benefit.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON)): PRO won the central clash on tax write-downs requiring non-exploitation. A kept focus on originals (not general licensed catalog), showed market discipline fails for post-erasure access, and reframed upfront rights costs as honest budgeting. CON conceded in B4 that forced availability must destroy the accounting upside and maintained a zero-minimum absolute, leaving A's pressure Q2 unanswered and weakening its own "strong titles find new homes" claim.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling the practical mechanics of Side B's proposal. By exposing that Side B's "ordinary consumption" standard was legally unworkable, Side A proved the mandate would either be arbitrary or result in forced indefinite hosting, while offering narrower, more proportionate remedies for the harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing perfectly crystallized the fatal flaw in Side B's standard, demonstrating that a window based on "ordinary consumption" cannot govern a real catalog without either stranding late-starters or creating a rolling completion right.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A established a clear, sustained path to victory by identifying a specific abuse—originals erased mid-run for tax or licensing gains after being marketed as subscription draws—and showing that narrower remedies (tax fixes, disclosure, secondary licensing) all fail to restore the subscriber's actual lost access. Side B landed strong workability objections, but three clipped speeches and an inability to neutralize A's core comparative weighing left their route intact. A's superior execution, format discipline, and ability to crystallize the clash around re-linking payment to product secured the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and closing sequence demonstrating that CON's alternative remedies—conditioning tax benefits, clearer disclosure, or early relicensing—each leave the primary harm untouched because the paying subscriber still loses mid-story access to the original they were sold. This reframed the debate so that the minimum period appeared as the only proportionate, complete fix.
