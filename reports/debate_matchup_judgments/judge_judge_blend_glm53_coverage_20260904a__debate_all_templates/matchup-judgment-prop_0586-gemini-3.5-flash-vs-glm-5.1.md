### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the stronger cumulative case through three decisive exchanges. First, CON's challenge to the empirical premise—that large minimum wage hikes cause catastrophic job losses—was never adequately answered; PRO's "large vs. moderate" distinction was valid but did not rebut CON's core claim that employers adjust through turnover, prices, and productivity. Second, and most decisively, CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's geographic-variation question was clean and unrebutted: 29 states and dozens of cities already tailor minimum wages to local conditions through ordinary sub-national legislation, eliminating the need for new sectoral institutions to achieve geographic precision. PRO never answered this. Third, the California fast-food board example—PRO's strongest concrete evidence—was effectively turned by CON: industry forced a referendum that modified the council's authority before it convened, illustrating the "more veto points, not fewer" argument. PRO's counter ($20/hour for 500k workers is a real outcome) had merit, but CON's broader structural point about multiplying opposition venues remained live. PRO's strengths—the "sledgehammer vs. precision instrument" framing, the cold-comfort job-loss reframe, and the tripartite structure defense—were well-executed but couldn't neutralize CON's unrebutted federalism point or the veto-points logic. CON maintained consistent argumentative discipline and made the debate easier to decide in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's geographic-variation pressure question: pointing out that 29 states and dozens of cities already set minimum wages above the federal floor through ordinary legislation was the most decisive single exchange. It directly neutralized PRO's core framing that a blunt minimum wage cannot handle geographic variation, without requiring any new institutional infrastructure—and PRO never answered it.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate by making the sectoral-board mechanism look too idealized for the US context. A had the cleaner intuitive case on tailoring wages by industry and landed a real hit on B's reliance on evidence about moderate minimum wage hikes, but A never fully proved that boards can reliably identify sustainable wage levels, avoid capture, cover fragmented workers, and implement raises faster than ordinary law. B's capture, delay, coverage, and veto-point arguments remained live, and B made the comparative choice clearer: minimum wage law is simple, broad, and empirically less damaging than A assumed, while sectoral bargaining adds procedural vulnerability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive exchange was turning A's California fast-food example from proof of bypassing gridlock into proof that sectoral boards create new venues for industry opposition, then linking that to capture and delay across every sector.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully challenging the empirical premise that large minimum wage hikes cause devastating job losses, explaining concrete adjustment mechanisms (turnover, prices, productivity) that PRO never fully neutralized. CON’s federalism reframe directly answered the “blunt instrument” critique by showing geographic tailoring already exists without new bureaucracy. PRO’s defense that the threat forced a $20/hr compromise was rhetorically strong but failed to neutralize CON’s structural point about vulnerability to capture and delay.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate primarily because it more directly answers the motion's comparative question. The precision argument—that sectoral standards can calibrate wage floors to industry-specific margins, raising pay aggressively where viable and moderating where closures would otherwise result—is coherent, well-grounded, and never directly refuted on the merits. CON's case is largely an attack on how sectoral bargaining might be administered (cartelization, rigid classifications, representation gaps) rather than a positive defense that flat minimum wage increases would produce fewer employment losses. This is a burden asymmetry: the motion requires a comparative judgment, and CON never seriously argues that blunt national floors outperform calibrated sectoral standards on pay and employment preservation—only that sectoral boards face governance risks. PRO's "imperfect voice beats silence" framing in Rebuttal 2 is the debate's most decisive move, neatly absorbing CON's strongest point (7% union density) while preserving the central advantage of worker input in standard-setting. CON's union carve-out response (A4.1) is plausible and real as a historical phenomenon, but it is speculative as applied to hypothetical US wage boards, and PRO's phased-adjustment answer to the automation question was adequate. CON's multi-sector boundary problem is a genuine concern but is somewhat neutralized by PRO's point that existing complexity already requires analogous jurisdictional rules. The clipped PRO closing is a minor execution cost but does not change the outcome because its core arguments were already crystallized in Rebuttal 2.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's union-density pressure question (A1): reframing 7% density from a weakness into the motivation for sectoral bargaining, arguing the real comparison is "some organized worker voice" versus "no worker voice at all" under flat legislative floors. This directly absorbed CON's strongest empirical point and shifted the weighing to CON's advantage without conceding the broader argument.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by making the cleaner comparative case: sectoral bargaining can calibrate wage floors by industry conditions, phase changes, and avoid the blunt shock of large across-the-board hikes. Side B’s cartelization and complexity critique was coherent and rhetorically sharp, but it leaned heavily on asserted inevitabilities about capture, work rules, and union carve-outs that were not shown to be inherent to wage boards. A’s answers on imperfect worker voice, automation being common to both systems, and flexibility directly addressed the main pressure and preserved the proposition’s core mechanism. A’s clipped closing is an execution cost, but not enough to erase its stronger comparative framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s second rebuttal on automation and representation was most decisive: it reframed B’s strongest attacks as risks present under either regime while arguing sectoral boards have more tools to manage them than a flat minimum wage hike.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully grounding the debate in the specific institutional realities of the US labor market, as required by the proposition. While Side B (PRO) offered a coherent theoretical case for sectoral "precision" over blunt minimum wage shocks, CON effectively demonstrated how US-specific factors—critically low union density, the documented practice of union carve-outs, and the compliance burden of multi-sector classifications on small businesses—would likely turn wage boards into engines of regulatory capture and job loss. CON's concrete examples (e.g., convenience store task-shifting, union leverage tactics) directly answered PRO's representation and boundary arguments, while PRO's reliance on "worker centers" and theoretical buy-in felt less responsive to the live pressure. CON's framing of a flat minimum as a "neutral shield" versus sectoral rules as a "weaponized" compliance maze provided a cleaner, more decisive reason to believe sectoral bargaining would cause greater employment losses in the US context.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over union representation and incentives. PRO argued that low union density makes wage boards necessary and that representatives would include community groups. CON countered with the concrete reality of "union carve-outs," showing how institutional unions actually use regulatory complexity to gain leverage over non-union competitors, directly undermining PRO's claim that worker voices would naturally protect small employers and prevent capture. This clash decisively shifted the employment-loss comparison in CON's favor.
