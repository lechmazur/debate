- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won by successfully holding one precise distinction throughout: policy should target upstream conditions (zoning, labor law, civic infrastructure) without making friendship itself the named policy object. That distinction was clean, principled, and never squarely refuted. The debate's decisive moment came in the Q1/A1 exchange: CON's pressure question forced PRO to name a specific institutional action, and PRO answered "municipal loneliness impact assessments." CON immediately turned that answer back as proof of exactly the bureaucratic overreach it had warned about—friendship as an unmeasurable, politicized metric filtering every land-use decision. PRO's own analogy in B4.2 then inadvertently confirmed CON's distinction: "we do not force people to eat vegetables, but policy does intervene to build grocery stores" is precisely CON's position—address upstream conditions (food access, communal space) without making the relationship behavior the named policy object. PRO's strongest surviving argument—that you cannot marshal political will to solve a crisis you refuse to name—was real but not decisive, because CON correctly noted that "loneliness" and "social isolation" can be named as the crisis without elevating friendship itself to policy-domain status. PRO also never cleanly explained what the motion's specific framing adds beyond the sensible upstream policies both sides already endorsed, which left its core burden largely unmet. CON's argument was tighter, adapted well across rounds, and its framing became sharper rather than merely repetitive as the debate progressed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Q1 forced PRO to commit to "municipal loneliness impact assessments" as the specific institutional action the motion requires. CON's A4.1 immediately turned that answer into evidence of the social-impact bureaucracy CON had been warning about, and separately, PRO's own grocery-store analogy in B4.2 accidentally demonstrated that targeting upstream conditions (food access / communal space) is different from making the behavior itself (eating / friending) the named policy object—exactly the distinction CON had been drawing all along.

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON successfully showed that the motion requires making friendship itself a named policy metric, not merely addressing upstream conditions like zoning or labor law. When PRO was pressed to name a specific action the framing requires, they offered municipal loneliness impact assessments for urban development—precisely the bureaucratic management of social bonds that CON warned would corrupt voluntary intimacy. PROs argument that naming the goal is necessary for political will was compelling but could not overcome the concrete demonstration that the motion dissolves the boundary between public policy and private relationships, a boundary CON showed PRO had already abandoned by analogizing friendship to sleep and diet as policy objects.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: CONs second rebuttal and closing, which seized on PROs loneliness impact assessments as the definitive proof that treating friendship as a policy concern installs a politicized social-impact bureaucracy over land-use decisions, validating CONs central category-error argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) established the debate's controlling frame early and held it throughout: institutions are already shaping friendship outcomes through policy choices—zoning, labor scheduling, infrastructure design—so the real question is not interference vs. neutrality but deliberate vs. accidental policy. CON never adequately answered this. The "just deregulate" counter was internally consistent but undercut by PRO's pointed observation that deregulation is itself a policy choice aimed at social conditions, and CON never supplied a concrete mechanism by which deregulation specifically reverses documented social-connection collapse. CON's strongest running argument—that making friendship an explicit institutional goal inevitably produces HR surveillance and engagement quotas—is a plausible slippery slope but remained speculative throughout; PRO's counter-analogy (libraries measure circulation, not bonding; labor law governs breaks without quizzing who you lunch with) was clean and CON never neutralized it. The "design rationale" distinction PRO introduced in Rebuttal 2 [B4.3–B4.4]—identical bricks, different intent—was genuinely clarifying and went unanswered. CON's "policy requires measurement" pressure was real but PRO deflected it well by separating infrastructure metrics from relationship auditing. PRO's Rebuttal 2 was clipped, a real execution cost, but the visible A1/A2 answers were substantive. CON's closings were cleaner rhetorically but largely repeated the "intimacy vs. bureaucracy" binary PRO had already reframed. Overall, PRO built and defended the stronger comparative path: refusing to act is not neutral, and the motion asks for deliberate good policy over accidental bad policy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 [B2.4–B2.5] answering CON's "just deregulate" argument: pointing out that single-family zoning was itself an active government choice—subsidized mortgages, interstates, mixed-use bans—meaning its loneliness outcome is a direct policy result, and "you cannot repair a policy outcome with less policy; you repair it with better policy." CON never found a clean answer to this across the remainder of the debate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins because they dismantled the bureaucracy strawman, showed institutions already shape friendship outcomes by policy choice, and framed the real alternative as deliberate vs accidental policy after every exchange; B's deregulation reply never neutralized that core comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.1-A2.6 forcing B to defend "just deregulate" as still a policy choice and clarifying infrastructure intent without measurement of relationships.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO won by breaking CON's central "intimacy versus bureaucracy" frame and replacing it with a more accurate distinction between accidental institutional shaping and deliberate, accountable design. PRO convincingly showed that workplaces, cities, and public institutions are already actively structuring adult friendship through zoning, scheduling, and platform design, so the status quo is not a neutral private sphere. PRO's concrete distinction between designing infrastructure for connection (benches that face each other, humane schedules, looping park paths) and managing personal intimacy directly answered CON's slippery-slope concerns about "friendship KPIs." CON's strongest objection—that explicit friendship goals invite measurement and instrumentalization—was blunted by PRO's analogies to existing institutions (libraries measure circulation, not bonding; labor law protects breaks without surveying lunch partners). CON never fully recovered from PRO's challenge that deregulation is itself a policy posture with social consequences, and CON's "spontaneous order" alternative remained thinner and less substantiated than PRO's causal story about policy-driven isolation. While CON mounted a principled and rhetorically sharp defense of privacy, PRO provided the cleaner comparative path: the choice is not policy versus no policy, but accidental bad policy versus deliberate good policy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer in Rebuttal 2 to CON's pressure question about measurement and KPIs (B4.1-B4.2), establishing that infrastructure can be designed to support organic connection without institutions auditing personal relationships—directly analogous to how libraries track circulation rather than social bonding, and labor law protects breaks without monitoring who eats lunch together.
