- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly, but B landed the more damaging blow in Rebuttal 1 by turning A's necessary permit exceptions (for nurses, tradespeople, deliveries, taxis) into an admission that "the ban is not a ban, it is a permission regime." This forced A to spend the rest of the debate defending a "need-based vs cash-based" distinction that B never let go unanswered: B's key point that classification "can refuse her even when she would pay for the space" was never directly rebutted in A's closing, leaving a live, decisive pressure point for B. B also consistently used the same concrete human example (the night nurse/shift worker) to keep pressure on A's exceptions, while its "same leftover asphalt" framing effectively neutralized A's safety/throughput claims by showing both regimes converge on a residual vehicle population in reallocated lanes. A's "default" reframe (burden of justification) was a reasonable conceptual move but stayed abstract and didn't answer the concrete bureaucratic-exclusion critique. B's closing crystallized the clash more sharply and left A's fairness case weaker.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 point that A's permit concessions mean "the ban is not a ban, it is a permission regime" [B2.2], compounded by B's later, unanswered claim that classification "can refuse her even when she would pay for the space" [B4.2], was the most decisive exchange—A never fully closed that gap.

  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON R1/R2 showing PRO's "ban" is a permission regime — once you list delivery vans, hotel shuttles, accessible taxis, loading windows and time-limited worker permits, the street still has vehicles at the crossing and emissions unless separately banned, so the decisive safety/emissions gain over a priced, lane-reallocated residual collapses.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): A built a memorable conceptual frame ("legal right to occupy the core forces design around cars," "velvet rope," "manages decline vs. redesigns the city") and used the pressure-question exchange skillfully, forcing B to concede the residual "should not be zero." But B's rebuttals did more actual damage: B repeatedly showed that A's "clean line" is not clean at all, since the ban still admits disability, freight, waste, construction, and hire-car traffic—requiring the same category-based bureaucracy A criticized in pricing. B also sustained a fairness point A never fully resolved: under a ban, the affordable private commute is forbidden while the wealthy simply become paying passengers (taxi/ride-hail), whereas pricing can be tuned with discounts, off-peak rates, and revenue for the least flexible. Crucially, B held the last word and used it to directly defuse A's closing pivot on the "should not be zero" concession, arguing that a small, price-managed residual does not recreate a "purchased corridor." A's opening being clipped is a minor execution cost. Overall B provided the more resilient, harder-to-refute practical case, while A's strongest material was rhetorical framing that ultimately got answered rather than resolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's repeated point that A's ban still requires official categorization of exceptions (disability, freight, waste, hire cars) — turning the "bright line" into its own permit bureaucracy — combined with B's closing neutralization of A's "should not be zero" concession, was the most decisive exchange.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively neutralizing Side B's core "street geometry" argument. By pointing out that Side B's ban still requires lanes for hire cars, deliveries, and service vehicles, Side A demonstrated that pricing can achieve the same physical redesign and volume targets without creating a massive permit bureaucracy or stranding low-income night workers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2 effectively dismantled Side B's "bright line" by showing that a ban with necessary exceptions just creates a bureaucratic permit system and pushes wealthy drivers into ride-hails, meaning both worlds still require street geometry for motor vehicles.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) kept the decisive distinction alive: the harm is volume/geometry dominance, not the mere legal existence of a private car. PRO's ban still needs disability, freight, and hire-car exceptions, so it is also a permit system that leaves paid motor traffic for the wealthy while forbidding the 3am caregiver's own car. PRO never solved the hire-car substitution problem or named when a low-volume priced street stops being a corridor, while CON showed pricing can hit the same street-space targets, cap hire traffic, and fund transit with progressive discounts. That head-to-head on class and bureaucracy resolved the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to the class and bright-line claims – that a ban forbids the affordable private commute but preserves taxi/ride-hail for the rich, and that disability/service exceptions turn the 'clean ban' into its own permit bureaucracy with motor volume – which B never fully neutralized.
