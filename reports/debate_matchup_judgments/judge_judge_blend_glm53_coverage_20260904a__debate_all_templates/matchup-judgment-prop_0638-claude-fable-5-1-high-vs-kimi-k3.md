### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B won a clear but competitive debate. A persuasively showed that short project cycles reward legible, safer work and consume research time, but B exposed the unresolved comparative problem: block grants do not remove selection or prediction, but relocate them to institutional gatekeepers whose decisions are less portable and obscure individual outsiders. A's formula and external-review answer addressed national allocation without adequately answering internal allocation, aggregate review, or hierarchy lock-in. A's clipped second rebuttal was an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that external institutional review evaluates departmental averages while internal administrators still decide which individual labs survive was decisive. It directly answered A's strongest accountability safeguard and undermined A's claim that block funding merely removes an unnecessary gate rather than replacing it with a less appealable one.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the motion's trade on the table: both systems use peer hiring/tenure, only project grants add a prophecy gate demanding predicted results with preliminary data. That gate explains consensus filtering and time waste, while external retrospective review (Britain REF / Max Planck closures) answers the scaling/patronage charge. CON's strongest pluralism/second-chance frame was materially damaged by the Karikó exchange - a decade of public competitive no's with rescue by industry, which exists under either system - leaving CON to defend accountability cost the motion explicitly prices in.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.2 person vs idea: backward assessment of a record vs forward prophecy of an outcome explains why same peers can spot daring people but average away daring projects, neutralizing A3 Q1 and the risk-aversion contradiction.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Side A wins by successfully distinguishing between evaluating a scientist's past record (which consensus panels can do at hiring/tenure) and predicting an untested idea (which consensus averages out). This directly and decisively answered Side B's strongest pressure question. Side A also effectively weaponized the Kariko example, highlighting that public competitive grants rejected her for a decade, which blunted Side B's pluralism argument. While Side B made excellent points about the dangers of institutional patronage and the risk of administrators purging outliers before review day, Side A's structural critique of project grants as a "prophecy gate" that inherently demands preliminary data remained largely unrefuted. Furthermore, Side A better embraced the motion's explicit concession regarding accountability, whereas Side B spent too much time fighting the premise of the concession itself. Side A's Turn 7 was slightly clipped, but the core answers were delivered clearly before the cutoff.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 to Side B's pressure question about consensus: explaining that judging a person relies on backward-looking evidence of rigor and productivity, while judging a project requires forward-looking prophecy where outliers inherently look implausible to risk-averse panels.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B won by making portability and the asymmetry of default decisive. A compellingly showed that short cycles punish risky work and waste researcher time, but never adequately answered why concentrating funds within institutional hierarchies would protect outsiders. B showed that applying to another institute is a job search, not an equivalent funding appeal, and that competitive non-renewal moves resources without requiring politically difficult institutional closure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal sharply turned A’s boom-bust complaint into proof that competitive funding actually withdraws money, then contrasted automatic non-renewal with block funding’s tendency to continue unless leaders actively dismantle an institution. The follow-up that Mill Hill was largely relocated rather than truly defunded weakened A’s principal closure example.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): CON (A) won the central portability/default clash. PRO showed peer review punishes risk and taxes time, but CON showed block grants convert one chair's no into a final verdict, freeze field mobility, and invert the funding default to automatic continuation. CON answered Q2 with non-renewal (success rates <1/3) and exposed PRO's contradiction between lamenting boom-bust and claiming competition never closes anything. PRO's Mill Hill closure was reframed as relocation, leaving no strong example of block defunding. The offered compromise of longer portable investigator awards plus lotteries undercut the necessity of the motion's trade of less accountability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4's asymmetry-of-default rebuttal — money stops unless re-earned vs continues unless tenured staff fired — plus using PRO's own B1.4 boom-bust line to contradict B4.1's "never closed anything" claim.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by establishing a decisive conceptual wedge: "portability." While Side A argued that block grants allow institutions to back risky science against conservative panels, Side B effectively demonstrated that when an institution's leadership says "no" (as with Karikó), competitive grants offer alternative doors, whereas block grants make the local "no" final. Side B's "asymmetry of default" argument in the late rounds brilliantly flipped Side A's complaint about the grant treadmill into a necessary feature of accountability. Side B's closing crystallized the clash memorably ("Discovery is what survives the wrong judge"). Side A's Mill Hill example was a strong specific answer to pressure, but Side B's parry (it was a relocation, not a true defunding) and overall structural advantages secured a solid win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing exchange on the "asymmetry of default" and the Karikó example. Side B's realization that under block funding, "every dollar in that building runs through exactly that person," making a wrong "no" the last word, completely neutralized Side A's claim that block funding protects daring outsiders.
