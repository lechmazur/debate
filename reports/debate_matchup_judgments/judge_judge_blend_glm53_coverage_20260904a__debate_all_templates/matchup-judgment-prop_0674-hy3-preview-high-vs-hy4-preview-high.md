### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases and executed the format cleanly (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 labeling, no fake citations). PRO ran a classic deterrence-by-strength case with clear mechanisms (cost imposition, moral hazard of rewarding aggression, the 2014–2022 freeze as cautionary precedent). CON's opening reframe—that "wider Russian aggression" means NATO-level or nuclear escalation, not incremental battlefield gains—was the pivotal strategic move: it forced PRO to defend a harder claim (that arming Ukraine to battlefield success doesn't corner a nuclear power into escalating) rather than the easier claim (that concessions embolden future land-grabs). CON also won the fight over the Korea analogy: their reading (armistice first, deterrent hardening afterward) maps more literally onto the actual proposition's choice between aid-now versus settlement-now, while PRO's rebuttal ("sustained commitment came after Korea's freeze") implicitly concedes the sequencing CON needed. CON's rebuttals also more directly exploited PRO's concrete examples (Kharkiv/Kherson triggering mobilization and nuclear rhetoric) than PRO managed to neutralize, with PRO's "pressure valve" dismissal reading as question-begging rather than a decisive rebuttal. PRO's tu quoque on electoral fragility was effective but didn't fully answer CON's asymmetry point that a static defensive tripwire is easier to sustain politically than open-ended offensive aid. PRO's moral-hazard argument remained a live and strong point CON never fully neutralized, keeping this competitive, but CON's tighter framing, sharper use of shared examples, and more memorable closing synthesis give it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turning PRO's own Korea analogy and the Kharkiv/Kherson offensive-momentum example back against PRO (B4.1–B4.4), forcing PRO into the weaker "pressure valve" dismissal rather than a direct rebuttal of the cornered-power escalation mechanism.
