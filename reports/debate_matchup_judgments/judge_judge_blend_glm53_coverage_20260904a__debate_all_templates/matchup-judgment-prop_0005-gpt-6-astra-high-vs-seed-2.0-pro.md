  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A1) decisively crushed Side B's claim that commercial entities would continue to fund unbranded symptom awareness campaigns at scale, cementing Side A's advantage on practical impacts.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) wins the central weighing clash. PRO's pressure exposes CON's frequency asymmetry and incentive assertion, which CON answers with unproven absolutes ("never have, anywhere"). B preserves symptom education and patient inquiry, separating getting into care from steering care.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's R1/R2 reframing that a doctor can be competent yet still induce higher-cost prescribing and that recurring refill costs outweigh a one-time 90-second correction, combined with exposing CON counting every beneficial appointment as brand-dependent while dismissing distortion as merely occasional.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A built a much stronger comparative case by effectively dismantling Side B's core trade-off. A successfully argued that the costs of direct-to-consumer advertising are not just a one-time 90-second doctor correction, but recurring financial and clinical costs from distorted prescriptions. Furthermore, A effectively challenged Side B's "life-saving" benefits as hypothetical and unquantified, while maintaining a sharp, persuasive distinction between merely getting patients into care and actively steering their treatment toward branded products.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side B's "one in ten" hypothetical and clearly distinguished between patient-initiated research and manufacturer-purchased mass persuasion, effectively neutralizing Side B's pressure questions and sharpening the core clash.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by maintaining strict comparative discipline, successfully arguing that commercial motive does not inherently prevent medical benefit under strict regulations. Side A effectively neutralized Side B's alternative of neutral public health campaigns by pointing out that permitting them does not guarantee their provision, leaving Side B's side with a net loss in patient outreach.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response in Rebuttal 2 to Side B's admission that they would ban even a perfectly balanced advertisement, sharply identifying that Side B was substituting a test of commercial motive for a test of medical consequences.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON wins the weighing clash. PRO admits it would ban even a fully accurate ad with equal risk prominence and cheaper alternatives, converting its case from harm-reduction to a motive principle. CON shows motive does not equal bad outcome because brand request is separable from medical need, enforcement costs exist under both policies, and banning removes beneficial care triggers that neutral outreach does not automatically replace. PRO never establishes under CON's safeguards that warped prescriptions outweigh lost consultations, relying on assertions like "most doctors prescribe" and "decades of failure."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4/A5 crystallizing PRO's concession that even the ideal regulated ad must be banned – showing selective information is not determinative, motive ≠ outcome, and exposing the missing comparative harm weighing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins by grounding its case in the inescapable profit motives of pharmaceutical advertising. While Side B argued competently for regulation and highlighted the overbreadth of a total ban, Side A effectively neutralized this by conceding the ban would include perfectly balanced ads, explaining that the harm lies in the profit-driven selection bias (advertising only lucrative branded drugs, not generics or lifestyle changes). Side A's argument that doctors are put on the defensive by corporate lead generation was more realistic than Side B's reliance on idealized clinical gatekeeping and perfect regulatory enforcement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's first pressure question, where A boldly conceded they would ban even a perfectly balanced ad and pivoted to the systemic harm of profit-driven selection bias, effectively dismantling B's overbreadth trap.
