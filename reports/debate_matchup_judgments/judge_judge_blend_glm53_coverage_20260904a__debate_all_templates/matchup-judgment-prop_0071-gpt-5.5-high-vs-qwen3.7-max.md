### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the debate's decisive intellectual clash by maintaining, across every round, a point CON never fully neutralized: a state already owes healthcare, schools, and housing to every citizen, including those who benefited from extraction. Distributing those baseline goods to everyone—wrongdoers and victims alike—discharges no specific debt; the reparative element evaporates. PRO crystallized this cleanly in closing: "If the state says 'we took from your community, but our repayment is a program everyone gets anyway,' then the reparative part has vanished." CON's A1 response to PRO's Q1 (that dismantling structural inequality satisfies the obligation) was precisely what PRO had already anticipated and answered—it treats a generic policy improvement as restitution without explaining why beneficiaries of the wrong should receive the same remedy as the injured community. CON scored real pressure on PRO through the proxy/leakage counter (PRO's own community proxies look like place-based welfare), the constitutional vulnerability argument, and political-coalition logic. These were genuine hits. But PRO answered them adequately: leakage also afflicts universal programs; legal design is a reason to build carefully, not to abandon the debt; and backlash threatens all serious redistribution. CON's practical objections were conditions of good design rather than fatal objections to the proposition. CON's strategic move—defending a third path (universal investment) rather than mere apologies—was coherent but ultimately conceded that targeted, community-specific repair has moral force, then argued only against one implementation. PRO's two clipped speeches are execution costs, but the core arguments survived and were reinforced in later rounds. On balance, PRO held the principled core, handled the pressure questions better, and gave the cleaner comparative reason to vote for the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's answer to CON's pressure question Q1 and the subsequent closing crystallization: the insight that states already owe every citizen baseline public goods (healthcare, schools, housing), so providing those goods to descendants cannot constitute restitution to a specific community whose labor and land were extracted to enrich others—including the very people who now receive the same remedy. CON never produced a principled response to why sharing universal benefits with beneficiaries of extraction discharges a targeted debt.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins because it squarely neutralized PRO’s core claim by showing direct reparations cannot survive legal/political reality and deliver zero material repair, while universal policies actually reach descendant communities; PRO’s answers on proxies and design left the administrative and court barriers largely intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 exposed the bureaucratic/legal nightmare in B’s workable proxies and court-indictment concession, damaging PRO’s feasibility route.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A defended a clearer principled frame: material extraction created a specific inherited debt that cannot be discharged by universal public goods a state already owes everyone. While Side B landed sharp practical blows on administrative complexity and legal risk, A showed that approximation is standard in governance and that imperfect administration is a design challenge, not a reason to erase the debt. B's alternative never fully overcame the intuition that paying a specific debt to everyone, including descendants of beneficiaries, is not restitution. A's case remained more resilient after the full exchange, despite B's stronger rebuttal fireworks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's turn in Rebuttal 1 that PRO's "workable proxies" (neighborhood, residence) collapse into place-based welfare, creating the very leakage and racially charged bureaucracy PRO accused CON of ignoring.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Both sides engaged the live clashes seriously, but CON built the stronger path through the exchange. PRO's philosophical anchor—specific debt repayment versus general welfare—was genuinely powerful and never completely dissolved. However, CON's dissection of PRO's bank analogy in Rebuttal 2 was the most precise blow of the debate: a real debt requires an identified creditor, debtor, amount, and legal transfer, and PRO had none of those. When PRO retreated to community trusts and geographic targeting, CON correctly pointed out this reintroduced all the eligibility problems PRO claimed to have solved, and turned "direct restitution" into political allocation. PRO's answers to CON's pressure questions were weaker than CON's answers to PRO's questions. PRO's Q1 (why should a middle-class descendant receive nothing?) was sharp, but CON answered it credibly—present-focused anti-discrimination and community investment help descendants where harms persist without a hereditary check. PRO's A2 to CON's Q2 (affluent descendant versus deprived neighbor) leaned entirely on the "different obligations" framing without engaging the fairness comparison. CON's distinction between tracking current inequality for civil rights enforcement and allocating centuries-old debt for cash transfers was a real and unanswered difference. PRO's thief-and-clinic analogy in the closing was rhetorically memorable, but CON's closing response—"PRO's model wins only if you accept an unadministrable ledger"—landed the cleaner final frame. CON's two clipped speeches are a real execution cost, but the surviving content remained crisper in the decisive exchanges. A clear but competitive win for CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 attack on PRO's bank analogy: "A real debt has an identified creditor, debtor, amount, and legal transfer. Here PRO has none of that." PRO's response—community recognition frameworks and trusts—was pointed out by CON to still require political allocation of historical causation, meaning the analogy collapsed rather than supported PRO's eligibility framework. This was the sharpest and most consequential single exchange in the debate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A wins by establishing that PRO's debt model is administratively arbitrary and produces new inequities while A's present-focused investments address living harms without requiring an unworkable ledger; clipped openings cost A some force but rebuttals and answers to pressure questions neutralized B's continuity and community-trust claims more effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1/A4.2 directly dismantled B's Q1/Q2 on eligibility and civil-rights capacity by distinguishing self-identification from hard ancestry allocation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): CON won by exposing that PRO's direct reparations model rests on a debt metaphor with no identifiable creditor, amount, or clean transfer, forcing PRO to slide arbitrarily between individuals, communities, trusts, and geography. This undermined PRO's claim to specificity and showed the scheme would become bureaucratic ancestry sorting with new injustices. CON's alternative of large, present-focused material investment targeted at continuing deprivation offered a more workable and fairer path to repair, reaching those actually harmed without impossible historical bookkeeping.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's dismantling of PRO's bank/debt analogy by showing direct reparations lack an identifiable creditor, amount, or transfer, reducing the program to arbitrary ancestry sorting disguised as precise accounting.
