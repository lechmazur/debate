### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): This was a tightly matched debate built around one real axis of clash: does irreversibility of open-weight release justify reversing the legal default, or does it merely justify stricter thresholds within a legality-by-default frame? B's opening was the strongest moment of the debate, correctly identifying that distribution format itself changes the risk calculus (loss of patch/revoke/trace) and that pre-release tests are snapshots against a permanent, adaptable platform. That framing drove much of the middle debate and forced A onto the back foot defending testability. A's pressure question (Q2) targeted exactly the right seam: what objective criteria would let B ever approve a release, and why aren't those just the motion's thresholds by another name. B's answer in Rebuttal 2 committed to a standard requiring releasers to prove, "assuming full fine-tuning, tool use, and iteration," that weights don't materially enable severe misuse — a standard A then exposed in closing as either unattainable (making B's "not a blanket ban" claim false) or reliant on the same imperfect testing B spent the debate calling insufficient. B's closing reasserted the asymmetric-cost framing but never repaired this specific coherence problem, leaving A's final volley unanswered at the point where the debate ended. That unresolved tension, landed at the most consequential moment, gives A the narrow edge, even though B's core irreversibility framework remained the more original and memorable engine of the debate throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing point that B's "prove containment under full future adaptation" standard is either impossible (contradicting B's claim of no blanket ban) or equivalent in reliability to PRO's own pre-release thresholds — left unanswered when the debate closed.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by demonstrating that CON's affirmative-approval alternative still relies on the same imperfect testing CON criticizes, so reversing the default does not solve the epistemic problem. PRO showed that irreversibility justifies conservative safety margins and stricter pre-release scrutiny, but not an impossible evidentiary standard that effectively prohibits beneficial open-weight models. PRO's concrete threshold example, unrebutted benefits for hospitals and competition, and sharp distinction between pre-release evaluation and post-harm response provided the cleaner comparative case. CON repeatedly substituted permanence for proof of dangerous magnitude, and its mischaracterization of PRO's pre-release test as waiting for real-world demonstration weakened its final rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing crystallization that CON's regime cannot escape testing incompleteness—forcing a choice between thresholds-in-disguise or indefinite prohibition—decisively blunted CON's irreversibility argument and clarified why the motion's framework is the more workable default.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively exposing the logical contradiction in Side B's alternative framework. Side B argued that pre-release testing is inherently incomplete, and therefore advanced open weights must be restricted by default, requiring affirmative proof of safety before release. Side A brilliantly pointed out in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing that Side B's "affirmative approval" relies on the exact same imperfect hostile testing that Side B spent the debate criticizing. Side A forced a dilemma: if finite testing is enough for Side B's approval, it is enough for Side A's threshold framework; if it is not, Side B's standard is an impossible bar that amounts to a de facto blanket ban. Side A's sharp pressure questions and logical progression made their comparative case significantly stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side B's "affirmative approval" standard by showing it relies on the same imperfect testing Side B criticized, effectively proving that Side B's framework either collapses into Side A's or becomes an unworkable ban.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides built a coherent, well-structured case and engaged directly across rounds, making this a genuinely close debate. B's strongest move was exposing a real tension in A's framework: A's admission that "testable" identified risks must be resolved before release edges toward B's own safety-case model, and B pressed the harder case of plausible-but-not-reliably-testable risk, which A never fully closed. But A inflicted more cumulative, less-answered damage on B's core architecture: the symmetry point that closed/authorized weights also leak, steal, and get reimplemented (undercutting B's claim that authorization meaningfully preserves irreversibility-avoidance) was never squarely rebutted, only met with a general "obstacles still help" response; the delay-cost argument (concentration, entrenched incumbents, clinics denied this year not helped by later approval) got only generic mitigations; and most tellingly, A's pressure question about controlled access collapsing into either open access or meaningless bureaucracy was answered with an explicit concession ("nothing guarantees... my claim is risk reduction, not impossibility") that materially weakened B's proposed middle-ground alternative. A's closing reasserted the "who is armed when the test misses" frame without directly closing B's best point, but B's closing also didn't fully answer A's symmetry and collapse critiques, which remained live. On balance, A absorbed one sharp unanswered hit while landing several equally serious ones that B never fully neutralized, giving A the cleaner net path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Q1 pressure exchange on controlled access (A3.1 vs B3's A1 answer conceding "nothing guarantees" copying can be stopped) was most decisive: it forced B to admit its proposed middle-ground alternative to full open-weight release offers only marginal, unquantified risk reduction rather than a real structural safeguard, undercutting B's central practical alternative to default legality.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): PRO (Side A) won by showing that CON’s prior-authorization alternative either disarmed defenders through “censorship by queue” or collapsed into open distribution plus unenforceable bureaucracy. CON landed strong conceptual points about irreversibility and epistemic uncertainty, but never fully answered PRO’s challenge that controlled access still permits copying while blocking clinics and independent auditors, nor overcame PRO’s closing comparative that when tests miss a danger, the regime equipping defenders is preferable. Side A turned CON’s best objections into reasons to prefer default legality with hard, pre-specified tripwires.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 and closing reframed irreversibility as a reason to prefer openness: both regimes can approve dangerous models, but only default legality arms defenders with the weights attackers will obtain, while prior authorization leaves independent researchers waiting in queue.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A built a more compelling and practically grounded case, effectively dismantling Side B's "controlled access" alternative by highlighting the contradiction between offline utility and copy prevention. Side A also won the crucial clash on defense asymmetry, persuasively arguing that Side B's prior authorization regime would leave independent defenders blind and queued while attackers inevitably obtain leaked or replicated weights. Side A successfully defended the proposition's specific "clearly specified thresholds" mechanism against Side B's attempts to broaden the burden into a discretionary "safety case" driven by unspecified dread.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 dismantling of Side B's "controlled access" model, pointing out that allowing offline use for clinics inherently grants copyable weights, reducing Side B's alternative to either a ban on the very uses it claims to support or merely "open distribution plus bureaucracy."
