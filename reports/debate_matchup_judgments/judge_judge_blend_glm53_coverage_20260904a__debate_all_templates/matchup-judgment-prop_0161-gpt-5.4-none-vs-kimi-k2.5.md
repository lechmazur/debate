### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): Side A won by establishing an existential asymmetry that Side B never neutralized: deliberately creating novel synthetic pandemic pathogens introduces a preventable, civilization-scale downside for speculative, incremental benefits. Side B repeatedly argued for studying "authentic" viruses and the limits of models, but never showed why natural, attenuated, or pseudovirus substitutes could not meet defensive needs, effectively eliding the motion's focus on *novel synthetic* creation. Side A's Rebuttal 2 decisively inverted Side B's enforcement objection—arguing that because viral genomes are information and hard to detect, legalization multiplies risk rather than reducing it—and exposed Side B's false dilemma between defenselessness and synthetic pathogen creation. Side A's pressure questions also landed more cleanly; Side B's answer to Q2 failed to identify any preparedness need that truly required *synthetic* rather than natural or attenuated systems. Side A incurred an execution cost from clipped rebuttals, but the visible material addressed the decisive clash and crystallized the brighter line.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 inversion of the "genomes are information" objection into a reason *for* prohibition, and its exposure of Side B's false dilemma between defenselessness and synthetic pathogen creation.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully framing the debate around risk asymmetry and turning Side A's core enforcement argument. While A effectively highlighted the limitations of surrogate models using the historical RSV vaccine failure, B consistently weighed those marginal preparedness gains against the irreversible, civilization-scale downside of a single leak, theft, or diffusion event. B also deftly answered pressure on definitional thresholds and turned A's "genomes are invisible information" point into a reason for prohibition rather than legalization: if detection is inherently difficult, proliferating lawful creators under elastic "defensive" labels multiplies catastrophic risk. A never fully resolved why mainstreaming synthetic pandemic pathogens is safer than a stigmatizing ban paired with safer research substitutes, leaving B's comparative weighing decisively intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's turn of A's verification argument (B4.5, B5.2). A claimed viral genomes lack physical signatures, making bans unenforceable and driving work underground. B flipped this by arguing that precisely because detection is hard, legalizing the capability is dangerous; prohibition shrinks the lawful market, stigmatizes the activity, and prevents an arms-race dynamic, making enforcement difficulty a reason for, not against, the ban.

### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON)): CON won by exposing two unresolved burdens in PRO's case. First, PRO's phenotypic standard for "clear pandemic potential" requires demonstrating efficient human-to-human transmission and lethality, which CON showed is an ex post test ill-suited to ex ante research governance; this left PRO oscillating between claiming the line is sharp and admitting uncertainty that would also cripple prohibition. Second, CON's asymmetric compliance argument—that a ban binds only responsible actors while rogue states and nature proceed anyway—was never adequately answered by PRO's appeals to stigma and taboo. CON successfully framed prohibition as strategic self-handicapping: compliant states would deliberately surrender defensive experimental knowledge while remaining exposed to covert programs and natural emergence. PRO's irreversibility argument remained live but was outweighed by CON's demonstration that prohibition does not eliminate the hazard and makes the remaining risk harder to survive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's demonstration that PRO's "demonstrated transmissibility" standard is ex post and unworkable for ex ante governance, combined with the closing crystallization that asymmetric compliance turns prohibition into deliberate vulnerability rather than survival.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (no reasoning) (CON); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by decisively exposing a structural flaw in PRO's regulatory mechanism: PRO's proposed "sharp line" relies on phenotypic demonstration in models, which is an ex post standard useless for an ex ante ban on creation. CON consistently leveraged this to show that PRO's ban either overreaches into legitimate mechanism work or fails to stop dangerous constructs until they already exist. CON also better weighed the compliance asymmetry, framing the ban as strategic self-handicapping that blinds transparent labs while rogue actors or natural emergence proceed. PRO's irreversibility and stigma arguments were rhetorically strong but never fully answered CON's core burden: how to prepare for failure modes under non-ideal compliance without experimental probing. CON's licensing framework offered a more coherent response to uncertainty.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing dismantling PRO's line-drawing: highlighting that PRO's "phenotypic demonstration" standard is ex post and therefore cannot govern ex ante design, turning PRO's own uncertainty concession against the feasibility of a categorical ban.
