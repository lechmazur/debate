### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides executed the format cleanly (proper Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 labeling) and built coherent, competing frames — CON on security/enforcement and PRO on gatekeeper rent and real-world precedent. The pivotal exchange was PRO's macOS/EU DMA analogy paired with the "unbundle review from payment" argument, which forced CON to defend why enforcement requires payment control rather than mere review/delisting authority. CON's answer in Rebuttal 2 and the Pressure Questions round ("too soon to tell," "Mac proves the opposite") was assertive but thin, never explaining the actual causal link between payment processing and enforceability, while PRO's answers (identity-check notarization, cert revocation, tiering permitted by the motion's "most apps" language) were more concrete and better tied to real precedent. CON's steering/dark-patterns point was strong and never fully resolved, but PRO's response — that it's a solvable disclosure/UI problem rather than grounds for a blanket ban — was a plausible and proportionate policy answer that blunted its force. PRO's economic examples (Kindle, Netflix, Amazon) and the "who actually benefits" reversal gave its case sharper concrete grounding, while CON leaned more on vivid but somewhat speculative worst-case scenarios in its closing. On balance PRO built the clearer, better-evidenced path to victory, though the debate was close and competitive throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's macOS/EU DMA rebuttal to CON's claim that curated security cannot survive payment choice — forcing CON into a weaker "too soon to tell" hedge in Rebuttal 2 rather than a direct rebuttal of the precedent.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's proposed mitigations. Side B relied heavily on the idea that users could still choose Apple billing and that UI regulations would prevent dark patterns, but Side A persuasively argued that 'choice at checkout is fiction when the seller designs the checkout' and that real-time enforcement across millions of apps is impossible. Side A also effectively neutralized Side B's 'tiering' argument by showing how scammers would simply disguise themselves as low-risk apps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 argument that choice at checkout is a fiction when the seller designs the checkout and uses dark patterns to steer users, effectively destroying Side B's claim that the safe option remains available.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully unbundling the safety apparatus from the payment monopoly and effectively using real-world counter-examples (macOS, EU DMA) to neutralize Side A's apocalyptic predictions. Side B also smartly leveraged the "most consumer apps" phrasing in the motion to advocate for tiered risk, which blunted Side A's strongest emotional appeals regarding children and banking scams. While Side A delivered highly polished rhetoric about dark patterns and user steering, Side B correctly identified these as UI regulation problems rather than justifications for a blanket ban, ultimately controlling the comparative clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it directly answered the pressure questions by reframing developer steering as a solvable UI regulation issue and explaining how macOS/EU notarization includes identity verification and instant cert revocation to stop viral scams, effectively collapsing Side A's empirical and structural defenses.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a more concrete safety case, tying integrated checkout to specific mechanisms (Ask to Buy, one-tap cancel, chargebacks, revocation) that PRO's proposed substitute (consumer law + card-network chargebacks across millions of independent sites) never convincingly matched at scale. A's pressure question about who enforces refunds/pricing "instantly across millions of sites" and who bears liability when Apple never touched the transaction exposed a real gap that B's A2 answer only gestured at ("same enforcers who police the web") without resolving the scale/latency problem — and A capitalized on this concession ("bounced between developer and bank") in Rebuttal 2. PRO's strongest moves — the "if the store is safer it wins voluntarily" framing and the late seatbelt reframe ("mandate protections, not the cashier") — were clever but didn't fully answer A's collective-harm/coercive-steering mechanism (dominant apps forcing exit even from users who'd prefer to stay), which A sustained across rounds with the "even 5% leaving creates reservoirs of harm" line. Both sides argued with discipline and avoided fake citations, but A's rebuttals did more damage to B's live routes than vice versa.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3-A4.4), turning B's own concession that "Apple owes no support for a sale it did not touch" into proof that independent checkout leaves users "bounced between developer and bank" — directly neutralizing B's proposed enforcement substitute raised in the Pressure Questions round.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's core premise of "voluntary choice." A convincingly argued that dominant apps would use exclusive features and discounts to coerce users out of the secure ecosystem, rendering B's "users can just stay in the walled garden" argument moot. A also successfully proved that specific safety features, like Ask to Buy and one-tap cancellation, are structurally dependent on a unified checkout and cannot simply be replaced by reactive bank chargebacks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's first pressure question was the decisive moment, brilliantly using the "mandatory seatbelt" analogy to explain why collective safety on mobile devices cannot rely on voluntary opt-ins when dominant apps have the power to coerce users to leave.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by successfully decoupling "safety" from mere "sandboxing" and tying it directly to integrated billing features like Ask to Buy, one-tap cancellation, and blocking data harvests before they occur. Side A also effectively neutralized Side B's "voluntary choice" argument by explaining how dominant apps can coerce users into sideloading or external payments, making the "if it's better, they'll stay" test fail in reality. Side B's reliance on general consumer law and OS permissions to replace the App Store's real-time fraud prevention and parental controls was comparatively hand-wavy and less convincing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's claim that OS permissions travel with the child, pointing out that family billing, Screen Time, and Ask to Buy only work inside the integrated checkout, and that a payment iframe can still exfiltrate data even with OS sandboxing.
