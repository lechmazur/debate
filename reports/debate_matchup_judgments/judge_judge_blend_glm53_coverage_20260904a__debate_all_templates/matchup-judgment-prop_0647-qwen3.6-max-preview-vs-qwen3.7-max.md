### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): PRO held the stronger through-line across all five rounds. The induced-demand argument—added capacity lowers driving costs, attracts new trips, and refills congestion—was introduced clearly in the opening, sharpened in both rebuttals, and never neutralized by CON. CON's closest answer was that induced demand "reflects growth, not a design flaw," but that concedes the mechanism while only contesting the label; it does not show that widening actually resolves congestion. PRO's equity, financial-liability, and land-value arguments were also introduced in the opening and never seriously contested by CON, which dismissed them as "localized aesthetics." CON's best material was the freight/last-mile delivery concern and the physical-geometry argument that surface streets multiply conflict points. PRO answered both with designated truck routes, off-peak delivery windows, and the point that distributed grids prevent the single-point failures freeways create. These answers were adequate if not airtight. CON repeatedly attacked a strawman—insisting PRO claimed traffic "evaporates"—when PRO consistently said trips consolidate, shift hours, or switch modes. That mischaracterization undermined CON's rebuttal credibility across rounds. Both sides followed required format (Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 labels). PRO's rhetorical framing ("leaky bucket," "subscription to perpetual construction") made the stakes easier to grasp. CON's "vital arteries" metaphor was vivid but repeated without adding new comparative force. Overall, PRO made the decisive clash clearer, answered CON's strongest pressure more directly, and closed with a sharper reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's sustained handling of induced demand across Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2, and the closing was the most decisive exchange. CON's only substantive counter—that induced demand reflects population growth rather than a design flaw—actually concedes that widening does not permanently solve congestion, which is precisely PRO's claim. This left CON's core promise of "optimizing corridors" without a mechanism for escaping the congestion cycle, and PRO exploited that gap consistently.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): A won by making the central comparison easier to decide: widening is self-defeating through induced demand, while removal changes travel behavior, reconnects neighborhoods, and frees valuable land. A also better handled the urban-specific scope and gave plausible mechanisms for managing freight and emergency access.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure and second rebuttal on induced demand were decisive: B's reply that demand reflects growth did not really answer the claim that added capacity attracts more driving and recreates congestion, while A tied that failure to land, debt, and neighborhood harms.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): B wins because it consistently centered the induced-demand mechanism and adaptation evidence across rebuttals and answers, making CON's regional-mobility defense look like a short-term fix that repeats the same cycle; A never neutralized that core comparison.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): PRO built the more coherent and durable case across the full exchange. The induced demand argument—widening generates the very congestion it claims to solve, ultimately flooding the same surface streets CON claims to protect—was introduced in the opening and never genuinely neutralized. CON's best counter was distinguishing inelastic trips (freight, emergency) from discretionary ones, but PRO's A1 response correctly noted that inelastic trips do not require general-purpose lane widening and can be served by dedicated infrastructure in removal scenarios. PRO's pressure question Q1 was the most decisive blow: exposing that widening demands immediate, certain home demolitions while CON simultaneously invoked speculative market displacement from removal as a dealbreaker. CON's A1 (shoulder conversions, tunneling) partially answered this but did not fully resolve the contradiction. CON's equity argument—keep toxic barriers to prevent gentrification—remained structurally weak throughout; PRO's rebuttal that the fix is housing policy, not permanent pollution, was more persuasive. CON's pressure question on transit build-out timing was sharp, and PRO's BRT-in-months answer was somewhat glib, but adequate enough. CON's clipped Rebuttal 1 is a minor execution cost. CON's inelastic demand point is real but narrower than CON treated it—most urban freeway volume is not freight or emergency—and CON never convincingly explained why that narrow slice justifies widening as a general default. PRO's framing of the motion as a "shift in default" was clearer and more defensible than CON's absolutist reading that it requires dismantling every spine immediately.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's pressure question Q1 was the most decisive exchange: forcing CON to explain why the certain, physical demolition of homes required by widening is acceptable while the manageable market risk of removal is a dealbreaker. CON's answer (shoulder conversions limit demolition) partially deflected but never escaped the asymmetry PRO exposed, leaving CON's equity narrative visibly inconsistent for the rest of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side B won by making the central comparison scale and timing: removals may work on redundant spurs, but A never proved they should be more common on primary urban corridors carrying freight, emergency traffic, and working commuters. A had strong induced-demand and neighborhood-harm arguments, but relied too broadly on traffic evaporation and speculative quick modal shift. B's answers on inelastic demand, cut-through traffic, and the motion's default standard left the cleaner route to vote CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal that traffic evaporation is localized to redundant segments and does not solve inelastic freight, emergency, and cross-metro demand, plus the timing contrast that removal displaces traffic overnight while induced demand develops over years, was decisive.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B wins because its induced demand and land-reclamation arguments consistently neutralized CON's inelastic-demand and worker-access claims, while B's direct A1/A2 answers to pressure questions exposed timing and scale weaknesses in A's case. A defended widening pragmatically but never overturned B's core treadmill critique.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2's A1/A2 directly dismantled A's freight and transit-timing objections by showing evaporation plus BRT as practical alternatives rather than waiting for perfect systems.
