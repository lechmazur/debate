### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built and maintained a cleaner path to victory across the full exchange. The decisive clash was over scope: CON repeatedly claimed wealthy coastlines are dominated by critical infrastructure requiring permanent armor, while PRO countered that the bulk of actual coastal defense spending flows to repetitive-loss residential neighborhoods and barrier-island subdivisions, not ports or downtowns. CON never adequately answered this empirical claim—it was asserted, not dismantled. PRO's concession that hard defenses may persist where infrastructure truly cannot move was a controlled narrowing of the proposition, not a self-defeating concession; CON's attempt to treat it as fatal was clever but hollow because the motion asks about priority, not exclusivity. PRO's coastal-squeeze argument (fixing shorelines squeezes wetlands between concrete and rising seas) likewise went unrefuted—CON's hybrid-engineering response essentially endorsed ecology in "low-density corridors," which is nearer PRO's position than CON's own. CON's strongest live arguments were political feasibility (buyouts require pre-disaster action legislatures rarely take) and market dynamics (retreat designation freezes credit and values). PRO answered these adequately—noting the status quo already produces annual emergency appropriations and that renters fare worse under catastrophic wall failure than under a planned relocation program—though PRO's bundled-voucher solution was sketched rather than fully defended. Both sides followed format requirements correctly. PRO's framing was more memorable and its narrative more consistent across rounds; CON's case rested too heavily on the critical-infrastructure move, which kept being exposed as covering only a fraction of the at-risk footprint the motion addresses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7) most decisively damaged CON's live route to victory. CON had tried to leverage PRO's own concession about critical infrastructure into a structural win, claiming defenses are therefore the default priority. PRO replied that critical ports and downtowns are "a sliver of the at-risk footprint" and that the motion turns on where the majority of public spending actually goes—repetitive-loss neighborhoods and barrier islands—not on the exceptional dense node. CON never recovered from this scope challenge because their entire closing still relied on the same assertion that "wealthy coastlines are dominated by ports, energy hubs, and dense populations" without actually refuting PRO's characterization of where the money flows.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. A’s strongest line was that ports, energy hubs, and dense population centers make hard defenses the functional priority, plus strong feasibility concerns about market collapse, renters, and tax bases. But B more persuasively framed the motion as a question of default priority, not absolute prohibition: hard defenses may remain for immovable critical infrastructure, while retreat should be prioritized for the broader repetitive-loss, low-density, and ecologically fragile high-risk coast. B also turned A’s fiscal/equity claims by arguing the current defense-and-rebuild model is itself a regressive open-ended bailout. A’s “concession destroys the motion” attack was forceful but overreached, because exceptions do not negate prioritization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to the critical-infrastructure concession was decisive: defense can be a last resort for genuinely immovable assets without remaining the default for every flooded neighborhood or barrier island. That blunted A’s central contradiction argument.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A won by establishing that the status quo of hard defenses mostly protects low-value repetitive-loss areas rather than just critical infrastructure, directly answering pressure questions on scope and renters while exposing CON's defense of the status quo as undefended. Side B had strong feasibility points but was weakened by repeated shrinking of the debate and failure to rebut A's narrowing of the actual spending patterns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.3: Direct answers to B's pressure questions on scope and renters, then reframing the debate around actual coastal spending to undercut CON's economic continuity claim.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): This was a genuinely competitive debate. CON's strongest move was the geographic-fixedness argument—ports, nuclear corridors, and transit networks are not liquid assets that can be phased toward inland hubs during routine capital cycles, and PRO's A2 answer was the weakest moment in the debate: "we phase capital investment toward resilient hubs" is vague and does not explain how a functioning deep-water port complex migrates without severing the supply chains that generate its value. That argument survived the exchange meaningfully. CON's hybrid model (defend dense/critical areas, retreat scattered/low-density zones) was also smart framing that PRO never fully neutralized. However, PRO held key advantages that matter more on balance. First, PRO's Q2 pressure—that private insurers are already withdrawing from high-risk coasts regardless of seawalls—directly undermined CON's core claim that hard defenses preserve municipal solvency by keeping insurance markets in place. CON's response (Rotterdam-style engineering lowers risk pricing) was plausible but did not address the broader trend PRO invoked, and this left the tax-base preservation argument partially deflated. Second, PRO's Q1 exposed a genuine gap in CON's case: CON never provided a clear decision rule for when defense costs exceed asset value and retreat becomes mandatory. CON's answer ("cost-benefit analysis") was generic and left PRO's causal story—that defenses defer collapse until walls overtop and retreat happens anyway at multiples of the cost—mostly standing. Third, CON's hybrid model strategically concedes that retreat is appropriate in many coastal settings, which partially reinforces rather than defeats the motion's framing that retreat should be the priority over hard defenses "on balance." PRO's compounding-versus-bounded-expenditure argument was the best-developed fiscal thread in the debate and was never decisively neutralized. These advantages, taken together, give PRO a clear but not dominant edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q2 in the Pressure Questions round was the most decisive exchange. By pointing out that private insurers are already withdrawing from high-risk coasts regardless of seawall presence, PRO directly undermined CON's claim that hard defenses preserve municipal solvency by keeping capital and insurance markets in place. CON's subsequent A2 response—that well-engineered coasts like Rotterdam or the Thames estuary attract lower risk pricing—was plausible as a narrow example but failed to address the general trend PRO invoked or explain why insurer withdrawal is already occurring across defended coastlines. This left CON's central tax-base-preservation argument materially weakened heading into the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by making the more concrete comparative case. Side B had a strong fiscal and physical-risk frame around compounding defense costs, but overextended into claims that retreat is broadly bounded and that economic networks can be phased inland. Side A repeatedly exposed that as under-specified for ports, dense transit, nuclear corridors, and other geographically fixed assets, while offering a plausible decision rule: defend dense and irreplaceable assets, retreat scattered low-value areas. That hybrid directly answered the motion’s prioritization question and made Side B’s “routine capital relocation” answer look too abstract.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s pressure and second rebuttal on whether agglomeration economies can actually be relocated was decisive: Side B’s answer that ports and networks could be shifted through capital cycles did not adequately meet the concrete challenge that deep-water ports and coastal infrastructure are geographically anchored.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B wins by establishing the irreplaceability of fixed coastal assets like ports and nuclear corridors and showing that retreat severs supply chains and tax bases without a viable relocation mechanism. B's direct answers to pressure questions on decision rules and insurance preservation neutralized A's compounding-risk framing, while A's phase-in response remained too abstract to counter concrete examples like Rotterdam. The clash on fiscal boundedness favored B once tax-roll losses and perpetual remediation were weighed against manageable defense upgrades.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 answers to both pressure questions, using cost-benefit for fixed assets and capital-flight risks to dismantle the one-time buyout claim.
