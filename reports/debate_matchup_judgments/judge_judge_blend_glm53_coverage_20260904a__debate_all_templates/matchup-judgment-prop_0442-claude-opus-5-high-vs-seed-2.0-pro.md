### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO won a competitive debate by keeping a clearer live route: a binding IMO timetable is the only demand signal that can stop fossil hull lock-in and unlock green-fuel supply, with enforcement via class/insurance/port-state physics rather than flag-state honor. CON's strongest line—partial compliance yields almost all cost and little climate gain, with bulk-import pain—was sharp and memorable, but PRO repeatedly forced the internal tension (if rules are waived for food, the famine scenario fades; if honest operators set the price floor while cheaters underbid only "on paper," the cheating incentive collapses) and answered pressure more cleanly on cost-benefit linkage and sequencing. CON's build-first alternative never escaped the chicken-and-egg problem PRO crystallized in closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's contradiction trap (A2.1 then A4.5): CON cannot use the poor both as proof the rule will not be enforced and as the victims of its enforcement, paired with the partial-compliance = partial-progress costing model against CON's worst-of-both-worlds floor-price story.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO won the enforcement-cost clash. A2.1 exposed that CON cannot run "nobody complies" and "40% freight rise starves the poor" simultaneously, and A4.1-4.4 resolved it: partial compliance = partial cost, because cost *is* the clean fuel, and enforcement shifts from molecule-in-tank (2020 sulphur) to hull-type, class certificate and insurance. CON's reply B4.1-4.2 that cheaters underbid but keep prices at the compliant floor is economically incoherent and was called out in A5.4. On lock-in and sequencing, A gave a clear mechanism for why mandate creates supply, while CON's "build first, mandate later" has no engine.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A2.1/A4.1-A4.5: contradiction that cheating cannot both suppress emission cuts and sustain high freight prices, plus shift from berth-refusal to class/insurance enforcement and the admission that humanitarian waivers undercut CON's own famine claim.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B dominated the debate by exposing fatal internal contradictions in Side A's case and providing a far more sophisticated causal story for both enforcement and infrastructure investment. Side B brilliantly trapped Side A in the pressure round: Side A could not simultaneously argue that the mandate would be strictly enforced on food shipments (causing a famine) and that ports would automatically waive the rule for food shipments (causing mass non-compliance). Furthermore, Side B's distinction between drop-in sulphur fuels and physically distinct carbon fuel systems (requiring class certificates and insurance) dismantled Side A's core enforcement analogy. Finally, Side B's closing argument about the 25-year shipyard ordering cycle perfectly neutralized Side A's "wait for scale" alternative, proving that the mandate is the necessary catalyst for the very infrastructure Side A demanded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.5), which decisively collapsed Side A's famine scenario by pointing out that if ports waive environmental rules for humanitarian food cargoes, the food ships do not pay the green premium, meaning the price-hike and famine harms evaporate entirely.
