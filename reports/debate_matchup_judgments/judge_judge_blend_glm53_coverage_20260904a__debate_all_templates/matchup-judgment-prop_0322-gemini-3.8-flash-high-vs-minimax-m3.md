### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by holding the decisive burden: PRO never showed that acoustic/synthetic origin is a material commercial fact independent of sponsorship and product claims already covered by existing rules. PRO’s strongest examples repeatedly smuggled false-demonstration or unreviewed-claims harms into a technology-label mandate; CON consistently separated those harms and argued the proposition still regulates a curiosity tax with live line-drawing costs. PRO framed parasocial authenticity well but lost the comparative clash on materiality and necessity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.2): PRO’s “product they never touched” hypo is false demonstration already policed; the AI label is not what catches it, so the mandate still lacks a unique live harm.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won the materiality clash. PRO’s strongest harm examples were synthetic demonstrations of products never used, which CON showed are already policed by FTC testimonial/demonstration rules, not by voice-origin rules. CON kept focus on the proposition as written – a technology-specific label – and forced PRO to defend why acoustic pathway is material when sponsorship is already disclosed and host-reads are known to be scripted, sponsor-selected commercial copy. PRO never established distinct consumer injury beyond assertions of parasocial trust, and its line-drawing answer (assist vs replace) did not address translation, consent, or disability overbreadth costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.2 – CON reframes B's synthetic demo as false demonstration already illegal, separating the puppet (false product claim) from the strings (AI voice) and collapsing PRO's live route to distinct deception.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO successfully established that the "host-read" format relies on parasocial trust and human conviction, making the authenticity of the performance a material commercial fact. While CON argued effectively that existing FTC rules cover false product claims and financial ties, PRO convincingly demonstrated that these rules do not cover the falsification of the performance itself. PRO also handled the line-drawing pressure well by distinguishing between audio modification and text-to-speech generation. CON's reliance on the assumption that audiences do not expect genuine human engagement in host-read ads weakened their case against PRO's strong framing of advertiser incentives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response to CON's "false demonstration" argument. PRO clarified that while existing law catches false claims about a product, it does not catch the false attribution of human testimony when an AI voice is used to read a factually true script, exposing the gap in CON's reliance on current FTC rules.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): B won a competitive debate by locking the live clash on the authorized-licensing gap and upstream trust calibration rather than post-hoc fraud. B showed existing truth-in-advertising law is a rear-guard tool that needs injury and cannot restore listeners' ability to tell which host-reads were genuinely vetted versus mass-produced clones, while disclosure is cheap and continuous with settled transparency norms. A was strong on liability attaching to authorization and on enforcement messiness, and fair pressure answers kept it close, but A's "if nothing is false, no harm" frame never fully neutralized B's undetectable-volume and trust-commons comparison. B's clipped Rebuttal 2 was a real execution cost, yet the overall path to victory remained clearer for the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Q1 + A1 structural-harm answer: fraud law is silent on consented broad voice licensing where listeners cannot tell vetted from generated reads.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO wins on the decisive calibration vs fraud clash. CON treats harm as only false claims, which existing law can punish. PRO shows the live gap is authorized, truthful scaling where listeners lose ability to tell what was genuinely vouched for, and market discipline fails precisely because synthesis is engineered to be undetectable. Disclosure is framed as cheap upstream trust protection, analogous to existing sponsorship labels, outweighing CON's enforcement-trap worry, which PRO answers with a workable generation vs processing line despite the clip cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2's stand-in vs aid distinction plus market-detectability point [B2.4-B2.8] – ghostwriter still requires human presence while clone erases it, so the market can't self-correct without disclosure.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the debate around the structural harm to the "trust commons" and the practical limitations of ex post fraud law. While Side B made a strong argument about the "enforcement trap" of distinguishing between AI generation and routine audio processing, Side A's distinction between "editorial patching" and "scripted replication" was legally plausible and sufficient to defuse the trap. Side A also won the pressure question exchange by providing nuanced, direct answers, whereas Side B largely repeated earlier points. Side A's rhetorical framing ("synthetic sincerity," "calibration not fraud") was highly effective and made the stakes of the parasocial podcast economy much clearer than Side B's formalistic reliance on existing FTC rules.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1, which sharply distinguished between delivery aids (ghostwriters/teleprompters) and stand-ins (AI voices), and dismantled Side B's reliance on fraud law by pointing out that market discipline only works if listeners can detect the synthetic nature of the endorsement.
