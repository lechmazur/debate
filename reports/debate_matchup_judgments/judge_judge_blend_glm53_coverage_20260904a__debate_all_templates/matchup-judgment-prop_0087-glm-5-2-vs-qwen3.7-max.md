### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger cumulative case across the full exchange. Three structural advantages decided the debate. First, PRO established early and held throughout that the motion calls for additional, differential regulation—not a replacement for universal safeguards—and CON never effectively engaged this point, consistently arguing for universal caps as an alternative rather than acknowledging governments can do both. Second, PRO's Rebuttal 2 offered a concrete enforcement metric (resolved wagers per unit time rather than visual spin duration) that meaningfully answered CON's arbitrariness challenge; CON's rejoinder about bet-bundling was plausible but underdeveloped, leaving PRO's metric standing as workable. Third, and most decisively, CON's own answer at A4.1—conceding psychological distress without financial ruin is still "a manageable clinical issue"—handed PRO the closing argument. PRO correctly seized on it: "manageable is not absent" was a clean, direct turn of CON's own words, and the lead paint/asbestos analogy that followed made the normative principle crisp: mechanisms can differ without one harm being supreme. CON's core framework—that harm equals financial ruin and capping money caps all relevant harm—was too reductive and PRO exposed it at every stage. CON made genuinely good points (regulatory arbitrage, conditioning linked to financial stakes, universal safeguards), but these were individual shots rather than a coherent alternative framework, and none neutralized the basic proposition that compressed reinforcement constitutes a distinct harm profile warranting distinct treatment. PRO's "counting casualties, not preventing the accident" framing remained unanswered in any satisfying way.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing use of CON's A4.1 concession was the most decisive moment. CON called psychological distress without financial ruin a "manageable clinical issue," intending to minimize it, but PRO turned this into a clean admission: manageable harm is still harm, the proposition requires only distinctness not supremacy, and a government that caps losses while permitting unlimited high-speed conditioning treats the wound while ignoring the blade. That sequence—using the opponent's own words to crystallize the winning comparison—was the sharpest argumentative move in the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side A won by keeping the debate tied to the proposition: whether ultra-fast products have a distinct harm profile warranting heightened regulation. A gave a plausible mechanism—compressed reinforcement, faster loss-chasing, and higher wager volume—and repeatedly showed that universal financial safeguards can coexist with speed-specific rules. Side B’s best material on affordability checks and arbitrage was relevant, but it often replaced the motion with whether financial ruin is the only harm. Once B conceded psychological distress and compulsive behavior are real harms, A’s closing crystallized that distinct does not have to mean exclusively or maximally harmful.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s rebuttal that CON created a false either/or between financial regulation and speed-based regulation was decisive, especially paired with the point that affordability caps limit fallout but do not address rapid reinforcement and addiction formation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): PRO won because they successfully defended that ultra-fast gambling products have a distinct harm profile—compressed reinforcement cycles and accelerated loss-chasing that slower forms do not replicate—and that this distinct mechanism warrants distinct, layered regulation. CON’s attempt to reduce harm solely to financial devastation was undermined by their own concession that psychological distress is a real, if "manageable," clinical issue, which PRO rightly identified as proof of distinctness. CON’s arbitrage and unenforceability concerns were plausible but insufficient to negate the core mechanistic distinction, especially since PRO showed that all regulatory thresholds involve line-drawing and that resolved wagers per unit time captures the harm compression at issue.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The decisive exchange was PRO’s second rebuttal and closing, which directly answered CON’s pressure questions by defining "resolved wagers per unit time" as an enforceable metric and then seized upon CON’s concession that psychological distress without financial ruin is a "vastly different, manageable clinical issue." PRO turned that answer on its head to show that CON itself recognized a non-financial harm profile distinct from catastrophic ruin, thereby proving the proposition’s core claim.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): This was genuinely competitive, but CON (Side A) held a clear edge across three decisive clashes. First, the "degree not kind" argument was never squarely defeated: PRO's compounding response (three features combine into distinctiveness) was met directly by CON's closing—three differences of degree on a spectrum don't produce a categorical difference—and PRO had no answer. Second, CON's "just as homeless" rebuttal to PRO's acute-vs.-chronic framing landed and was never rebutted; PRO's framework optimises for dramatic, visible harm while leaving the far larger aggregate harm from slower normalised products undertreated, and this trade-off went unanswered. Third, CON's closing turnback—that if dissociation destroys the rational agency pre-commitment requires, it equally destroys the ability to stay on a regulated product rather than switch to an unregulated substitute—was clever and only partially addressed by PRO's structural-intervention reply. PRO's strongest live argument (mandatory breaks and stake caps are built into the product and can't be bypassed) is real, but CON's regulatory-arbitrage rebuttal throughout the debate (operators redesign just below thresholds) paired with the closing turnback left PRO without a clean comparative story. CON consistently maintained that the regulatory tools needed (pre-committed loss limits, affordability checks) are the same across products—calibration differs, but the instrument doesn't—which is a coherent and less-easily-gamed framework. PRO's opening causal story was the most compelling single speech, but CON's rebuttal work progressively narrowed the field until PRO's remaining arguments were either unanswered or outweighed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's attack on the IV-drip analogy in Rebuttal 1 was the most decisive single exchange. PRO built a vivid structural case on the idea that delivery mechanism itself creates toxicity. CON exposed the analogy as broken: alcohol harm genuinely scales with absorption rate because the liver processes at a fixed pace, but gambling harm is driven by total exposure, psychological entrapment, and loss-chasing—mechanisms present across the whole speed spectrum. This stripped the most memorable structural argument from PRO's case and forced PRO to retreat to the more defensive "compounding" framing in Rebuttal 2.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate by making loss velocity, frictionless access, and dissociative play into a distinct acute crisis profile rather than just a larger quantity of the same harm. B’s uniform pre-commitment alternative and degree-versus-kind attack were strong, but B leaned too heavily on the claim that identical final outcomes mean identical harm profiles, which did not fully answer A’s point that time-to-loss changes psychology, intervention chances, and immediate crisis risk. A also limited the arbitrage objection by framing regulation around a structural bundle of ultra-fast features, not an arbitrary second-count threshold.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest rebuttal was that migration from ultra-fast products to slower channels can still reduce acute catastrophic harm, exposing B’s assumption that all gambling losses are fungible regardless of speed.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A wins because it successfully defended the distinct harm profile of ultra-fast gambling—combining dissociative speed, frictionless delivery, and catastrophic loss velocity into a categorically different crisis profile—while showing that uniform regulation cannot catch acute session-destroying binges. Side B’s arbitrage and continuum arguments were sharp, but its claim that slow and fast harm produce identical outcomes ignored the decisive difference in acute psychological shock and intervention time, and its framing of PRO as downgrading slower products mischaracterized the motion. A’s compounding defense and redirection-as-harm-reduction responses resolved B’s key pressure, leaving a cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to Side B’s pressure questions in Rebuttal 2, where A explained that neuroscience, friction, and loss velocity compound into a unified distinct mechanism rather than shifting criteria, and that redirection to slower channels still reduces acute harm velocity—neutralizing B’s arbitrage attack without widening the debate.
