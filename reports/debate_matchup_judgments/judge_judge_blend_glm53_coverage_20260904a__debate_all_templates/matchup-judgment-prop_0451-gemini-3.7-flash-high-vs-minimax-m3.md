### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B built the more resilient case. B established early and maintained throughout that the proposition's "primarily threat" framing is a blunt, origin-based trigger rather than an evidence-based one, and repeatedly offered a concrete alternative (targeted safeguards, diversification, recycling, procurement) that A never fully displaced. Crucially, B turned A's own solar precedent and democratic-legitimacy argument back on itself: the solar failure shows a policy-pairing problem (deployment without domestic investment), not proof that imports are inherently predatory, and raising transition costs via blanket threat treatment equally threatens the "political consensus" A relies on. A's rebuttals largely reasserted the opening's claims (margin collapse, chokehold, dependency) without directly engaging this counterfactual, and A's answer to B's sharp pressure question about a "concrete threshold" for chokehold power remained qualitative and evasive, leaving a live line of attack unresolved. B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("threat first" vs "opportunity first, discipline the risk"), while A's closing was largely a repackaged recap without new engagement. Both sides respected the Q/A format, but B used its answers to sharpen comparison while A's answers left B's core challenge only partially addressed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 turning the solar precedent into a counterfactual argument (deployment without domestic investment, not import openness itself, caused the failure) and simultaneously flipping A's "democratic legitimacy" claim to argue that higher transition costs from blanket protectionism equally erode public support for climate policy — a point A's closing never rebutted.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing why cheap deployment could undermine the industrial capacity, private investment, supply security, and political legitimacy needed for durable decarbonization. Side A offered a credible targeted-safeguards alternative, but never fully explained how European firms retain margins and attract capital while competing against heavily subsidized imports. B's overstatements and erroneous C-reference IDs narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was its challenge to A's proposed coexistence of open imports and patient industrial investment: if subsidized imports capture demand and crush margins, a growing market does not necessarily finance European scaling. A's appeal to demand and public support did not fully resolve that mechanism.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won because it anchored its case in the concrete historical destruction of European solar manufacturing, provided a coherent economic mechanism for why state-subsidized below-cost imports irreversibly destroy domestic margins and innovation capacity, and effectively used democratic legitimacy as a weighing argument. While Side B skillfully argued for calibrated safeguards over blunt classification, it never fully answered how European firms survive the margin collapse long enough to benefit from "patient investment," and its answers to Side A's pressure questions were less direct than Side A's answers to its own. Side A made the decisive clash—the irreversibility of industrial hollowing-out—clearer and better supported.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (Turn 7), where it directly answered B's two pressure questions with A1 and A2, then used those answers to expose the "category mistake" of treating decarbonization as a procurement calculation and defend the irreversibility of industrial ecosystem loss.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): A built a sustained, coherent throughline (the solar-manufacturing precedent, the distinction between one-time consumption and durable industrial capability, and a specific policy mechanism—targeted duties/procurement rather than blanket bans) that directly defused B's central "deadline panic" objection. A's pressure questions extracted a concrete admission from B (naming CATL/Envision/Stellantis JVs), which A then exploited effectively in closing by labeling them "offshore finishing" since cathode/cell chemistry still originates in China—a point B's closing never rebutted, leaving it live and decisive. B's case was well-argued and consistently framed the climate urgency and "permanent capital" logic, and its rebuttals landed some real hits (e.g., pushing A to concede that domestic production costs more), but B's final speeches largely recapped rather than directly engaging A's sharpest late rebuttal, and B's answer to the replacement-cycle question ("diversified supplier base") remained more assertion than argument. A's case did suffer real execution costs from being clipped twice in the word limit, which cost it some concluding synthesis, but the core arguments landed before the cuts and were carried forward effectively in later rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's closing rebuttal that the CATL/Envision/Stellantis JVs cited by B in [B4.1] are "offshore finishing" rather than genuine localization, since cell chemistry and firmware still originate in China—a pointed, specific rebuttal that B's own closing failed to answer, leaving it as the most consequential unresolved clash in the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): A won a competitive debate by making the immediate, irreversible climate and affordability benefits more concrete than B's speculative replacement-cycle dependency. B established a genuine industrial risk and exposed A's weak proof of core-IP localization, but never reconciled tariffs and local-content rules with maintaining deployment pace. Its claim that China weaponized solar during the 2022 crisis was also unsupported, while A persuasively argued that installed clean technology reduced fossil dependence. B's two clipped turns were an additional execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3 most decisively exposed the tension between B4.1's promise that threat-oriented measures would not slow deployment and B4.3's concession that protected European production costs more. B answered who should receive the premium, but not why imposing it would leave near-term adoption unchanged.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing a concrete empirical precedent in solar, demonstrating that treating imports as an industrial threat does not require banning them or missing climate deadlines (via calibrated countervailing duties and CBAM), and turning Side B's FDI examples into evidence of shallow "offshore finishing" rather than genuine supply-chain anchoring. B never adequately answered who would manufacture replacements during the 2030s replacement wave or why Chinese suppliers would remain price-disciplined once European competitors were gone, relying instead on repeated assertions about climate deadlines that A showed were compatible with industrial preservation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's sustained dismantling of B's localization claims by showing that cited plants (CATL, Envision) represent assembly of cells whose core chemistry, cathodes, and firmware still originate in China, neutralizing B's best empirical answer to the dependency problem.
