### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won the decisive framing battle. From the opening, CON distinguished "tunneling is sometimes justified at a bottleneck" from "countries should invest heavily in tunneling as national policy," and pressed that gap relentlessly through rebuttals and pressure questions. PRO's answers to this pressure (e.g., "scale is established by recurrence across essential networks, not by one showcase project") largely restated the premise that needs are widespread rather than demonstrating that tunnel projects actually outrank competing uses of scarce capital. CON's pointed pressure questions ("what establishes that enough tunnels outrank competing repairs...") exposed this gap cleanly, and CON's answers to PRO's own questions (conceding tunnels can be justified at genuine bottlenecks while denying that this scales to "heavy investment" as policy) were more precise and better closed the loop. PRO's case on disruption costs and land scarcity was substantive and not fully neutralized, but PRO never fully bridged the necessity-versus-scale gap, and PRO's closing repeated the scarcity framing without directly answering CON's final "creating a right-of-way doesn't create a budget" rebuttal. PRO's opening and second rebuttal were also clipped for exceeding word limits, a real execution cost that cut off argument development at key moments. CON's tighter discipline, sharper pressure, and cleaner closing gave it the more persuasive path to the resolution as worded.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A1: "'outperform surface options' is not the whole test... your question assumes repeated victories in that comparison; your case establishes repeated infrastructure needs. Those are not equivalent") most decisively exposed the unclosed gap in PRO's case between demonstrated necessity and the motion's demand for heavy national investment, a point CON's closing then reinforced with "creating another right-of-way does not create another budget."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO built the stronger path to victory by showing that structural scarcity across multiple sectors (transport, drainage, power, water, communications) predictably forces bottlenecks where surface optimization collapses into displacement trade-offs, and that sustainable delivery requires heavy upstream investment in capability and pipelines rather than ad hoc projects. CON landed a sharp analytical blow by exposing that recurring need does not automatically equal recurring justification against all other capital demands (A4.1–A4.2), but CON never particularized those superior forgone alternatives forcefully enough to overcome PRO’s concrete multi-sector case; CON’s objection remained more abstract than PRO’s positive demonstration of necessity. PRO also successfully absorbed the opportunity-cost worry by noting that deferral often produces costlier retrofits under greater future density, while CON’s “prepare but do not commit” distinction, though clever, conceded too much of the intergenerational and cross-sectoral frame. PRO’s clipped opening and rebuttal imposed a real execution cost and kept the debate competitive, but did not erase PRO’s live structural route to affirming the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON Rebuttal 2 (A4.1–A4.2): exposing that PRO equated recurring infrastructure needs with recurring tunneling victories in priority comparisons against other demands on scarce capital—a gap PRO never fully closed.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON won the scale clash. PRO showed tunneling can beat a destructive surface alignment at bottlenecks and that selective wins can aggregate, but CON consistently pressed the missing comparison: next tunnel vs best forgone public investment, not just vs surface. PRO's two clipped speeches incurred execution costs and left the heavy-as-portfolio claim assertive. A4's answers conceded aggregation is possible while exposing that recurring needs ≠ recurring tunneling answers and that capability/preparation does not equal a heavy construction commitment, which crystallized why heavy investment remains unjustified as a presumption.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4's A1/A2 - conceding heavy aggregation is possible in principle but shifting the test to priority vs other repairs/upgrades and distinguishing recurring decisions from recurring answers, plus separating preparation investment from construction pipeline, directly undercutting B3.1/B4 capability argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Both sides ran a genuinely engaged, well-structured debate, but CON (Side A) more successfully controlled the decisive frame: whether "heavy investment" must be a prior policy presumption or merely a possible outcome of rigorous, case-by-case appraisal. CON's PQ answer (A1) was a shrewd, honest concession that reframed the whole debate around burden of proof without conceding the substantive fight, and its Rebuttal 2 point that PRO's "sustained delivery capacity" creates an idle-capacity-or-lowered-standards dilemma was never squarely answered by PRO in the closing, which instead repeated the "small footprint ≠ small responsibility" line. PRO's PQ answer to CON's Q1 ("widespread is the wrong test") also sidestepped the request for a decision rule/evidence rather than directly engaging it. CON's closing line distilling the clash ("let necessity produce the portfolio, don't declare the portfolio necessary first") gave the cleaner final reason to vote, even accounting for the clipped opening as a real but minor execution cost.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point ([A4.5]) that PRO's promised "sustained delivery capacity" with "no obligation" to keep funding creates an unresolved idle-capacity/pressure-to-approve dilemma, which PRO's closing never directly neutralized.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON wins because it showed that PRO’s “heavy investment” either collapses into ordinary project-by-project appraisal or creates institutional pressure to fund marginal schemes, while cheaper, flexible alternatives can address most constraints. PRO’s bottleneck argument stayed plausible but never proved a sustained national tunnel portfolio is generally necessary rather than exceptional.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal, which demonstrated that PRO’s aggregation rule is identical to project-by-project justification and that maintaining specialist tunneling capacity without a guaranteed pipeline forces idleness or lowered approval standards.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON's sequencing/collapse argument was decisive: PRO conceded risk-adjusted whole-life appraisal and no obligation to keep tunneling, which leaves "heavy" as a possible outcome not a prior doctrine. PRO never proved qualifying corridors are numerous/widespread enough across countries to justify a general heavy presumption, nor resolved the fixed-cost pipeline incentive. B's small-footprint vs small-need distinction is strong but does not outweigh the opportunity-cost and flexibility case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.5 collapse — that PRO's own rule (aggregate projects whose benefits justify costs) is CON's project-by-project standard, and that sustaining specialist capacity creates pressure to approve marginal projects unless a pipeline is already proven, undercuts PRO's live route to heavy-as-strategy.
