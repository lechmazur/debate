### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides argued competently, but B identified and sustained a decisive structural weakness in A's case: A's opening promised that a default consent right would economically protect individual illustrators, writers, and musicians, but under pressure A conceded (A4.2) that platforms would likely respond by striking bulk licensing deals with large media conglomerates rather than paying individual creators. A's only reply was that this would at least be "legitimate," which abandons the economic-protection rationale and retreats to a thinner consent-for-its-own-sake claim. B pressed this contradiction repeatedly (B2.2, B2.5, B4.5, B5.3) and it was never squarely resolved, while A's counter that incumbents already dominate via scraping infrastructure did not fully answer B's narrower point that a licensing-based moat specifically locks out independent creators and smaller/open-source developers. B's legal framing (pattern-extraction vs. reproduction) also stayed closer to a coherent, hard-to-dislodge line throughout the debate, and B answered pressure questions with slightly more direct engagement. A's "public visibility is not a commercial license" framing remained intuitively appealing but did not resolve the practical payoff problem B kept exposing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's repeated exploitation of A's concession that AI developers would pivot to bulk licensing deals with corporate catalog holders (first raised B1.4, pressed in B2.2/B2.5, and never adequately rebutted after A4.2 conceded the mechanism while calling it "legitimate") was the most decisive exchange, undercutting A's core promise that the proposed right would economically protect ordinary creators.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO won a competitive debate by keeping the motion’s actual decision rule—creators’ baseline right to stop unconsented commercial use of their own public works—clearer than CON’s side-effects case. PRO’s category distinction between human study and industrial-scale extraction for market substitutes held up better under pressure, and CON never fully justified an unconditional free entitlement once creators need public visibility to earn. CON landed real damage on concentration and weak individual leverage, but those practical objections did not neutralize the consent baseline or convert “no windfall checks” into a reason to leave scraping as the default.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO R2 (B4.1–B4.3): answered CON’s legal/leverage pressure directly, then flipped CON’s admission that models need broad contemporary human data into a contradiction with free, unconsented seizure.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully framing the proposition as an unprecedented expansion of copyright that protects against observation rather than reproduction, and by decisively weaponizing Side B's concessions regarding corporate licensing. Side A proved that the default right would bypass independent creators in favor of bulk corporate deals, turning the proposition's ostensible beneficiaries into its primary casualties.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's radio/streaming analogy by distinguishing between transmitting actual works and synthesizing new expressions, and capitalized on Side B's concession that platforms would pivot to corporate catalogs to prove the policy guarantees independent creator obsolescence.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and answered pressure questions directly with proper A1/A2 formatting. B's monopoly-entrenchment argument and idea/expression framing were legally grounded and consistently pressed. But A landed the more damaging unanswered blow: the reversal showing that the status quo CON defends already lets Big Tech build free data moats, so CON's "monopoly" fear is actually truer of the world without any consent right than with one. A also sharpened the debate by reframing the dispute as about unauthorized reproduction of whole protected files during training, not mere "idea extraction," which B's closing never squarely rebutted (it kept answering with output-level arguments). A's collective-licensing answer to the monopoly question was imperfect but gave a concrete institutional alternative that B's "micro-licensing is impossible" reply only partially undercut. The debate was close and both sides progressed cleanly across rounds, but A ended with the less-answered structural point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's reversal in [A2.3]/[A4.1] — that unpaid mass ingestion under the status quo is precisely what entrenches Big Tech moats, so CON's monopoly warning cuts against its own position — combined with B's answer never fully closing that gap, was the most decisive exchange.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO built the clearer path by locking the clash on consent and asymmetric extraction, consistently showing that status-quo free scraping already cements Big Tech moats while a default stop-right plus collective licensing restores agency without banning AI. CON’s open-learning and monopoly-entrenchment points were sharp and repeatedly pressed, but never fully escaped the unpaid-subsidy contradiction PRO exposed. Later rounds narrowed rather than widened, and PRO’s weighing on voluntary commercial use versus involuntary feedstock proved more decisive for a third-party reader.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 reverse of CON’s monopoly claim: free web strip-mining already builds capital moats; consent attaches cost and enables collective leverage instead of entrenching incumbents.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully dismantling Side A's core analogy that AI training is equivalent to human reading or search indexing, emphasizing the mechanical ingestion of protected files to generate commercial substitutes. While Side A presented a highly compelling practical argument that default opt-in requirements would entrench Big Tech monopolies by pricing out open-source developers, Side B effectively countered that the status quo already subsidizes Big Tech through uncompensated extraction. Side B's normative framing around consent, involuntary expropriation, and the distinction between public display and commercial surrender was more cohesive and directly addressed the proposition's focus on a "default right." Side A's reliance on the "unprotectable ideas" defense struggled against Side B's point about the wholesale copying of fixed digital files during the training process.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 dismantling the search engine and human learning analogies by highlighting that AI bypasses the creator to generate market substitutes, unlike search which drives traffic, and unlike humans who do not mechanically ingest millions of files to automate production.
