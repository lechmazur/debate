### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO built a durable frame—cash bail is a wealth test that leaves wealthy dangerous defendants unsupervised—and used it consistently through pressure questions and closing. Crucially, PRO's rebuttal that New York's reform banned dangerousness-based risk assessment (so it wasn't the model under debate) neutralized CON's strongest empirical anchor; CON's counter (a vague, unelaborated claim about NJ "backlash" and rollbacks) never fully restored that evidence's force. CON's best theoretical points—algorithms are backward-looking and miss situational violence, and supervised release can't physically stop a bullet—were vivid but never resolved the core asymmetry PRO pressed: under cash bail, wealthy volatile offenders buy total freedom with zero oversight, a point CON's Q1 answer (family indemnitors) sidestepped rather than answered. PRO's answers to pressure questions were more directly responsive and tied back to the comparative framework (risk-based detention vs. wealth-based release), giving the debate a cleaner resolution in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's [A2.3] move distinguishing New York's outright bail elimination (no risk assessment) from the actual proposition (validated risk assessment + supervised release) was decisive: it stripped CON's marquee real-world example of relevance, and CON's later attempt to salvage the empirical point via vague NJ "backlash" claims never fully recovered the lost ground.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won the comparative. It showed that cash bail does not detain according to dangerousness and can release wealthy high-risk defendants without supervision, while risk-based detention can release poor low-risk defendants and hold genuine threats. Side A identified plausible false negatives but never established a rate-level increase in violence, relying mainly on hypotheticals and isolated incidents. B also distinguished New York from the specific model in the proposition and offered more relevant proof-of-concept jurisdictions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s response that cash bail is a price tag rather than physical containment was decisive. It exposed that A compared reform against perfect incapacitation rather than the actual cash-bail baseline, and A’s family-accountability answer did not overcome the wealthy-offender loophole.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won by decisively dismantling Side A's core premise that cash bail effectively incapacitates dangerous individuals. Side B repeatedly and effectively highlighted the "wealthy offender loophole," demonstrating that cash bail allows dangerous people with money to walk free with zero supervision, whereas the proposition's model allows judges to deny release based on objective risk. While Side A made strong points about algorithmic blind spots regarding situational violence, Side B successfully countered that validated risk assessments inform rather than replace judicial discretion and factor in current charges and pending cases. Side B's structural comparison was cleaner, more logically sound, and ultimately more persuasive, leaving Side A to defend a system with a glaring, unresolved safety loophole.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal, where they neutralized Side A's New York example by pointing out that NY explicitly prohibited the use of dangerousness standards and risk assessments—meaning it did not implement the model being debated—while contrasting it with New Jersey's successful implementation.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): The pivotal clash was B's "math contradiction" argument—that you cannot simultaneously slash jail populations and expand preventive detention of dangerous offenders without algorithms being fudged. A neutralized this cleanly by arguing (A4.1) that most detainees are low-risk nonviolent people, not a reclassified violent minority, so decarceration and targeted preventive detention are not in tension. B never directly refuted this distributional claim in Rebuttal 2, instead reasserting the contradiction abstractly. A also won the dueling case-study exchange: A's distinction that NY lacked risk-assessment and dangerousness review (unlike the proposition as framed) effectively defused B's central empirical counter-example, and B never engaged with that distinguishing move. B's own counter on NJ rollbacks, raised late in Rebuttal 2/Pressure Answers, was a fair point that went unanswered in A's closing, leaving one live thread for B, but it wasn't developed enough or paired with rebuttal of A's stronger distributional logic to flip the debate. B's private-accountability-network mechanism (co-signer liability) was an original and plausible point sustained across rounds, but A's counter—that bail forfeiture incentivizes court appearance, not violence prevention—was never fully rebutted either, making this exchange roughly a wash. Overall A provided a more coherent, responsive throughline that better withstood pressure, while B's most damaging argument was answered better than B's rebuttal to that answer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response in A4.1 to B's "math contradiction" pressure question—reframing jail populations as mostly low-risk nonviolent detainees rather than a pool of dangerous people needing reclassification—was the most decisive exchange, since B never adequately countered this distributional claim afterward.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the cleaner comparison: cash bail poorly tracks danger, whereas risk-based detention can hold dangerous defendants and release poor, low-risk defendants under actual conditions. Side A never established that reducing poverty-based detention requires lowering safety thresholds, and its central claim that bondsmen and relatives provide continuous behavioral surveillance was implausible and repeatedly conflated preventing crime with ensuring court appearance. Both sides leaned on loosely supported jurisdictional claims, but B had the stronger causal case and answered the pressure questions directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s decisive response was that A demanded perfect prediction and physical intervention from supervised release while defending cash bail, which provides neither and can release affluent high-risk defendants. This neutralized A’s monitoring critique and exposed the weakness of its supposed private surveillance network.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won by decisively dismantling Side A's core arguments regarding private accountability and the mathematical trade-off of decarceration. B correctly identified that cash bail guarantees court appearance rather than public safety, effectively neutralizing A's claim that bondsmen and families act as a proactive private surveillance network. Furthermore, B cleanly resolved A's attempted contradiction about jail populations by explaining that current jails are disproportionately filled with low-risk, indigent defendants; releasing them drops populations without requiring the release of high-risk individuals. B's rebuttals were sharper, more factually grounded, and directly answered the pressure questions with superior logic, while A relied on overstated claims about bail mechanics and brittle assertions regarding state rollbacks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to Side A's claim that algorithms and decarceration targets force the release of dangerous offenders. B clarified that jail populations are inflated by low-risk, poor defendants, so releasing them achieves the population drop without compromising the preventive detention of high-risk individuals, completely deflating Side A's central contradiction.
