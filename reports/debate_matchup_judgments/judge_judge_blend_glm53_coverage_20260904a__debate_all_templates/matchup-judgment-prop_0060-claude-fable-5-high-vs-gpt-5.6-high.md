### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Side B wins by successfully leveraging the motion's exact wording ("many" and "full-time") and out-weighing Side A on the "team ledger" clash. While Side A effectively argued that remote work exports coordination delays to teammates, Side B persuasively countered that full-time office attendance exports interruption costs to focused workers. Side A's heavy reliance on pandemic data as the definitive measure weakened after Side A conceded it was contaminated. Side B's flip of Side A's "insurance" metaphor sealed the debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing effectively turned Side A's "insurance" metaphor, arguing that full-time attendance charges a daily, guaranteed premium (commute and fractured concentration) for a strictly conditional payout.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): CON wins by cleanly establishing an evidentiary asymmetry and a systemic accounting frame that PRO could not escape. CON’s strongest move was demonstrating that PRO, when pressed twice for objective measured support, could only offer vague assertions that trials "have often found" gains while failing to name conditions, roles, or metrics [A3.1, A4.1]. Meanwhile, CON placed a concrete directional pattern on the table—longer hours with flat or falling output during the pandemic transition—that PRO never neutralized with better data, only with the plausible but non-decisive contamination objection. Even more damaging was PRO’s concession in [B4.2] that exported coordination costs "belong on the team ledger." Once CON shifted the debate from isolated individual focus time to team-level "net" productivity, PRO needed to show the team still netted positive, but PRO never offered measured support for that claim. CON crystallized this in the closing as an issue of invisible, delayed, systemic costs that the motion’s own word "net" requires us to count. PRO’s "marginal day" logic and insurance-metaphor response were crisp, but they did not recover from the burden of showing that the predictable daily tax of commuting and interruptions is outweighed by coordination benefits that PRO conceded were only occasional for the "many" independent workers in question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal (A4) was the decisive exchange: it exposed that PRO’s answer to the evidence pressure remained vague and mechanism-driven, then leveraged PRO’s [B4.2] admission that exported coordination costs belong on the team ledger to force systemic accounting that PRO never discharged with concrete team-level measurements.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by effectively leveraging the specific wording of the proposition, focusing on "many" knowledge workers and contrasting WFH with "full-time" attendance. A successfully argued that while some coordination is necessary, it justifies planned office days rather than a blanket full-time mandate, which imposes daily commute and interruption costs on deep-work days. A's dismantling of B's "insurance" metaphor in the closing crystallized the clash perfectly. B's reliance on pandemic-era data and the "team ledger" argument was adequately neutralized by A's point that office interruptions also externalize costs onto focused workers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal of the "insurance" metaphor in the closing, pointing out that paying a daily premium of commutes and interruptions for conditional, unpredictable coordination needs is systematic overpayment, not productive insurance, combined with the Rebuttal 2 point that instant office access merely externalizes interruption costs onto the focused worker.
