### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing the prohibition as overinclusive. Side B repeatedly grounded its strongest harms in germline inheritance, while failing to justify banning non-heritable somatic enhancement in consenting adults. B's enforcement-collapse answer was plausible but could not overcome A's point that invisible edits also make a ban difficult to enforce, while therapeutic gene editing means the relevant clinics and expertise would continue to exist. A therefore offered the cleaner regulatory alternative and met B's pressure more directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1-A4.4 was decisive: A answered unverifiability with ex ante licensing and audits, then showed that B's logic would also threaten therapeutic somatic gene editing and that a blanket ban cannot reliably detect the same invisible conduct. B's closing largely repeated the paperwork objection without resolving those counterarguments.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully confining Side A's harms to heritable germline editing, forcing A to argue that the somatic/germline distinction is unenforceable. When A argued that gene edits are "invisible" and thus require a total ban, B decisively pointed out that invisibility makes a ban just as unenforceable as regulation, and that A's logic would absurdly require banning therapeutic somatic editing too.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal, which weaponized Side A's "unverifiability" argument to show that if edits are invisible, a strict ban is impossible to enforce and would logically demand banning medical gene therapies.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) wins because PRO never identified a harm specific to non-heritable somatic enhancement in consenting adults—the only class the motion actually targets. Every major harm PRO raised (future generations, embryos, off-label germline drift) concerned heritable modifications already banned under separate rules. CON decisively rebutted PRO's enforcement-collapse narrative by showing that (1) ex ante regulation (licensing, supply chains, protocol audits) already polices invisible medical boundaries, and (2) if structural unverifiability justified banning recreational somatic editing, it would equally justify banning therapeutic somatic gene therapy—a bullet PRO refused to bite. CON's "invisibility cuts both ways" argument demonstrated that a strict ban is no more enforceable than licensing against bad actors, while only licensing creates records and oversight. By the end of the debate, PRO had still not named a victim of the specific somatic adult edit his proposition would criminalize, leaving the burden of justification unmet.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8) answering PRO's enforcement-collapse and unverifiability claims by arguing that ex ante regulation already manages invisible boundaries (controlled substances, IVF), that the same invisible edits occur in therapeutic somatic gene therapy, and that invisibility makes a ban equally unenforceable while driving the practice offshore—directly neutralizing PRO's only remaining route to victory.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by exploiting the breadth of strict prohibition: it separated self-regarding somatic enhancement from germline intervention and offered licensing plus a direct germline ban. Side B persuasively identified uncertainty, commercialization, and social pressure, but never established that regulated somatic enhancement must produce germline abuse or that every acceptable-risk adult use warrants prohibition. A incurred costs for unsupported assurances of safety, clipping, and nonexistent C-reference labels, keeping the result competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal most decisively answered the germline-slippage pressure: commercial incentives do not themselves prove legal boundaries unenforceable, while autonomous adult modification and parental imposition can be separated by consent and third-party-harm principles.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully isolating the debate to somatic edits, neutralizing Side A's heavy reliance on germline risks. Side B decisively defeated Side A's claim that medicine forbids grave non-therapeutic interventions by listing concrete counterexamples, and effectively dismantled Side A's slippery-slope argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's R1 systematically crushing Side A's claim that medicine never accepts consent for grave non-therapeutic risks by listing elective surgeries and living kidney donations.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON wins because it successfully severed PRO's strongest appeals from the actual resolution. PRO built much of its case on germline risks and inherited harm, but the motion explicitly concerns consenting adults, and CON showed that adult aesthetic enhancement targets somatic tissue that is not inherited. PRO was left relying on a speculative slippery slope—that a legal somatic market would inevitably erode the germline ban—but CON undermined that prediction with concrete analogies (we did not ban all pharmaceuticals to stop opioid abuse; FDA already maintains the somatic/germline boundary) and a clear limiting principle: consent must be self-regarding, which bars minor/parental edits but fully protects autonomous adults. Meanwhile, CON neutralized PRO's "fictional consent" argument by demonstrating it proves too much, since the same social pressures justify banning cosmetic surgery, gym culture, and makeup, all of which remain legal. PRO never supplied the mechanism showing why enforcement would uniquely fail here, so the call for strict prohibition remained an unsupported prediction rather than a justified ban.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that PRO's "consent is fictional" and social-pressure arguments prove too much—since identical pressures already apply to legal cosmetic surgery, dental work, and gym culture, none of which are prohibited—defanged PRO's autonomy critique and forced PRO to bear the burden of showing genetic enhancement is categorically different, which PRO never established.
