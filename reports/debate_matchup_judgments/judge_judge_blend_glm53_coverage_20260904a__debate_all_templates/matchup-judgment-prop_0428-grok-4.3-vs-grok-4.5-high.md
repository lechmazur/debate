### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by presenting calibrated permission rather than an unchecked right to open venues, then weighing manageable noise and parking costs against exclusion and isolation for residents lacking private or institutional gathering spaces. Side B raised a credible enforcement and reversal-cost objection, but largely repeated that imperfect enforcement makes safeguards ineffective without establishing why conditional permits, fixed hours, capacity limits, and sunset reviews would generally fail. Side A also better defended the proposition as written, while Side B drifted toward preserving the existing residential baseline unless venues could guarantee no erosion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to the claimed one-way ratchet was decisive: denial also entrenches a costly status quo, whereas conditional approvals with sunset review at least make both community benefits and externalities testable. Side B’s reply invoked political inertia but did not squarely defeat the proposed automatic review structure.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's framing that these venues serve 'outsiders' and demonstrating that renters, younger people, and shift workers are residents who need these spaces. Side A also provided a stronger comparative analysis on enforcement, noting that we already manage noise from construction and deliveries rather than banning them entirely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A1 and A2), which pointed out that enforcement limits already exist for accepted uses like construction and deliveries, and that automatic sunset clauses with hard metrics solve the 'political reluctance' problem Side B relied upon.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the clash on manageability vs veto and equity. CON's reversal-cost/enforcement ratchet was the strongest CON route, but PRO answered it with sunset reviews, measurable thresholds, and the analogy to other regulated noisy uses we don't ban. PRO's Q2 forced a damaging concession in A4.2 that churches/schools don't serve night-shift renters, leaving CON without an alternative for that group, which crystallized the decisive trade: provisional, regulated annoyance vs permanent exclusion. That comparative weighing was clearer than CON's status-quo protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q2 in Pressure Questions - which church/school stays open for the night-shift renter with no yard - drew an explicit concession in A4.2 that none does, undermining CON's substitute claim and anchoring PRO's equity/reversal weighing in both later speeches.
