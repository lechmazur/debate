### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): CON wins by exposing a decisive internal contradiction in PRO's model: meaningful digital transferability requires ongoing enforcement that functionally replicates the revocable licensing architecture PRO condemns. PRO never provides a coherent technical account of how one-time verified deletion works across backups and devices without persistent platform oversight. While PRO raises real concerns about consumer deception and lost libraries, these amount to transparency critiques rather than a workable alternative. CON successfully distinguishes physical and digital scarcity and crystallizes the clash in closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal showing that a secure "one-time" transfer requires exactly the kind of persistent, centralized oversight that PRO opposes—banking works through reversible ledgers, while cryptography cannot stop private backups—demolishes PRO's analogy between digital ownership and physical resale.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): B won the technical feasibility clash that decided the debate. PRO's moral "buy button" point survived, but B exposed that PRO's own one-time verified transfer requires persistent central checking for backups/cloud copies — the revocable authority PRO condemns — and A never solved that after pressure. Combined with repeated clipping for word-limit excess, PRO left its core model self-contradictory while CON's physics/economics route stayed live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.3 - banking analogy reversal showing crypto transfer needs a trusted ledger with freeze/reverse power, and that true singularity can't be enforced without ongoing device checks, making ownership collapse into licensing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing and capitalizing on the central technical contradiction in Side A's case: enforcing digital transferability without enabling piracy requires the exact persistent, centralized DRM architecture that Side A condemns. Side A's answers to this paradox were logically incomplete, relying on a "one-time" verification that Side B correctly noted would require invasive, ongoing oversight to prevent local backups. Furthermore, Side A suffered execution costs from multiple clipped turns. Side B's pressure questions and rebuttals sharply dismantled PRO's mechanism, making CON's defense of licensing as a practical necessity highly persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, which dismantled Side A's "one-time transfer" mechanism by explaining that verifying the deletion of local backups and cloud copies requires the exact persistent centralized control and DRM that PRO explicitly opposes.
