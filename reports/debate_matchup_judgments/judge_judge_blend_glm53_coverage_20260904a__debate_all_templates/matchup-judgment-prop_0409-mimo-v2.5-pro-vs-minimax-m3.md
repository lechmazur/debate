### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A (CON) won on the core clash because it consistently demanded a concrete funding mechanism from PRO and supplied one—concession contracts with reinvestment percentages, sunset clauses, and public reporting, anchored by the Central Park Conservancy and High Line examples. PRO's counter-mechanism ("adequate public funding" backed by "cities choose priorities") never graduated from assertion to plausible process. The "formally open versus actually usable" reframe in CON's Rebuttal 2 and closing was the most decisive argumentative move of the debate: it shifted the evaluative metric away from PRO's symbolic openness and onto genuine maintenance and access, and PRO never squarely answered it. CON also partially neutralized the High Line gentrification counter by correctly distinguishing a housing and zoning problem from an enclosure problem, and that distinction held through the exchange. PRO's strongest surviving argument—the ratchet/dependency problem and the observation that zoning tools already exist yet waterfronts still shrink—was real pressure that CON's "democratic accountability" answer did not fully satisfy. But this was outweighed by PRO's inability to close the mechanism gap. PRO's multiple clipped speeches (Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2, Closing) were an execution cost that left some threads visibly unfinished. CON answered both pressure questions directly with A1:/A2: structure and concrete content; PRO's A1 effectively reframed CON's question but then used DC and European capitals as a loose approximation rather than a grounded counter. On cumulative argumentative discipline, CON built the cleaner and better-grounded path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's High Line gentrification attack [A2.1]: CON correctly separated the gentrification story (a housing and zoning failure) from the enclosure claim (the park itself remains free and diverse), stripping PRO's flagship counter-example of its force and then immediately demanding PRO explain who maintains the space if commercial revenue is ruled out. This exchange left PRO's equity argument weaker than it entered the round.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A wins by effectively severing Side B's main premise. A concedes that commercial revenue is acceptable (at the edges) but draws a hard line against footprint enclosure. Side B fails to prove why the revenue specifically requires physical enclosure of the commons. Furthermore, A's argument about the 'dependency trap' definitively neutralizes B's proposed mechanisms for preventing infinite enclosure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing explanation of the dependency trap, which showed that B's 'democratic accountability' brakes fail because a city reliant on lease revenue cannot politically afford to revoke those leases.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): CON won by grounding the debate in the material failure of municipal budgets to maintain public space without reliable revenue streams. CON successfully severed the link between commercial revenue and enclosure by citing functioning mixed-use models (the High Line, Central Park) that remain freely accessible, and provided concrete contractual safeguards (reinvestment requirements, sunset clauses, public reporting) to prevent the ratchet effect. PRO's principled case against enclosure was compelling, but their alternative—relying on tax revenue and political will—remained abstract and counterfactual against decades of documented underfunding. PRO's repeated word-limit clipping and incomplete speeches compounded their execution costs, leaving their closing case literally unfinished.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The pressure question exchange on funding mechanisms: CON exposed that PRO's alternative lacked a concrete delivery mechanism beyond hope, while CON provided specific contractual tools and real-world examples showing that mixed-use models can keep public space both free and well-maintained.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate. The decisive clash was whether a principled line exists between acceptable incidental commerce and enclosure of the civic asset. PRO articulated this standard crisply in A1 of Rebuttal 2 — "the underlying civic asset stays open to any person who shows up" — and held it under pressure throughout. CON's strongest move was catching PRO endorsing parking fees and concession agreements as themselves forms of paid access, attempting to collapse PRO's distinction into aesthetics. But CON's own answer to PRO's first pressure question — admitting it could not name a single beach where a private club on ten percent improved the other ninety — materially weakened its structural logic argument, its primary response to the specificity problem. PRO's counter that chronic underfunding making privatization "inevitable" is "a counsel of despair dressed up as pragmatism" was rhetorically effective and substantively sound. CON was clever in noting that parking fees and kiosk leases sort citizens by willingness to pay, but never fully destroyed the intuitive and legally coherent difference between transactional services layered onto an open asset versus reserving a slice of the commons behind a turnstile. PRO's standard survived the exchange; CON's alternative produced no clean line of its own. Both sides were generally disciplined with sourcing, avoiding brittle fake precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2, where it drew the explicit standard — the underlying civic asset stays open to anyone who shows up, with commerce being incidental versus reserving access itself — directly answered CON's sharpest pressure question while simultaneously explaining why parking fees and kiosks are distinguishable from gated beach clubs. CON's attempt to collapse this distinction as merely aesthetic (parking gates the shoreline too) was real pressure but ultimately less persuasive than the live standard PRO offered, especially after CON admitted it could not name an example supporting its structural logic.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A established a clear, intuitive distinction between incidental commerce (which keeps the commons open) and paid enclosure (which fences it off). Side B attempted to collapse this distinction, but struggled to explain how a kiosk is an "enclosure." Furthermore, Side A decisively won the Pressure Questions round by forcing Side B to admit they had no real-world examples of their core mechanism, leaving Side B's benefits entirely theoretical against Side A's highly plausible harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Q1 and subsequent Rebuttal 2 exposing Side B's inability to name a single real-world example of a private club improving the surrounding public beach, which cemented Side A's claim that enclosures function as a "sorting machine."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) defended the proposition by drawing a principled and textually grounded distinction between incidental revenue tools that keep the underlying civic asset open to all, and genuine enclosure that reserves public land for paying customers only. Side A (CON) produced a strong funding critique but could not verify its central empirical claim that private clubs cross-subsidize public space, and undermined its credibility by misdescribing Side B as endorsing waterfront-closing festival permits. Side B answered pressure directly, maintained the equality and commons framing, and crystallized the clash more cleanly in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's pressure questions (B3) exposing Side A's lack of verified examples for hybrid cross-subsidy, Side A's honest but damaging admission that it could not name one (A4.1), and Side B's subsequent clear articulation of the open-asset-versus-reserved-asset standard (B4.1–B4.2).
