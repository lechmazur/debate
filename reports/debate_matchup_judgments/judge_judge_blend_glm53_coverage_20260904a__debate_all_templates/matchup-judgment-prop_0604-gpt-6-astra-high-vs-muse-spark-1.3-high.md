### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and handled the Pressure Questions round with proper A1/A2 formatting, but B's rebuttal work was slightly sharper and more decisive. B's central "two-tier Europe" framing—that licensing freezes compliant EU hospitals/factories/researchers on stale models while doing nothing to stop weights already trained, hosted, or queryable from abroad—was never fully neutralized by A. When A tried to turn this into a symmetry argument against withdrawal orders (A4.6), B had already preemptively distinguished the two mechanisms in Rebuttal 2 (withdrawal acts on evidenced harm; licensing blocks all lawful use pre-emptively), and A's repeated symmetry claim in the second rebuttal did not add new force against that distinction. A's strongest material—burden-of-proof framing, irreversibility of diffusion, bounded review with a 30-day deadline—was a genuinely strong precautionary case, but its concession that hospitals "can continue using the authorized version" while upgrades are reviewed effectively validated B's core critique about competitive lag rather than refuting it. B's closing crystallized the clash more memorably and left less unresolved pressure on the table.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.2–B4.3), distinguishing withdrawal ("acts after a specific harm is evidenced") from licensing ("blocks every lawful EU use before any harm is shown, while the same capability remains queryable from abroad") and turning A's own upgrade-review fix into evidence of a "two-tier Europe," was the most decisive exchange, since A's later attempt to re-assert symmetry (A4.6) did not fully answer this distinction.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A systematically dismantled Side B's core impact (prevention of irreversible diffusion) by pointing out that a regional EU license cannot stop global diffusion, weight theft, or foreign queries. Side A then successfully weaponized Side B's own 30-day review timeline to prove that licensing actively harms local security by freezing defenders on stale models.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A2 and A4.3, which effectively differentiated withdrawal from licensing regarding foreign evasion, and turned Side B's 30-day upgrade review into a major vulnerability ("defenders frozen on stale models").
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON built the clearer path to victory by showing licensing adds a permission gate that neither contains irreversible global diffusion nor improves on existing advance duties, suspension, and liability, while locking lawful EU users onto stale authorized versions. PRO’s burden-shift default was conceptually sharp and consistently defended, but did not neutralize the two-tier freeze, foreign-query asymmetry, or iteration drag that remained live after answers on upgrade thresholds and review deadlines.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 on asymmetry and two-tier Europe (withdrawal targets evidenced harm after the fact; licensing freezes every lawful EU use while attackers and foreign hosts run the frontier).

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides argued a sophisticated, closely matched case, but CON maintained a tighter and more consistent definitional line: risk-based regulation can already include mandatory independent verification, pre-launch evidence submission, and preventive stop orders, so PRO had to show what "licensing" adds beyond those tools. PRO's repeated "self-certification" characterization of CON's position was directly and repeatedly rejected by CON from the opening onward, and PRO never fully engaged with CON's specific stop-order mechanism as an answer to "who says no before launch." Crucially, in Pressure Questions CON asked what happens if a compliant applicant is stuck when the authority misses its review deadline; PRO's answer—permitting "provisional staged deployment" under monitoring and liability without completed clearance—handed CON exactly the concession it needed: PRO's own fallback looks identical to the risk-based regime CON was defending. CON exploited this cleanly in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, and PRO's closing response ("nothing can make after-the-fact enforcement pre-market") never resolved why its own provisional mechanism didn't already prove CON's point. PRO's aviation/pharma analogies and irreversibility framing were vivid but did not survive CON's narrower rebuttals about disanalogy (open-ended GPAI use vs. defined operating envelopes) and entrenchment. Overall CON gave the cleaner, more resolved path to a decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question about a missed regulatory deadline, and PRO's answer permitting "provisional staged deployment" without completed clearance, which CON then used in Rebuttal 2/Closing to show PRO's own safety mechanism collapses into the risk-based model it was arguing against.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by systematically dismantling Side B's core claim that only a mandatory license can prevent harm. A successfully establishes that risk-based regulation can still enforce stringent pre-deployment obligations, like mandatory evidence submission and preventive stop orders. When Side B attempts to solve the regulatory bottleneck by conceding that models could enter "provisional staged deployment" if regulators miss a deadline, Side A masterfully weaponizes this concession, proving that enforceable safeguards can protect the public even without completed clearance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (A4.4) brilliantly turns B's "provisional staged deployment" concession into proof that the licensing gate itself is unnecessary for pre-market safety.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won by narrowing the decisive clash to whether a mandatory market-entry gate adds safety beyond tools both sides accept—independent verification, investigative power, stop orders, and pre-release conditions. PRO’s irreversibility and “who can say no” framing stayed vivid, but CON repeatedly showed those powers can bind before ship without universal discretionary clearance, then turned PRO’s own missed-deadline “provisional staged deployment” answer against the claim that only completed permission makes safeguards pre-market. PRO never cleanly established unique safety value of the gate after that concession, while the bottleneck/incumbent weighing remained live.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2/closing: PRO’s provisional deployment without finished clearance undercuts the claim that only the licence makes tools binding before harm.
