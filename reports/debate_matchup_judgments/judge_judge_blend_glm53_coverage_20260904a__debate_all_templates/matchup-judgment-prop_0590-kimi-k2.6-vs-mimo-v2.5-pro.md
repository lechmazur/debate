### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): This was a genuinely competitive debate with real intellectual substance on both sides. CON's strongest contributions were the deployer-as-compliance-anchor argument (A4.1) and the market-placing obligations point (A4.2), which together represent a coherent answer to the "no physical importer" problem PRO kept pressing. However, PRO sustained its core framing more cleanly across all five turns: the jurisdictional anchor belongs where harm materializes, the SaaS/API era has no customs queue for software, and even a hard-to-enforce legal predicate is superior to structural exclusion. Three of CON's speeches were clipped before the judge saw the full argument — Opening, Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2, and Closing — representing a real execution cost that left key burdens only partially developed. PRO had no clipped speeches, answered both pressure questions directly with A1:/A2: labels, and its closing crystallized the clash rather than widening it. CON's best rebuttal move — deployers already bear compliance duties under the Act — was developed late and PRO's response (deployers lack technical control over the model they didn't design) was never fully neutralized. The live unanswered burden favoring PRO: CON never squared the circle between "the deployer is your compliance anchor" and "the deployer cannot fix discrimination baked into a model it did not build." That gap kept PRO's central claim alive at the close.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's B2.4 noting that when algorithmic harm materializes the "responsible architect sits abroad, insulated from direct accountability while a European gatekeeper with no technical control bears the blame" was the most decisive exchange. CON answered in A4.1 by identifying the deployer as the compliance anchor, but never closed the loop on why a deployer with no authority over the model's design adequately protects the injured citizen — leaving PRO's veil-piercing argument standing at the end of the debate.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): PRO built the stronger path to victory by catching CON's self-contradiction early: CON's proposed alternative (regulate EU importers/deployers) is itself triggered by the fact that AI impacts fall within EU borders, making the opposition's jurisdictional objection a dispute about enforcement routing rather than first principles. PRO answered pressure questions promptly with proper A1/A2 labeling, honestly conceded the hard case (direct API with no EU intermediary), and showed that CON's model also resolves that case through the EU deployer—meaning both models converge on the same enforcement entity. The decisive edge is PRO's argument that explicit impact-based jurisdiction at least preserves the EU's legal authority to act if enforcement pathways emerge, while CON's market-entry-only model abandons the jurisdictional claim entirely against foreign providers with no EU touchpoints. CON's territorial-market-entry distinction was real and well-maintained throughout, but PRO correctly identified it as a difference in enforcement vehicle rather than jurisdictional premise, and CON never fully escaped that framing. CON's enforcement and fragmentation concerns were genuine but addressed sufficiently by PRO's market-leverage and DSA-style arguments. PRO's philosophical coherence, sharper first rebuttal, and better pressure questions gave it the cleaner path across the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 [B2.1–B2.2] catching CON's self-contradiction: CON proposed regulating "EU importers or deployers," and PRO correctly identified this as impact-based jurisdiction implemented through an intermediary—the trigger is still that impacts fall within EU borders. CON spent the rest of the debate attempting to distinguish "market entry" from "impact" as the formal jurisdictional hook, but that move never fully neutralized PRO's core framing, and CON's own enforcement proposals consistently required EU-based entities, lending continued force to PRO's claim that CON was defending a difference in routing while conceding the underlying jurisdictional premise.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by cleanly distinguishing between the normative motive for regulation (preventing local harm) and the functional basis for jurisdiction (territorial market entry). Side B attempted to co-opt Side A's enforcement model, but Side A successfully showed that relying on local intermediaries proves the necessity of territorial jurisdiction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction in the final rounds that Side B was confusing the motive for the law with the basis for jurisdiction, effectively dismantling Side B's claim that Side A had conceded the debate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): B won because it maintained a clear distinction between territorial market regulation (via importers/deployers) and true extraterritorial claims over foreign providers, while showing A's enforcement mechanisms collapse into the same local-hook approach. A tried to reframe B's positions as concessions but failed to resolve the no-intermediary gap or provide a workable direct hook, with added execution costs from clipped turns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 2 and answers to pressure questions exposed A's reliance on EU-based entities, forcing the concession that direct cases default to deployer liability rather than provider jurisdiction based purely on impacts.
