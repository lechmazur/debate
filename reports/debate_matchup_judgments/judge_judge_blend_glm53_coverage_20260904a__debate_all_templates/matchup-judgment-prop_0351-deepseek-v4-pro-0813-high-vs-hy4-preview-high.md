### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent causal stories and executed the pressure-question format cleanly with proper A1/A2 answers. PRO's strongest asset was the "lag" framing (current output masks future stagnation) and the apprenticeship/sponsorship mechanism, sharpened in rebuttal by distinguishing residue from judgment. But CON preemptively flagged the unfalsifiability risk in the lag argument in its opening, then sustained a consistent "relocation, not loss" reframe with a clever twist: the old office system's proximity bias was itself a hidden pipeline problem, so remote work's auditable trail (tickets, calendars, threads) makes allocation visible and manageable rather than hidden. The decisive exchange came when PRO's closing tried to convert CON's own "role sorting" explanation (B4.5) into proof of hidden harm, but CON's closing turned this back around effectively: sorting by task visibility is not new, and its visibility on-screen (versus the old invisible seating-based sorting) means it is a recognized, addressable problem rather than an understated one. Since CON had the last word and it directly neutralized PRO's most creative closing maneuver while staying tied to the motion's actual comparative burden ("more than usually admit"), CON's case ends the debate in a stronger position, though the margin is not large because PRO's mechanism-based case was substantive throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's closing response to PRO's "role sorting is the hidden harm" pivot—arguing that visible task allocation (backend tickets vs. client-facing work) is auditable and thus a known, manageable friction rather than proof of understated damage, while tying it back to the claim that the old office ran the same sorting via invisible proximity bias.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by keeping the comparison focused on development after hiring and explaining why acceptable current output can conceal weaker judgment, sponsorship, and promotion prospects. Side B effectively challenged measurement and showed that office apprenticeship was unequal, but its access gains did not answer A's same-junior developmental comparison. Its claim that remote juniors receive less visible assignments also reinforced A's causal mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing turn on role sorting was decisive: Side B argued that remote juniors often receive backend work while on-site juniors receive visible, client-facing assignments, and A correctly reframed that not merely as a confound but as career damage caused through the work arrangement. Side B's reply that allocation is auditable did not show that the developmental loss disappears or is fully admitted.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a live path: remote converts apprenticeship into auditable residue, thins feedback and sponsorship, and hides as role-sorting. CON's relocation/access reply explains visibility but never guarantees calibration for juniors who don't know what to ask, and its same-employer defense was turned in closings when CON admitted backend-ticket sorting – PRO showed that sorting IS the understated pipeline loss, not a measurement artifact. The admitted baseline of loneliness vs unadmitted promotion delay survived.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B5.1-B5.4 turning A4.5 – CON said divergence is role sorting not remote; PRO reframed sorting into the motion's worsening mechanism: working from home determines who gets developmental assignments while still looking productive.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides built plausible, well-grounded causal stories, but B identified and repeatedly exploited a structural tension in A's case: A's opening insisted the harm was "invisible" and disguised by short-term metrics, yet A's rebuttals leaned on visible employer behavior (mandating office days for juniors) as proof of harm. B's pressure questions crystallized this ("if employers are acting on it, what exactly is still being under-admitted?"), and A's answer—distinguishing a "vague mentoring gap" admission from a "structural career tax" confession—was a plausible but ultimately thin rescue that never fully dissolved the contradiction. B also weakened A's baseline by reframing the office as an uneven, exclusionary lottery rather than a reliable apprenticeship system, which A only partially answered ("office was uneven but forced exposure"). B's closing sharpened this into the decisive frame: PRO's own falsification standard (matched promotion velocity/sponsorship) was never met with comparative evidence, only inferred from policy shifts that are themselves evidence of admission. A's case was vivid and mechanistically rich (sponsorship-as-political-capital, "efficiently unavailable" seniors), but it never cleanly escaped the "have it both ways" bind B kept pressing, making B's path to victory clearer and more resolved by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question ("If employers are acting on it, what exactly is still being under-admitted?") and its follow-through in Rebuttal 2/Closing, forcing A into a strained distinction between symptom-admission and cost-confession that never fully closed the gap.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A offered a persuasive mechanism around lost tacit learning and the conversion gap between visible output and sponsorship. However, Side B exposed that A’s principal proof—junior office mandates—neither demonstrates slower promotion nor coheres cleanly with the claim that the harm is under-admitted. A identified matched promotion and sponsorship as its falsification test, then asserted failure without supplying a comparative basis. B also plausibly showed that remote records can broaden access beyond the office’s uneven proximity system. B’s erroneous reference IDs were an execution cost, but not enough to outweigh its cleaner burden analysis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s decisive response was that A set equal promotion velocity and sponsorship as the proper test but inferred unequal outcomes merely from office mandates. Distinguishing mandates from promotion evidence directly weakened both A’s harm claim and its under-admission claim.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON wins on the motion's second clause. PRO builds a vivid tacit-learning and sponsorship story, but CON forces a falsification standard in Pressure Q2 and then shows PRO never meets it: PRO defines falsification as equal promotion velocity/sponsorship [B4.2] yet cites only office mandates [B2.5] as harm. CON turns that evidence into admission, arguing explicit mandates and constant mentoring discussion mean the risk is admitted, not hidden, and that remote's written, searchable record broadens access versus the old proximity lottery. The conversion-gap vs legibility clash is left with PRO asserting hidden tax while CON explains mandates as onboarding/coordination.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Q2 forced PRO to stipulate equal promotion velocity/sponsorship as falsifier, then A4.4 used that commitment to show PRO's only evidence was office mandates, not cohort promotion data, collapsing the 'more than they admit' claim.
