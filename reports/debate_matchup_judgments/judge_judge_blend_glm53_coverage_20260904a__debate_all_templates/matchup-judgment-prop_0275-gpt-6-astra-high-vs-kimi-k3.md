### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and followed format well, but CON built the more resilient structural case: it conceded little ground while exploiting PRO's own concession that fee disclosure is largely redundant under existing drip-pricing law, then isolated the remaining "house rules" territory as the genuine site of dispute. CON's privacy-policy/boilerplate analogy and "penalty for omission converts curation into coverage" mechanism gave a plausible causal story for why the mandate could actively worsen comprehension, and PRO's rebuttal (rules are already enforceable at checkout, so moving them earlier changes nothing) didn't fully neutralize the distinct legal-duty-to-display incentive CON identified. PRO's strongest asset—the late-arriving-family example and the "wasted comparison effort" framing—was met with a market-incentive counter (hosts who hide disqualifying rules lose bookings) that PRO answered only with "hope isn't a guarantee," a comparatively thin response. CON's closing crystallized the debate cleanly around "certain costs vs. already-happening benefits," which was harder for PRO's closing to dislodge. PRO's trigger definition (checkout/request-to-book vs. inquiry) was a reasonable answer to CON's Q2, but CON's follow-up that enforcement still turns on contested "what the action does" kept that pressure partially alive. Overall CON narrowed the dispute more effectively and left PRO defending a narrower, less clearly proven "sliver" of unique benefit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.1-B4.2 response to A3.1 (Q1), distinguishing contract-enforceability incentives from legal-duty-to-display incentives, was the sharpest single exchange—it directly answered PRO's pressure question while reinforcing CON's boilerplate-inflation mechanism, and PRO never fully dismantled the distinction in Rebuttal 2 or Closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully narrowing the debate to the marginal impact of the mandate and proving that its unique mechanism creates negative unintended consequences. A's Rebuttal 2 expertly established the causal link for defensive rule-inflation by distinguishing between a commercial risk and a regulatory violation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 perfectly answered Q1 by explaining how a legal duty to display rules converts curation into defensive coverage, cementing the 'privacy policy' harm.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively splitting the motion and neutralizing both halves. They successfully argued that the fee disclosure requirement is redundant given existing drip-pricing laws and market shifts, forcing Side B to defend the exact timing mandate for house rules. Side A's "privacy policy dynamic" argument—that a legal duty to display pre-booking changes the penalty for omission and drives defensive boilerplate—was highly persuasive, especially when clearly articulated in Rebuttal 2. Furthermore, Side A effectively countered Side B's primary example (the late-arriving family) by pointing out that hosts already have strong market incentives to disclose disqualifying rules to avoid cancellations and disputes. Side B was competent but struggled to overcome the redundancy and compliance friction arguments, relying on a "guarantee vs. hoping" framing that was less compelling than Side A's market incentive logic.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they decisively answered the pressure questions by explaining the causal mechanism of defensive rule-inflation (omission under legal duty becomes a violation, converting curation into coverage) and demonstrated that hosts already voluntarily surface strict check-in windows to avoid the exact friction Side B described.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but PRO (A) sustained a clearer throughline: a concrete example, a behavioral mechanism (drip-pricing anchoring at checkout), and a decisive dilemma against CON's "obligation-free review screen" hypothetical (if harmless earlier disclosure costs nothing, the mandate is costless; if platforms resist it, that resistance is itself evidence of manipulative intent). CON (B) mounted a genuinely strong counter-case distinguishing navigation labels from substantive protections and pressed A on whether its own "conspicuousness" standard also requires judgment calls, forcing A into a partial concession in closing that the "harmless click" does no independent harm. That concession narrowed A's win to a proportionality argument (bright lines permissibly catch some harmless conduct) rather than a direct harm claim, and B capitalized on this cleanly in its closing. However, A recovered with a sharper error-cost comparison (near-zero cost of redundant early disclosure vs. continued legal drip-pricing behind a "gentle" review screen that has no enforcement mechanism guaranteeing it stays gentle), while B's alternative regime remained largely hypothetical and never explained what would actually stop platforms from re-inserting pressure tactics at its proposed review screen absent the very mandate it opposes. That practical gap, plus A's more vivid framing and effective use of the pressure-question exchange, gives PRO a clear but competitive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Rebuttal 2 exchange where A's "label determines nothing, only sequence" answer produced a fork (harmless-and-costless vs. resisted-and-confessional), which B then used to claim A had conceded its review-screen workflow — and A's closing partially conceded "the harmless click does nothing wrong," shifting the debate to a proportionality argument that B directly challenged in its final speech.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side A won by successfully separating the regulation of honest total prices from the specific mandate to display itemized breakdowns and full operational rules before checkout. A effectively neutralized B's core "drip pricing" offense and then dismantled B's "bright line" defense by exposing that B's reliance on "conspicuousness standards" still requires the same subjective enforcement B criticized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's R2 and Closing expertly pointed out that B's defense of a "bright line" rule collapses because B explicitly relies on "conspicuousness standards," meaning both worlds require subjective, screen-by-screen enforcement.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B built a much stronger, more practical case grounded in the reality of platform incentives and user psychology. They effectively trapped Side A with a dilemma regarding the cost of moving disclosures one click earlier, and successfully argued that Side A's "gentle checkout" relies on an imaginary platform design that voluntary markets do not produce. Side B's error-cost weighing in the closing crystallized the clash perfectly, proving that the downside of their bright-line rule is trivial compared to the downside of Side A's patchwork standards.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, specifically the "fork" argument exposing that if the mandate is harmless, CON's objection is purely semantic, and if it is harmful, it proves platforms want to manipulate users inside the funnel.
