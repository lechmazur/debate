### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): B won because it established the stronger population-wide mechanism: defaults convert procrastinating workers into savers, while larger deductions disproportionately reward people already willing and able to contribute. A compellingly highlighted liquidity, low defaults, and leakage, but overstated these harms and never adequately showed that deductions would create enough new, persistent saving to outperform enrollment. B's reliance on escalation, matches, and rollovers beyond enrollment itself, plus erroneous reference IDs, kept the debate competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure that deductions require both upfront liquidity and tax liability forced A to concede they do nothing for current zero savers. A defended that concession well for truly cash-strapped households, but did not neutralize B's larger group of workers who can save yet fail to act because of inertia.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): B successfully reframed the debate around net wealth adequacy rather than participation headcount, and demonstrated that auto-enrollment creates shallow, leaky, potentially debt-financed accounts that fail to generate genuine long-run security. A's reliance on automatic escalation and employer matches to solve inadequacy went beyond the proposition, while B's critique of debt substitution and cash-out leakage remained largely unanswered. B's closing crystallized the core trade-off effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 marked the decisive turn by exposing A's inconsistency on inertia—arguing that if inertia is strong enough to boost enrollment, it is also strong enough to trap cash-strapped workers into harmful debt and low default rates—and by distinguishing persistent adequate saving from fragile participation.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A (CON) won by successfully reframing the debate from mere participation to the quality and persistence of net wealth. CON's rebuttals were exceptionally sharp, particularly the devastating comparative point that employer matches exist in both systems, but tax deductions incentivize workers to contribute enough to capture the full match, whereas auto-enrollment defaults leave free money unclaimed due to inertia. CON also brilliantly turned PRO's core "inertia" argument against them, demonstrating that strong inertia forces cash-strapped households into high-interest debt substitution rather than allowing them to safely opt out. Side B (PRO) relied heavily on catchy phrases like "cannot compound a zero," but failed to adequately answer CON's pressure regarding debt substitution and leakage, making CON's path to victory much clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 [A4.3], where they dismantled Side B's employer match defense by showing that a deduction-incentivized saver is pushed to capture the full match, while the 3% auto-defaulter systematically leaves free money on the table.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won through the clearer population-level comparison: automatic enrollment creates balances for people otherwise left at zero, while larger deductions disproportionately reward people already willing and able to save. Side A identified genuine liquidity, anchoring, and leakage risks, but never established that deductions would motivate low-income non-savers or outperform enrollment overall. Its answer that voluntary control protects liquidity conceded that many vulnerable households would simply remain without retirement savings. Some of B's precise participation and design claims were insufficiently grounded, but its core mechanisms and baseline comparisons remained stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's decisive response was that both policies fund the same illiquid, potentially penalized accounts, so Side A's liquidity objection does not uniquely favor deductions. B then sharpened the comparison: its imperfect account can still produce net wealth, whereas A's voluntary model often leaves the relevant household at zero.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won by demonstrating that auto-enrollment attacks the extensive margin of non-savers, while tax deductions are structurally regressive and ineffective for households with near-zero marginal rates. A decisively turned Side B's liquidity critique by showing that CON's own policy also relies on the same illiquid, penalized retirement accounts—except without auto-enrollment those households never build a balance to begin with. B never recovered from this comparative framing or adequately answered how deductions create new savers among the most insecure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's turn in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 that tax deductions for voluntary contributions are also locked in illiquid retirement accounts with the same early-withdrawal penalties, so CON cannot solve the liquidity crisis it identifies; combined with the crystallizing line that A's worst-case scenario (opt-out) is simply B's everyday case (permanent non-enrollment).
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B dominated the debate by exposing a fatal flaw in Side A's case: retirement accounts are inherently illiquid and subject to early withdrawal penalties regardless of how a worker enrolls. Side B effectively argued that Side A's alternative (increasing tax deductions) subsidizes this exact same illiquidity but only for those who voluntarily save, while doing absolutely nothing for the zero-balance households that need security most. Side B also cleanly neutralized Side A's anchoring and leakage concerns with practical design solutions (auto-escalation tied to raises, auto-portability) and stark baseline comparisons (e.g., $1,400 after penalties is still better than the $0 baseline under Side A's system). Side A failed to overcome the mathematical regressivity of tax deductions or adequately answer Side B's pointed pressure questions, leaving Side B with a much stronger, more logically sound path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's dismantling of the liquidity crisis argument in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2, pointing out that Side A's preferred tax deductions apply to the exact same penalized, illiquid accounts, thereby subsidizing the very "debt trap" Side A claimed to oppose.
