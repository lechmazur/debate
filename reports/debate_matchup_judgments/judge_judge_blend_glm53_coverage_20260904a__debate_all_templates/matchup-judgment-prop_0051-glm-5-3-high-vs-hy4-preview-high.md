### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON executed more cleanly. CON's core mechanism critiques were concrete and hard to dislodge: the "pennies per item" claim was directly punctured by the fixed-cost-on-a-$3-item incidence argument, and the "audit then squeeze the shadow subcontractor" example exposed a real gap in PRO's disclosure theory that PRO's "order volume vs. declared capacity" reply only partially patched. CON's answer to the apparent contradiction PRO raised in pressure questions (inspection capacity vs. recalls) was logically clean: ex post targeted bans/recalls use data platforms already hold, unlike impossible ex ante tracing of millions of parcels — this neutralized what looked like PRO's best "gotcha." PRO's case was strong in framing (the "fire brigade vs fire code" and "reactive vs preventive" reframes were sharp and its flip of the regressive-harm argument was persuasive), but it leaned on somewhat hand-wavy mechanisms (platform "leverage," vague "red flags") that CON kept puncturing with concrete exit/forgery dynamics. PRO also suffered a real execution cost: its Opening and first Rebuttal were both clipped before completing points, including the direct rebuttal to the regressive-access objection, which is treated as a genuine cost under the judging frame. Both sides correctly used Q1/Q2 and A1/A2 formatting, so no format penalty there. On balance, CON's tighter, more concrete rebuttals and cleaner handling of pressure gave it a modest but clear edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 point that on a $3 item fixed testing/verification costs are "a large slice of price, not pennies" directly and effectively punctured PRO's unsupported "pennies per item" claim, and CON's ex ante/ex post distinction in Rebuttal 2 cleanly resolved the contradiction PRO's pressure question tried to expose.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by demonstrating that CON's own reactive alternative—targeted bans, recalls, and delisting—depends on the very platform traceability and obligations CON claims are impossible, while offering no remedy at all for labor abuses. PRO's closing crystallized this asymmetry effectively: CON wants the fire brigade after harm but rejects the upstream fire code. The regressive-cost framing remained contested, but PRO successfully reframed the status quo as a hidden transfer of risk onto poor shoppers, and showed that imperfect obligations still shift incentives upstream where CON offered only enforcement theater.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal and closing exposed that CON's proposed safety tools (targeted recalls, SKU delisting, buyer notification) implicitly concede platform-level duties and traceability, contradicting CON's core claim that ex ante traceability is impossible, and highlighted that CON has zero mechanism to address labor conditions.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO wins the central comparison. Despite three clipped turns, A exposes that B's own alternative — targeted bans, delisting, purchase-record recalls — requires the platform traceability and duties B calls impossible, collapsing the "toll vs filter" distinction [B4.1 vs A5.2]. On labor, B has no targeted-ban analogue, leaving a live route that dashboards plus liability make spikes visible via volume vs capacity. B's regressive-price argument is strong but PRO counters that cheap unsafe goods transfer unpriced health risk onto the same poor shoppers, and B never justifies why cross-border alone should be exempt from duties every domestic seller carries.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Q1 in Pressure Questions — that recalls/bans require the traceability B says doesn't exist [B1.3 vs B2.8] — and A5.2 crystallizing it as fire brigade conceding fire code, which B4.1's ex ante/ex post distinction never fully resolves.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, escalating cases, but Con's "certain regressive costs versus a speculative mechanism" frame proved more durable under pressure. Con's pressure questions forced Pro to specify how a pre-listing safety gate actually works, and Pro's answer (an accredited test-report gate) was then undercut by Con's technically sound point that batch-level physical safety testing can't be automated the way IP-matching can, plus the sharp economic point that lawsuit exposure vastly outweighs "lost fee revenue" as a platform incentive—an argument Pro never directly rebutted. Con's closing then weaponized Pro's own concession that the safety file "can be forged" to collapse the mechanism's credibility, landing the more decisive final blow. Pro's best counter—turning Con's "payment holds/chargebacks" alternative into support for pre-sale platform leverage—was clever but arrived late and didn't fully repair the unanswered incentive-asymmetry point. Con also had an unexploited tension (relying on customs sampling after arguing customs is too weak to inspect parcels), but Pro never called this out, so it cost Con nothing. Overall Con's throughline was tighter and better defended.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Con's rebuttal 2 (B4.1-B4.2), distinguishing automated IP-matching from batch-variable physical safety testing and arguing lawsuit risk swamps "lost fee" incentives, followed by the closing's use of Pro's own "can be forged" concession—this sequence did the most damage to Pro's core mechanism claim.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): CON wins because it successfully undermined PRO's core mechanism. By distinguishing automated IP enforcement from physical, batch-variable safety verification, CON showed that platform liability would likely trigger mass purging of small suppliers rather than genuine verification, and it capitalized on PRO's closing concession that safety files can be forged. PRO's chokepoint theory remained plausible, but CON demonstrated that the promised safety benefits were administratively speculative compared to the certain regressive costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's distinction between IP and safety verification (B4.1), which broke PRO's "platforms already do this at scale" argument and reinforced the consolidation and forgery problems.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): PRO wins by converting the enforcement paradox into a chokepoint advantage: auditing a few platforms with pre-listing safety files and factory disclosure versus inspecting millions of parcels, and by reframing regressive-cost as vulnerability to burns/toxins you can't sue a ghost for. CON's strongest hits — IP vs batch testing asymmetry and lawsuit vs lost-fee purge incentives plus forgery risk — land, but CON then concedes payment holds/chargebacks/market surveillance as viable, leaving no ex-ante alternative for anonymous $5 dresses. The motion accepts slowdown, so certain price/delay does not defeat concrete traceability gains.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 A1 — IP detection is exact-reference automatable while safety needs batch physical testing, so platform liability = lawsuits vs mere lost fees, predicting mass purge of small sellers not verification — directly undercut A3 Q1 and exposed the core mechanism weakness.
