### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by making the central comparison easier to decide: inheritance at death is far less substitutive of new sales than immediate, repeatable resale, while the present loss of paid-for libraries is concrete. Side A raised credible platform-cost and perpetual-chain concerns, but its prediction that the law would eliminate purchasable libraries remained speculative and did not defeat B's ownership-versus-subscription response. Side A was also clipped twice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's sharpest exchange distinguished death from resale: resale can immediately and repeatedly replace new purchases, whereas inheritance occurs infrequently and preserves only one non-duplicated entitlement. Side A's friction and perpetual-chain answer created pressure but did not establish comparable market substitution.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): B built the stronger path to victory by anchoring the debate in the concrete, intuitive mismatch between a "Buy" button and a confiscated library, then parrying CON's economic objections with a clean distinction between resale and mortality. CON's case suffered real execution costs from two clipped speeches and never offered a satisfying answer to the daughter with 900 books, retreating instead to an abstract consumer-law remedy that PRO showed was practically hollow.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The distinction between resale (voluntary, repeatable, immediate) and death transfer (involuntary, unrepeatable, once per estate), which repeatedly neutralized CON's central claim that inheritable digital assets would functionally become a zero-friction resale market and collapse publishing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO (B) kept the decisive moral/economic tradeoff in focus: one-time purchase at ownership prices, one non-duplicating copy with decedent access extinguished. CON's licence and zero-friction chain arguments were answered by mortality not substituting for purchase and by paperbooks already persisting generations. CON's clipped speeches and vague "regulator refund after closure" remedy left pressure Q B3.2 unanswered, while A's Q about subscription was answered with honest buy/rent tiers. Death-as-friction plus Buy-button adhesion was the cleaner live route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3-B2.4 and B4.3-B4.4 — involuntary, unrepeatable, decades-later inheritance does not substitute for a new sale, so no resale market; plus turning CON's subscription threat into an honesty argument.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by staying closer to the motion and showing that succession requires transfer of one existing entitlement, not duplication or perpetual platform survival. Side A raised sharp concerns about privacy, indivisibility, and durability, but B answered privacy through ordinary wills and intestacy, while indivisibility and counterparty risk do not prevent other assets from being inherited. A’s preferred DRM-free-file regime was attractive but effectively conceded legally mandated transferability while substituting a broader consumer-law reform for the proposition. B’s two clipped turns modestly narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer that one seat transfers rather than multiplying, followed by the point that bankruptcy or title withdrawal limits an inherited asset’s value without disproving succession. This undercut A’s central claim that a legally inheritable digital entitlement must behave like permanent physical property.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON demonstrated that PRO's inheritance guarantee preserves the flawed license model rather than fixing it, leaving heirs with an unsplittable, unsaleable seat that expires when the platform does. PRO's concession that "the heir's claim dies with the entity" became the decisive blow: the guarantee is a receipt for a promise, not property. PRO's moral intuition that purchasers are deceived by "Buy" buttons was powerful, but CON successfully showed that honest labeling and rivalrous DRM-free files are the real cure, whereas bolting a death-triggered transfer onto a non-transferable license creates an incoherent, fragile entitlement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that an inherited digital seat is unsplittable and unsaleable, unlike houses or pianos which probate can liquidate, forcing a zero-sum fight among heirs with no market remedy. PRO never recovered from the distinction between indivisible-but-saleable physical assets and a locked, non-transferable platform account.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won the core design clash. PRO's one-seat transfer answered multiplication but left an unsplittable, unsaleable seat that cannot be divided for value like a house or piano, and conceded at [B4.3] that the claim dies with the platform. A framed this as promise vs property and contrasted it with a rivalrous file that is actually splittable, sellable, willable and outlives the seller. B's next-of-kin vs will defense neutralized the privacy point but did not rehabilitate the guarantee as a guarantee.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.3-A4.5 - A shows probate divides indivisible assets by selling them, which a non-transferable seat cannot do, so two of three children get nothing, then weaponizes B's own concession that the heir's claim dies with the entity to collapse the motion's meaning of guarantee.
