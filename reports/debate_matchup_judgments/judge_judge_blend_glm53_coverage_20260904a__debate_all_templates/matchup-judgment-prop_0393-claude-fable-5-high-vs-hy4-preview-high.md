### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by forcing A to specify what stronger standards would add beyond existing baselines, then showing the resulting tension between inexpensive adaptability and genuinely usable access. A established a credible lifecycle-cost and aging-in-place case, but never adequately outweighed B's supply and targeting arguments, and its claimed majority usage relied too heavily on needs already served by ordinary designs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal was decisive: it used A's newly specified standard to contrast curbless interiors on inaccessible upper floors with the exempted elevator, undermining A's claim that universality supplied access efficiently. A answered that some stair-capable residents would still benefit, but did not fully defeat B's feasibility and targeting comparison.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): CON won the decisive definition fight. PRO’s lifetime-cost and ordinary-need case was coherent, but once forced to name a “stronger” standard it either restated features already in fair-housing baselines, carved out elevators on small walk-ups (conceding feasibility targeting), or mandated interiors on floors the mobility-impaired cannot reach. CON kept the supply-at-the-margin mechanism live under the motion’s own modest-cost concession and crystallized a cleaner choice: targeted access houses people; blanket mandates decorate stairs. PRO never fully neutralized that route.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B R2 after PRO named step-free/curbless interiors without elevators: features on unreachable third floors plus the elevator carve-out as reductio of universalism.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by forcing Side B to define what "stronger" standards actually entail, and then exposing the logical contradictions in Side B's specific examples. Side A effectively demonstrated that the cheap, space-neutral features Side B championed are already required by existing fair housing laws. When Side B pivoted to genuinely stronger features like curbless showers and step-free thresholds for three-story walk-ups, Side A delivered a devastating rebuttal: mandating a step-free threshold on the third floor of a building without an elevator provides cost without corresponding access for wheelchair users. Furthermore, Side A successfully framed Side B's exemption of elevators in small walk-ups as a concession that feasibility and targeting must override strict universality. Side A also provided a much clearer causal mechanism for how modest costs kill marginal housing projects on thin spreads, while Side B relied on semantic dodges about the definition of "modest."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's concrete examples (step-free thresholds and curbless showers in elevator-less walk-ups) as absurdities that mandate "cost without corresponding access," and proved that Side B's elevator carve-out inherently conceded the CON principle of targeting over universality.
