### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): PRO (Side A) built and maintained the cleaner path to victory by identifying the central distinction—necessity versus priority—early and holding it throughout. CON's opening and rebuttals established convincingly that security is necessary, but PRO's A2.1 reframe correctly noted this proves only that security matters, not that it matters more. CON never dissolved this distinction with a genuine limiting principle: their answer to the limiting-principle question (constitutional structure, courts, legislatures) was somewhat circular because PRO correctly observed that those very institutions require privacy to function without being monitored into submission. CON's strongest blow was the "have it both ways" accusation—if warrants override privacy, security is the real master value—and PRO's answer (rights can have exceptions while remaining the default priority) was philosophically coherent but not crisply enough developed to fully neutralize it. Still, PRO's closing crystallized the debate cleanly: the recurring danger in a free society is not just bombs but normalized surveillance, and privacy is what makes security legitimate rather than sovereign. CON's closing was largely repetitive, restating "side-constraint" framing without advancing the weighing. Both sides were clipped multiple times, but the visible portions gave PRO the more sustained argumentative progression across rounds. CON's "monitoring is not suppression" line and physical-infrastructure argument had real force but were not sufficient to overcome the unanswered limiting-principle problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2.1–A2.4 turn in Rebuttal 1: "CON's case proves necessity, not priority" and the self-defeating strongman point—noting that the security-first logic CON endorses is precisely the political path to tyranny—materially damaged CON's framework and was never fully answered. CON's constitutional-structure response in B4.1 was too circular to close the gap.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a razor-thin but outstanding debate by successfully distinguishing between necessity and priority. Side B absorbed Side A's strongest argument—that security is a physical precondition for rights—by arguing that a precondition does not automatically become the highest value. Side A applied immense pressure with the argument that warrants overriding privacy proves security is the master value, but Side B masterfully reframed warrants as proof that privacy is the default condition. Side A's defense stumbled slightly when claiming that "monitoring is not suppression" to avoid conceding Side B's chilling effect mechanisms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 response (A1) explaining that warrants do not prove security is superior, but rather establish privacy as the default condition that forces security to pass strict tests.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side B won by consistently exposing A's concession that privacy yields to warrants/emergencies, proving security is the precondition and master value rather than a side constraint; A never neutralized this priority claim or offered a workable limiting principle. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): PRO built and maintained a coherent argumentative thread across every round: privacy is the default, security is the justified exception, and the asymmetry—security must always justify intrusion while privacy never needs to justify existence—is the moral hierarchy. The "security guards the house; privacy is the house" metaphor crystallized that framing memorably and was used consistently from opening through closing. CON's sharpest material—that procedural requirements are not the same as a moral hierarchy, that defaults are starting points not substantive rankings, and that at the point of collision privacy yields to security—was genuinely strong, especially in Rebuttal 2 and the closing's punishment analogy. However, CON had two speeches clipped for exceeding the word limit (Opening and Rebuttal 2), each at a point where the argument appeared to still be developing. That is a real execution cost: we cannot credit arguments that were cut before landing. CON's claim that "defaults are procedural, not moral" is the best counter in the debate, but PRO's response—that the structure of always placing the burden on the intruder and tightly fencing scope IS the substantive hierarchy—was at least equally plausible and better sustained across rounds. PRO also correctly noted early that every exception CON cited (warrants, exigency, screening) was itself bounded by privacy norms, and CON never fully escaped that trap. The nonexistent reference IDs in PRO's second rebuttal are a minor error but did not damage the argument. On balance, PRO's cleaner framing, consistent development, and CON's dual clipping costs give PRO a clear but competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was the running clash over whether procedural requirements reveal a moral hierarchy. PRO argued that because security must always justify intrusion and privacy never must justify its existence, privacy is the governing principle. CON responded in Rebuttal 2 that "defaults are procedural starting points, not moral hierarchies" and used the punishment analogy—we fence imprisonment, but that doesn't mean the offender's liberty outranks public safety. PRO had initiated this clash in Rebuttal 1 with "Every exception they cite is bounded by the very privacy they claim is secondary," forcing CON to develop the procedural/substantive distinction. CON's answer was philosophically sharp but came late and was partly clipped; PRO's framing had accumulated weight across prior rounds, giving it the slight edge.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's core logical framework. Side B argued that because security intrusions require justification (like warrants), privacy is the default and therefore the morally superior value. Side A decisively rebutted this by showing that procedural defaults exist to prevent arbitrary state power, not to establish a moral hierarchy, using the analogy that restricting criminal punishment doesn't mean an offender's liberty matters more than public safety. Side A also successfully argued that privacy itself relies on the security provided against non-state actors like stalkers and mobs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing successfully neutralized Side B's core "asymmetry" argument by explaining that procedural constraints exist to prevent arbitrary power, not to subordinate security, and that at the actual point of collision, privacy yields.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by establishing security as precondition for any usable privacy and using PRO's own concessions on bounded exceptions (warrants, exigency) to show security prevails at actual collisions. A's default/hierarchy argument and metaphors were clear but did not overcome the enabling role of security against private violence and coercion. B answered pressure directly and crystallized the live clash in rebuttals/closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) reframing procedural burdens as legitimacy rather than proof of privacy supremacy and distinguishing defaults from moral hierarchy.
