  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 completely neutralized B's "tiered access" defense by pointing out that viewing raw sequences inside a secure room is exactly the privacy violation A was warning about, rendering the noise added to exported data irrelevant.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by consistently framing the comparative choice as imperfect external scrutiny versus permanent opacity controlled by self-interested gatekeepers. While Side B landed sharp privacy objections—especially the inside-the-room exposure risk and the politicization of "societal impact"—it never fully overcame the asymmetry that platforms already exploit the same behavioral traces commercially with fewer safeguards. Side A's concrete tiered-access mechanism (secure research environments for rare trajectories plus formal privacy budgets on exported data) gave a plausible path to comply with the mandate, whereas Side B's alternative of direct regulation and data minimization remained underdeveloped about how regulators would know what to target without the very access the mandate provides. Side A also crystallized the clash more cleanly in its closing, making the decisive weighing easier to grasp.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over differential privacy and rare trajectories: CON showed that noise would drown small-subgroup findings or require privacy budgets so large as to be theater; PRO responded with tiered access (vetted analysts in secure rooms for raw sequences, differential privacy governing exports); CON countered that inside-the-room exposure to analysts is still a breach that output checks and penalties cannot undo. This sequence defined the central tradeoff of the debate.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's core "privacy-preserving" claim. When Side B conceded that raw data must be viewed in secure rooms to study rare trajectories, Side A pounced on the reality that this destroys the privacy promise for the most vulnerable users, as human insiders can still leak or memorize data. Side A also successfully neutralized Side B's strongest asymmetry argument ("platforms already use the data commercially") by arguing that two exploitations do not cancel and that the proper remedy is data minimization, not adding more watchers. Furthermore, Side A's distinction between technical safety metrics (like crash tests) and contested viewpoint judgments (like polarization) severely weakened Side B's regulatory analogies, leaving Side B's mandate looking like a dangerous political licensing scheme.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled the "secure room" concession by pointing out that noise on exports does nothing to protect the teen whose raw data is being stared at by a stranger inside the room, and its rejection of the asymmetry argument by stating that platform over-collection argues for collecting less, not deputizing an unaccountable research class.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively maintaining the 'safe vs. useful' dilemma. Side A clearly explained why longitudinal behavioral data cannot be anonymized without destroying the utility needed for Side B's specific examples (like cascades), and decisively defeated Side B's equivalence between regulator audits and researcher access.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 cleanly differentiated regulator audits (which output sanctions, not replicable studies) from researcher access (which outputs public methods vulnerable to differencing attacks), neutralizing Side B's strongest pressure question.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B sustained a decisive, unresolved contradiction in the proposition: mandated researcher access cannot be both privacy-preserving and useful for studying recommender-system harms like radicalization cascades. When pressed, Side A chose pseudonymized individual histories inside secure rooms with noise-checked aggregates leaving. Side B showed that longitudinal behavioral traces remain re-identifiable, while aggregates hardened against differencing attacks would be too coarse to answer Side A’s own causal questions. Side A never closed that technical gap, leaning instead on analogies to hospitals and census data that Side B successfully distinguished. Side B also cleanly answered Side A’s pressure questions by explaining how regulator audits differ in kind (fewer eyes, no publication, enforceable sanctions), and crystallized that exposure and platform whitewashing are not mutually exclusive. Side A’s closing was rhetorically strong but left the mechanism’s workability unproven, giving Side B the cleaner live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing that demonstrated pseudonymized individual histories inside secure rooms still enable re-identification, while noise-checked aggregates preventing differencing attacks would be too coarse to answer Side A’s own cascade and counterfactual questions—showing the mandate cannot deliver both safety and usefulness.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively exposing the privacy-utility paradox in Side B's model. Side A effectively argues that the high-dimensional, longitudinal nature of recommender data makes true anonymization impossible, and that the safeguards required to prevent re-identification (like noise-checked aggregates to block differencing attacks) destroy the utility needed to study the exact societal impacts (like radicalization cascades) Side B promised. Furthermore, Side A successfully defends its alternative of regulatory audits by distinguishing the leak surface of published, replicable academic research from closed-door regulatory sanctions, a distinction Side B attempts to wave away in closing but fails to adequately rebut.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it turns Side B's Pressure Question answer (pseudonymized histories inside, noise-checked aggregates out) against it, demonstrating that the required privacy safeguards would render the data too coarse to answer the headline research questions, thus collapsing Side B's core mechanism.
