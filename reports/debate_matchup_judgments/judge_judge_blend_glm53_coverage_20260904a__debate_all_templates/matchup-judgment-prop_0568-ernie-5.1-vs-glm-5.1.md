### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by keeping the debate centered on access to essential goods for unbanked and cash-dependent people, and by showing that B's alternatives impose fees, documentation, enrollment, or data burdens. B's strongest route was cost pass-through, but it relied on underdeveloped claims about dedicated lanes, regional margins, and innovation, while the proposition was limited to major retailers and existing essential transactions. B also effectively conceded that digital alternatives remain insufficient, which strengthened A's access argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal that prepaid cards, digital wallets, SNAP/WIC, and kiosks are not true substitutes because they impose fees, forms, data trails, or eligibility burdens was the decisive exchange; B never restored those alternatives as adequate access for cash-only essential purchases.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B won a blowout victory after Side A completely collapsed under pressure. Side B effectively neutralized Side A's security argument by pointing out that retailers already process cash for non-essentials. Crucially, Side A's second rebuttal contained a catastrophic concession, directly admitting that their proposed digital alternatives are "insufficient" and "require ID, fees, or enrollment that many cash-users lack," entirely validating Side B's core argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal powerfully reframed Side A's digital alternatives as "toll bridges" that extract fees from the poor, a framing so effective that Side A ultimately conceded the point.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.1 (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A maintained a clear, consistent route to victory: cash remains the only genuinely zero-barrier payment for millions of unbanked Americans, and the narrow mandate imposes negligible incremental cost on major retailers that already handle cash for non-essentials. Side B’s security objection collapsed when B conceded that cash drawers remain open for tobacco and electronics, and B’s crucial admission in Rebuttal 2 that digital alternatives are “insufficient” for the seven million unbanked households confirmed A’s central access crisis. While B’s cost-pass-through argument remained live, A successfully framed it as retailers externalizing costs onto the vulnerable rather than a genuine public-interest concern, and B never squarely rebutted the pharmacy-counter scenario that crystallized the stakes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s rebuttal dismantling Side B’s security argument by showing that since major retailers already accept cash for non-essential goods like cigarettes and electronics, refusing cash for essentials creates no incremental robbery risk while punishing only the cash-dependent customer—forcing B to abandon robbery and retreat to operational cost.
