### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): Both sides ran competent, structured debates, but PRO (Side B) holds a narrow overall edge. PRO's foundational claim—that default bias is a structural, psychological mechanism that placement exploits regardless of quality—survives the full exchange in better shape than CON's opposing pillars. CON's "users stay because they're satisfied" response [A2.1] is circular: it assumes the absence of genuine choice rather than demonstrating it, and CON never explains why OS vendors consistently bury default-change options if users genuinely prefer the first-party product. PRO's Q1 pressure [B3.1] sharpened this contradiction, and CON's answer [A4.1]—asserting "prominent prompts during setup"—is the most contestable specific claim in the debate given real-world iOS and Android history. CON's strongest surviving argument is the synchronized patching point [A4.2]: first-party tools can push OS-linked security patches simultaneously in ways third-party tools structurally cannot, and PRO's dismissal ("solvable engineering requirement") is unargued assertion rather than a refutation. This limits PRO's security-through-competition claim to partial credit. The transaction-fee vs. device-cost weighing ends roughly as a draw—both sides made plausible but unverifiable projections about who bears cost under each regime, though CON's "mandatory upfront vs. optional fee savings" framing [A5.2] has real structural force that PRO never squarely neutralized. Ultimately, the proposition concerns dominant providers, which tilts the analytical frame toward market-power concerns—PRO's home ground. CON defended a well-chosen set of objections but never produced a clean affirmative reason to let dominant gatekeepers control default placement; the case was purely defensive. PRO's closing crystallized the core trade-off more memorably ("the referee playing on the team"), and the default-bias argument remained the debate's most durable live claim.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question [B3.1]—asking why OS interface designs obscure default settings if user choice is genuine—was the sharpest single exchange. CON's response [A4.1] asserted "prominent prompts during setup," an empirically contestable claim given the documented friction in changing browsers or wallets on major platforms. This exchange materially undermined CON's recurring "30-second change" argument without CON producing a satisfying answer, leaving PRO's default-bias case as the debate's strongest live claim going into the closing.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): Side B won by making the central default-bias and gatekeeper-control argument clearer and more tied to the motion. Side A had a plausible security and integration case, especially around patching and vulnerable users, but too often assumed the rule would force unsafe third-party defaults rather than neutralize self-preferencing, and leaned on speculative subsidy claims and some overconfident unsupported factual assertions. Side B's own fee claims were not fully proven, but its less-restrictive answer of security standards plus neutral competition was enough to keep the pro route live and stronger.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's most decisive rebuttal was that easy settings changes do not defeat default bias, and that security can be handled through equal standards rather than letting the OS provider use safety as a justification for permanent self-preference.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a more resilient causal framework. A's default-bias mechanism provided a durable foundation for why pre-installation constitutes captivity rather than choice, directly undermining B's claim that users are merely satisfied. A successfully turned B's vulnerable-user argument by showing that low-income households suffer more from guaranteed lifetime transaction fees and monopolistic rents than from speculative upfront hardware subsidies, and that security is better served by competitive certification than by permanent incumbent monopoly. B's closing reliance on mandatory upfront costs versus optional fee savings collapsed because it presupposed users freely switch defaults, which A's inertia argument consistently denied. B also slightly mischaracterized the proposition as trading one default for another, and overreached with the specific claim that every major OS prominently prompts users during setup. A's closing crystallized the clash cleanly as guaranteed extraction versus hypothetical bugs, while B's final speech largely restated earlier points without resolving the core comparative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's turn on the low-income burden, showing that guaranteed monopolistic transaction fees hurt vulnerable users more than speculative upfront hardware subsidies, undermining B's core vulnerable-user case.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built a cleaner, more disciplined path to victory across all five rounds. The core proposition was simple and defensible: neutral defaults at setup neither ban first-party tools nor require compromising secure enclaves, but they do strip OS providers of the unearned, inertia-driven market advantage that costs consumers via excess fees and restricts access to better privacy tools. CON's primary rebuttal—that first-party integration is structurally necessary for secure enclaves—was largely assertoric, relying on rhetorical invocations like "you cannot legislate away physics" without explaining why the existing app-store vetting model, which PRO correctly identified as analogous, cannot be extended to enclave access. PRO's A1 answer (publish objective eligibility criteria, ban arbitrary denials, the app store already works this way) directly neutralized CON's gatekeeping concern without requiring fake precision. CON's sharpest pressure was Q2—alleging a contradiction between PRO's user-inertia claim and the claim that users will actively choose superior tools during setup. PRO's A2 answer was the debate's decisive moment: the distinction between passive post-setup inertia (settings almost never revisited) and active prompted choices during onboarding is real and well-grounded in ordinary UX knowledge, and it resolved the apparent contradiction cleanly. CON's closing recycled the security-as-physics framing without adding new comparative weight. PRO's closing crystallized the key trade effectively without overreach. CON had live surface area—gatekeeping incentives, fragmented attack surfaces—but never grounded those risks concretely enough to outweigh PRO's well-anchored consumer-harm case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A2 in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange. CON's Q2 correctly identified an apparent contradiction between PRO's inertia-based lock-in claim and the claim that users will voluntarily choose superior tools at setup. PRO answered by drawing a meaningful and well-grounded distinction: passive default inertia applies to settings users never revisit after onboarding, while active setup prompts—like the existing language, Wi-Fi, and accessibility screens—elicit deliberate, preference-aligned choices. This resolved CON's strongest pressure point cleanly and reaffirmed both the consumer-harm argument and the innovation-reward argument simultaneously.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B won because it kept the motion narrow: barring default preferencing does not ban first-party tools, integration, or security vetting. Side A’s security case was plausible but repeatedly overstated the rule as forcing unsafe kernel access or dismantling secure infrastructure. B also gave the clearer consumer-harm story around default inertia, fees, privacy, and foreclosure, and answered the innovation and friction objections with a simple setup-choice comparison. A’s attack-surface concern kept the debate competitive, but it did not prove first-party default preference is necessary for security.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s rebuttal that the proposition only removes passive default lock-in, while allowing secure vetted access and active user choice, was the decisive answer to A’s security and innovation framing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): PRO successfully reframed the debate away from CON’s false security-versus-competition dichotomy. By answering CON’s sharpest pressure questions with the app-store vetting analogy and the active-choice/inertia distinction, PRO showed that neutral defaults can preserve security while eliminating unearned monopoly rents. CON never squarely rebutted the core claim that default lock-in extracts billions in excess fees, and CON’s innovation argument undermined itself by simultaneously claiming first-party tools are superior yet unable to survive a neutral choice screen.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s second rebuttal (Turn 7) directly answering CON’s pressure questions: deploying standardized app-store-style vetting to neutralize the gatekeeping objection and distinguishing deliberate setup prompts from post-setup inertia to dissolve CON’s claimed contradiction.
