### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully detaching the proposed label from the harms Side A claimed it would solve. Side B cornered Side A into admitting the label wouldn't tell a user if they were actually penalized or suppressed, making Side A's "loaded odds" analogy fail.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 dismantling the casino analogy: "A notice that said only 'computers run the tables' would not disclose loaded odds. The material story is being smuggled in; it is not in the sentence."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): PRO successfully defended that AI-driven ranking is meaningfully different from traditional curation—opaque, adaptive, and operating at scale—and that a simple disclosure right functions as a necessary trigger for autonomy even if it is not a full cure. CON’s "empty notice" objection was sharp and sustained, but PRO’s "trigger not cure" framing, crystallized in the casino analogy, showed that knowing visibility is algorithmically constructed rather than organic materially changes consent, self-assessment, and consumer judgment. CON never fully overcame the intuition that being told an opaque AI scores your desirability is a non-trivial fact, and its "ranking is the product" refrain elided the distinction between inspectable rules and adaptive prediction. PRO answered pressure questions more directly and progressed the clash more cleanly, leaving a clearer live route to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s "trigger not cure" defense against CON’s "empty notice" critique, especially the casino analogy showing that knowing the system is machine-shaped changes the frame of consent without diagnosing individual harm.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling the utility of the proposed notice. A effectively demonstrated that a simple "AI ranks you" disclosure does not actually inform users whether they are being artificially suppressed, nor does it solve the self-blame problem since it cannot distinguish between algorithmic burial and a thin dating pool. A also successfully argued that focusing the right specifically on "AI" is arbitrary, as the underlying harms of opacity and manufactured scarcity apply to any ranking system. Side B's emotional appeals regarding intimacy were strong, but they could not overcome A's precise logical critiques of the motion's specific mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal to Side B's "loaded odds" analogy, pointing out that disclosing "AI ranks you" is akin to disclosing "computers run the tables" and does not actually reveal artificial suppression or "loaded odds."
