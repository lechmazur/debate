### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but B's structural critique proved more durable under pressure. B's fungibility argument (fiscal space must be redeployable for whatever crisis actually hits) and its reductio that "automaticity" doesn't eliminate creditor discretion but relocates it into certification, audits, and suspension triggers went largely unrebutted at the mechanism level. Crucially, in the Pressure Questions exchange, A's answer to B's Q2 (that famine and other non-climate shocks are "rarely detached from climate") overclaimed to the point that B turned it into a decisive reductio in Rebuttal 2: if every crisis can be labeled climate-related, the earmark either swallows everything or arbitrarily excludes urgent needs. A never answered this overbreadth charge, nor B's "moral excuse" point that earmarking gives creditors cover to write down less while claiming resilience credit. A's own answers to B's pressure questions leaned on assertions ("standardized metrics eliminate bilateral haggling") without explaining who certifies compliance, leaving B's veto-relocation critique intact through the final round. A's closing repeated the death-spiral framing without engaging these live wounds, while B's closing crisply reasserted the fungibility/control distinction that had never been squarely answered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point (B4.5) turning A's claim that "famine is climate-linked" into a reductio—showing the earmark's definition either captures every crisis or arbitrarily excludes real emergencies—was the most damaging, unanswered blow of the debate.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side B won by showing that resilience is valuable without proving debt relief should be converted into an automatic spending condition. Its comparison between fungible fiscal relief and a potentially slow compliance regime directly addressed the motion. Side A never adequately answered non-climate emergencies such as bank failures or health outbreaks, and its claim that standardization eliminates creditor vetoes remained asserted rather than demonstrated. B also persuasively argued that conditions can relocate bargaining into definitions, audits, and certification. A established the debt-climate cycle well, but did not show that automatic conditionality solves it better than unconditional relief paired with separately governed adaptation finance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange answered A’s standardization claim: even uniform resilience criteria still require contested definitions, audits, and enforcement. B then sharpened the dilemma that a broad climate definition makes the condition meaningless, while a narrow one prevents governments from responding to urgent non-climate needs.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won the core mechanism clash. PRO established the debt-climate spiral but never solved CON's decisive constraints: automatic earmark adds no new money, only a compliance regime that restricts fungible fiscal space needed for non-climate shocks, and relocates haggling to what counts/audits/suspension. PRO's answers that standardized benchmarks linked to NAPs remove vetoes and that famines are climate-linked did not neutralize the flexibility and control costs. CON's alternative – rapid unconditional relief for solvency plus separate adaptation grants – better preserved autonomy while answering survivability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON A4.5 / A2.3-A2.5 – showing automaticity doesn't end creditor haggling but moves it to certification and that if every crisis is re-labeled climate, the earmark either captures everything or justifies withholding relief for urgent needs.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly within the resolution and followed format correctly, but CON (B) won the decisive exchange. When pressed with "rubber stamp vs. rigid covenant" (B3.1), PRO's answer (A4.1) conceded that diversion to famine/epidemic triggers "mandatory corrective investment and tougher future terms" — an admission that the automatic conditionality does impose a real penalty on governments prioritizing survival. CON seized on this in Rebuttal 2 and Closing ("PRO's model actively penalizes governments... for prioritizing human life over donor-mandated covenants"), and PRO's closing never directly answered or neutralized this concession, instead retreating to the general "durable solvency" framing. CON also successfully held PRO to the actual text of the motion ("automatically contingent"), pointing out that PRO's softened design (earmarked shares, force-majeure pauses, substitute projects) was drifting away from what the resolution actually mandates — a legitimate and effective rebuttal technique that PRO did not fully rebut. CON's core frame — debt relief as emergency resuscitation versus climate finance as a distinct long-term tool — was clear, consistently applied, and never squarely dismantled by PRO. PRO's case was coherent and well-argued but left this specific, exploited weakness unresolved into the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B3.1's pressure question forcing PRO to admit consequences for diversion during famine/epidemic, and PRO's answer (A4.1) conceding "tougher future terms" — which CON then weaponized in Rebuttal 2 and Closing as proof of the penalization PRO claimed did not exist.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that automatic climate conditionality can conflict with the immediate liquidity and humanitarian purposes of debt relief. Side A offered sensible flexibility through partial earmarks, audits, and emergency pauses, but those additions weakened automaticity and did not fully resolve creditor coordination, enforcement, or the cost of tougher future terms. B also supplied the cleaner alternative: unconditional solvency first and separate grant-based climate finance. B's claim that every earmarked dollar necessarily causes catastrophe was overstated, which kept the margin moderate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange exposed the enforcement dilemma: if emergency diversion has no consequence, conditionality risks becoming toothless; if it brings corrective obligations or tougher terms, crisis-stricken governments are penalized for prioritizing immediate survival. Side A answered directly with force-majeure flexibility, but its tougher-future-terms concession left B's central pressure substantially alive.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO won the central trade-off. CON stressed acute liquidity and creditor-coordination delay, but PRO answered the decisive pressure: automatic rules tied to NAPs and published pipelines can speed up, not slow, restructuring, and force-majeure pauses with make-up investment avoids both rubber-stamp and snapback default. CON's replies to B's pressure questions (grant-based finance elsewhere, any earmark starves survival) left the repeat-default problem unanswered, while PRO's durable-solvency framing showed why earmarked resilience preserves, not crowds out, medicine and payroll.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 - resolving CON's revocation-vs-rubber-stamp dilemma with force-majeure pauses and corrective investment, and reframing automatic standardized covenants as reducing ad hoc creditor bargaining delay.
