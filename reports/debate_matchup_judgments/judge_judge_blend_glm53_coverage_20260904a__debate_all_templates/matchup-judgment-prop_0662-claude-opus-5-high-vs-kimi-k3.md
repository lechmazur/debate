### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. It showed why annual tax forms are not substitutes for standardized, contemporaneous eligibility records, identified clear claimants even if marginal cases remain difficult, and persuasively argued that reporting need not settle employment classification. Side A exposed genuine problems involving self-scheduled hours, cost incidence, and platform-controlled data, but leaned too heavily on a speculative safe-harbor bargain and understated B's reduced-demand cases. Side A was also clipped twice. B ultimately offered the cleaner motion-specific comparison: imperfect adjudication with coverage is preferable to excluding platform work categorically.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange combined the annual-1099 distinction with the partial-unemployment analogy: records need not prove every loss automatically, because agencies can use earnings histories plus ordinary availability tests and still cover clear cases. Side A's self-scheduling challenge remained meaningful, but did not justify excluding all platform earnings from eligibility.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): CON won by narrowing the live dispute to what the premium actually buys and whether platform records can prove involuntary loss. PRO’s opening two-driver frame and “records close the gap” mechanism were strong, but CON repeatedly forced the availability/why problem, showed experience-rating incentives to starve work quietly, and converted PRO’s own limited claimable set (deactivation/market exit) into a many-pay/few-collect levy. PRO’s floor-now versus decade-of-litigation weighing retained force, yet never fully neutralized baseline and bureaucracy objections or the capture risk; CON’s closing crystallized the cleaner decision rule. Clipped A turns were an execution cost but not decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s rebuttal/closing chain turning PRO’s claimed beneficiaries (deactivation/app exit) into a narrow insured class funded by reduced pay for everyone else, plus the van-driver image failing as a UI case.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by decisively dismantling PRO's core mechanism and analogies. CON proved that standardized earnings records do not solve the fundamental UI adjudication problem (distinguishing voluntary scheduling choices from involuntary demand drops for self-scheduling workers) and exposed the structural flaw in PRO's risk pool, which would exclude the most common form of gig income loss. CON's closing was exceptional, sharply rebutting PRO's opening van analogy by distinguishing between injury and unemployment, and crystallizing the political reality that platforms will use this mandate to harden independent-contractor status. While both sides had minor clipping execution costs, CON's substantive command of UI mechanics was far superior.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's dismantling of PRO's "deactivation is a layoff with a server log" claim. CON pointed out that if platforms pay experience-rated premiums, they have a direct financial incentive to quietly starve workers of demand via the algorithm rather than formally deactivate them, rendering the platform's own logs useless for verifying involuntary job loss.
