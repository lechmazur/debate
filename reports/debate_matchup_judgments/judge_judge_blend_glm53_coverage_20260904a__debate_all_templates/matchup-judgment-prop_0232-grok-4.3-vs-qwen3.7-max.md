### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side B (CON) built the stronger cumulative case. Its opening successfully established that Latin American cartels have already diversified far beyond cannabis, immediately pressuring PRO's causal mechanism. The decisive exchange came when CON labeled PRO's [A2.4] admission — that violence would shift to legal supply chains — as a concession rather than a win, and then anchored the state-capacity failure argument to the concrete, uncontested example of cartel extortion of legal avocado and lime farmers in Mexico. PRO never gave a satisfying answer to why cannabis regulation would succeed where protection of existing legal agriculture has failed. PRO's best counterpoint — that legal operators avoid the overhead of armed protection and bribery, potentially matching illegal prices — was plausible but underdeveloped; CON effectively responded that cartels using forced labor and stolen land undercut that cost arithmetic entirely. On the "enforceable choke points" argument, PRO's vision of license revocation and asset seizure was credible in the abstract but fell apart against CON's realistic picture of assassinated license holders, installed fronts, and compromised regulators. PRO's core opacity-versus-exposure frame had genuine merit and CON never fully dismantled it, but it was insufficient to overcome the unanswered institutional capacity problem. Both sides were epistemically disciplined — no fake statistics or invented sourcing — but CON's rhetoric was sharper ("state-subsidized laundromat," "violent monopolies," "sanitized vision") and its examples were more grounded and memorable throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's exploitation of PRO's [A2.4] "violence shifts to legal supply chains" point was the most decisive exchange. PRO framed it as an improvement because the state gains documented targets; CON reframed it as concentrating fixed, high-value victims for cartel extortion, exactly as already happens with legal avocado farmers. PRO's responses in Rebuttal 2 (license revocation, asset seizure) never credibly addressed why cartels would use courts rather than assassins, and CON's follow-up closing landed the point cleanly.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A won by making the key comparative issue state capacity and cartel adaptation. Side B had a coherent mechanism that legalization reduces opacity and removes a low-risk cannabis revenue stream, but it never convincingly answered A’s strongest objection: in weak and corrupt enforcement environments, cartels can extort, infiltrate, or attack legal businesses rather than simply lose the market. A also undermined the premise that cannabis is central enough to weaken diversified organizations and pressed the black-market/price problem more effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s response that “regulated visibility” can become a cartel target list was the decisive exchange. B kept asserting licenses, banking trails, and asset seizure as enforceable choke points, but A more persuasively argued that compromised regulators, violence against owners, and extortion of legal producers defeat ordinary commercial enforcement.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): Side B won by decisively winning the clash over state capacity. Side A's mechanism relied heavily on the state using commercial law, banking trails, and license revocations to control the market. Side B successfully dismantled this by pointing out that commercial law is useless against armed cartels in weak states, using the concrete example of the avocado industry to prove that market visibility merely creates a map of fixed targets for extortion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's argument that "revoking a license is useless when a cartel assassinates the legal owner" and that "commercial law" fails against sicarios expertly neutralized Side A's core enforcement mechanism.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A built a progressively stronger comparative case across the debate, culminating in the "visibility versus invisibility" framing in Rebuttal 2 that B never squarely neutralized. The most decisive moment was A's A1 answer to B's sharp pressure question about revenue data: rather than retreat, A smartly reframed cannabis not as the largest revenue source but as the daily operational payroll—bribes, lookouts, foot soldiers—that sustains territorial control. This is a plausible, logically coherent reframe that B's Rebuttal 2 only partially answered by noting extortion already fills similar roles. A's core comparative claim survived: even imperfect, corruptible regulation forces cartel activity into traceable channels, while prohibition guarantees an absolute black box. B raised legitimate and well-grounded challenges about institutional weakness, cartel adaptability, and centralized choke points becoming extortion targets. These concerns were real and not fully dissolved. However, B's responses in Rebuttal 2 and the closing were noticeably shorter, somewhat repetitive of earlier rounds, and never landed a clean neutralizing argument against A's comparative claim that a flawed legal market outperforms guaranteed prohibition-era opacity. B's strongest pressure question (Q1 on revenue data) was answered effectively; B's closing largely recycled rather than crystallized the live clash. A's closing, while not introducing new material, successfully distilled the key comparison and tied it to the motion's actual decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, particularly the reframe of cannabis as "daily operational payroll" rather than the largest single revenue source, directly answered B's strongest pressure question and shifted the comparison to "visibility vs. invisibility." This framing—even corrupt regulation beats an untraceable shadow economy—was the cleanest decision rule in the debate and B never fully countered it.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side B won by making the motion’s burden clearer: legalization need not destroy cartels, only degrade them by moving a major market out of the shadows and forcing syndicates into higher-friction crimes. Side A effectively pressed cartel diversification, weak institutions, and regulatory capture, but often asserted that adaptation would fully offset lost cannabis revenue without proving why partial market capture would not still weaken organized crime. B’s “visibility versus invisibility” frame and “perfection is not the standard” weighing made the comparative choice cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response to the corruption and adaptation arguments was the most decisive: it reframed weak regulation as still preferable to prohibition’s total black box, and argued that even cartel pivots to extortion or harder drugs would be more visible and vulnerable than cannabis-funded territorial control.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by successfully distinguishing the economic function of cannabis (daily payroll, localized grip) from harder drugs (episodic transit). This proved that even if cartels pivot, losing cannabis degrades their territorial control and forces them into high-friction activities. Side B effectively established cartel diversification, but could not overcome Side A's comparative weighing that a forced pivot to vulnerable crimes is better than the status quo.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1 in Rebuttal 2, which neutralized Side B's pressure regarding cartel revenue diversification by framing cannabis as the steady grassroots payroll versus episodic cocaine transit.
