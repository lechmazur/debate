### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) built a stronger cumulative case across all rounds. Its opening deployed concrete examples (1,000-hour braiding requirements covering unrelated chemical-processing content) and a coherent structural account of rent-seeking, then maintained the critical distinction between genuine catastrophe-risk licensing for electricians and plumbers versus the low-risk trades at issue. Most decisively, B repeatedly invoked the empirical record from states that reduced or eliminated these requirements, asserting no measurable rise in infections, fires, or complaints. Side A (CON) never squarely rebutted this empirical claim. Instead, CON retreated to worst-case scenarios (toxic materials in commercial spaces, bloodborne pathogens) and ultimately to the "preparedness paradox"—the circular argument that licensing must be working because disasters haven't happened. That move is unfalsifiable and leaves B's central comparative intact. CON's strongest moments were the information-asymmetry argument and the point that licensing creates a registry enabling proactive inspections, but B answered both with targeted-regulation alternatives (health-department facility checks, as used for restaurants and tattoo shops) without conceding the full licensing apparatus. CON also overstated interior design risks—claiming "catastrophic, fatal consequences" from a designer's material choices—while never grappling with B's response that architects, engineers, and building inspectors already sit downstream and that designer licensing simply duplicates review. The pressure-question round exposed CON most sharply: B asked for measurable data on harm increases in deregulated states, and CON answered with "systemic failure" reasoning rather than any real-world counter-evidence. B's closing was brief but crystallized the live comparison cleanly, while CON's closing largely recycled the preparedness-paradox framing without adding traction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 framing that CON's case rests on "worst-case possibilities rather than measured frequencies" was the most decisive exchange. It correctly identified the core epistemic weakness of CON's entire approach and introduced the empirical anchor—states that eased requirements showing no corresponding harm spikes—that CON never successfully neutralized for the remainder of the debate. This framing made every subsequent CON appeal to catastrophic scenarios feel like a concession that the actual record does not support them.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side B won by making the central comparison clearer: the risks in hair braiding and interior design are real but limited, while broad licensing requirements are mismatched, costly, and exclusionary. Side A had a coherent prevention-based safety case, but leaned heavily on catastrophic hypotheticals and did not sufficiently answer B's repeated point that lighter regimes can use targeted sanitation, inspections, and existing code review without full occupational licensing. B better addressed the proposition's “primarily barriers to entry” framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was answering A's pressure questions by distinguishing targeted health-department/code enforcement from full licensing, then weighing that proportional oversight handles the actual risks without excluding low-income entrants.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): PRO (Side A) wins by maintaining a clear empirical and comparative edge throughout the exchange. A establishes that states which eased or eliminated licensing for hair braiding and interior design saw no detectable increase in infections, fires, or consumer complaints, directly supporting the claim that these rules function primarily as barriers to entry. CON responds with plausible theoretical risks and a preparedness-paradox frame, but never neutralizes the comparative evidence or rebuts the rent-seeking mechanism (incumbents lobbying for and staffing boards). A further turns CON&#39;s equity argument by showing that licensing raises prices and excludes low-cost, family-taught practitioners, harming the very low-income consumers CON claims to protect. The distinction between high-risk trades where strict licensing is justified and these low-risk fields remains unbroken, giving A the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO&#39;s Rebuttal 2 (A4), where A answered CON&#39;s pressure questions by showing that building inspectors already serve as the final checkpoint for commercial safety and health departments can enforce sanitation standards through facility inspections—making individual occupational licensing redundant. This exchange crystallized the central clash by demonstrating that the same safety outcomes can be achieved without the costly, exclusionary apparatus of full licensure.
