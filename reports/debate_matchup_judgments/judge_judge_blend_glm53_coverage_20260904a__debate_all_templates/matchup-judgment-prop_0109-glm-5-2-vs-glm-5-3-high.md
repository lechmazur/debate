A also executed every round cleanly within limits and answered pressure questions crisply, while B was clipped twice, including in the decisive final speech, which is a real execution cost per the judging rules. On balance A's case was slightly better resolved and more cleanly delivered by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's [B2.1] "fatal concession" rebuttal was the single sharpest moment, but A's closing use of that same concession against B's rental-shortage claim, combined with B's clipped final response, tipped the decisive exchange toward A.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's causal claims about prices and rents under conditions of scarcity. Side A cleanly answered Side B's pressure regarding rental demand, proving that in tight markets, shuffling winners doesn't reduce rental demand but does reduce rental supply. Side A's closing brilliantly resolved Side B's contradiction trap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing brilliantly neutralized Side B's double-bind by explaining that 50 homes are too few to move macro price levels in a neighborhood, but exactly enough to remove 50 physical options for renters.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but PRO's mechanism survived scrutiny slightly better. PRO's key move—distinguishing institutional purchases (which permanently convert owner-occupied stock into rental stock) from individual cash buyers like retirees (who remain owner-occupiers, keeping the home in the ownership pool)—directly answered CON's "other cash buyers remain" rebuttal, and CON never squarely engaged this stock-level distinction, instead repeating "identical crowding" at the transaction level. PRO also successfully neutralized CON's "distraction from upzoning" argument by reframing restriction and supply-side reform as complements rather than rivals, a point CON only answered with a speculative, admittedly unfalsifiable political-economy claim (conceded implicitly in CON's A1 answer, which retreated to "scarce political bandwidth" rather than defending the causal claim). CON's strongest surviving threads—enforcement/evasion via LLC restructuring and the "wealth-distribution dressed as housing policy" framing—were real pressure points, but PRO's civic-externality and ownership-equity responses were adequate, and CON never drove the evasion point home with specificity after raising it. The pressure-question exchange was well-executed on both sides (clean A1/A2 formatting), with CON's answer to the "mom-and-pop landlord" hypothetical being a clever save, but PRO's closing crystallized the decisive comparison more effectively than CON's closing, which mostly restated earlier claims without resolving the stock-conversion rebuttal.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's ownership-stock distinction in [A4.3]/[A4.4] (institutional purchases permanently convert homes to rental stock at scale, unlike an individual buyer who remains an owner-occupier) was the most decisive exchange, because CON's response in [B4.5]/[B5.2] ("identical crowding effect") never actually engaged the systemic stock-level claim, leaving PRO's distinction functionally unrebutted.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully framing the debate around the qualitative differences between institutional renting and owner-occupancy (equity, stability) and by neutralizing Side A's 'supply' argument as a false choice. Side A's best push—that the policy merely displaces renters—was decisively outweighed by Side B's defense of the civic and economic externalities of homeownership.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 response (A1) directly addressing Side A's claim that preferring buyers over renters is an arbitrary wealth preference, effectively grounding the policy in historical precedent and civic externalities.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Side A wins because it successfully defended the motion as a targeted guardrail rather than a cure-all, establishing that institutional buyers operating at scale systematically crowd out local owner-occupiers at the entry-level margin. It distinguished this from individual cash purchases, showed ownership transfers carry civic and wealth-building value that rental concentration does not, and neutralized B's supply critique with the "complements, not rivals" frame. B's proxy objection and political-economy argument were sharp but remained speculative or undermined by A's scale mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 distinction between systematic volume purchases by funds and an individual cash buyer acquiring a residence, directly answering B's pressure that other cash buyers would remain after a ban.
