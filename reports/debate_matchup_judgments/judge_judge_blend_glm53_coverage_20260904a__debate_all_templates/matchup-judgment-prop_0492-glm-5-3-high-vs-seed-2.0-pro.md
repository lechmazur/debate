  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it answered the timing dilemma through a pooled fund that pays support immediately while collecting from realized gains later. This preserved the causal funding link without requiring distressed workers to wait, while Side A's response mainly repeated that taxation necessarily eliminates the gains.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won because it successfully defused CON's strongest timing objection by showing the tax could track realized gains while front-loading support through standard public-finance bridging, preserving the causal link without crushing upfront investment. PRO also exposed a persistent gap in CON's alternative: CON never justified why funding adjustment through general revenue—making all taxpayers, including displaced workers, foot the bill—was fairer than taxing the automating firms causing the displacement. CON's avoidance and productivity arguments were blunted by PRO's headcount-per-output metric and political-license framing, leaving PRO's core route to victory intact after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal (A4), where it directly answered CON's pressure questions by explaining how a fund could disburse retraining immediately while collecting against realized automation gains later, and reframed CON's "double standard" attack by noting the online retailer itself relies on massive automation—sharpening the principle of targeting identifiable, capital-intensive displacement.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO survived CON's strongest timing attack by reframing collection vs disbursement through an adjustment fund, preserving the motion's linkage, while CON's double-standard and avoidance arguments were partially blunted by trade-adjustment precedent and headcount-per-output design. ### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by showing that the tax requires contestable causal judgments, can redirect automation investment abroad, and relies on an unstable revenue base. Side B never established its fifty-cent estimate and incorrectly assumed that capping the tax at estimated public costs guarantees local automation remains cheaper than relocation. A also supplied a clearer alternative: progressive general-revenue funding that supports displaced workers regardless of why their jobs disappeared. A incurred some execution cost from two clipped turns, but its decisive analysis remained visible.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal decisively exposed that Side B’s offshoring answer depended on a nonexistent link between estimated public costs and the actual cost gap between domestic and foreign investment. It then connected that flaw to Side B’s admitted reliance on a rough fifty-cent estimate, undermining both the tax rate and the promised retraining revenue.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing structural flaws Side A never answered: a displacement-financed fund shrinks if it deters automation or if firms move offshore, and defining "replacement" requires unworkable counterfactual audits unlike event-based severance. B’s general-revenue alternative addressed the same need without these defects. A’s externality framing was strong but collapsed on implementation and sustainability under pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s identification of the self-undermining revenue base: taxing domestic displacement to fund retraining creates a fund that disappears precisely when it succeeds or when investment relocates, a flaw A ignored across multiple rounds.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): CON won the operational clash. PRO kept a moral externality frame but never answered that severance taxes an observable role loss while this tax taxes motive/causation, requiring counterfactual audits, and that its revenue base evaporates if firms automate abroad or are deterred locally. PRO's concession that the 50-cent figure was a rough estimate undercut its own safety claim on offshoring. CON's general-revenue alternative captured gains more reliably and covers all job-loss causes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1/B2.6 distinction that severance is observable but automation tax is causation, plus fiscal self-defeat: fund financed by displacement that shrinks when tax succeeds or investment moves, leaving workers with nothing.
