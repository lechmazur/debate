### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO wins on three cumulative advantages. First, the pivotal admission: in B4.2, CON explicitly states that "the off-peak scheduling restriction is a deliberate choice to reserve peak capacity for highest-margin PLF showtimes." This directly concedes the proposition's harm claim — that ordinary moviegoers are intentionally left with inconvenient standard options — and PRO correctly identified and weaponized it in the closing. CON's closing never grappled with this self-inflicted wound. Second, PRO's pressure questions were sharper. Q1 exposed a real logical contradiction in CON's demand argument (simultaneous claim of sold-out standard slots and empty-seat risk from adding more), and while CON's A1 attempted a segmentation answer, it was incomplete and strained. PRO's A1 and A2 in Rebuttal 2 were more responsive and began with correct A1:/A2: labels; CON also received a format clipping penalty. Third, CON's core economic argument — that PLF's 4-5x margins prevent a 30%+ standard price spike — was stated with confident precision lacking grounding in a closed-book setting, and PRO's market elasticity counter (theaters already charge what the market bears; a 30% spike drives audiences to streaming, destroying concession revenue) was a plausible and un-refuted mechanism. CON's subsidy story requires theaters to act charitably toward budget viewers, which CON's own B4.2 admission directly contradicts. PRO did not prove every sub-claim but consistently held the more defensible ground through the exchange, and the opponent's key concession was decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's exploitation of CON's B4.2 admission was the decisive exchange. CON, in attempting to answer PRO's Q2 about why PLF profits don't appear as consumer savings, admitted that off-peak scheduling is "a deliberate choice to reserve peak capacity for highest-margin PLF showtimes" — not a market response to demand but a profit-maximizing restriction of standard inventory. PRO correctly seized on this in the closing as a direct confirmation of the proposition's harm claim, and CON's closing offered no response to it.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B built the stronger case by directly exposing CON's zero-sum contradiction and artificial scarcity admissions in rebuttals and closings; Side A's clipped rebuttal 2 and weaker answers to B's pressure questions on demand mechanics left key burdens unanswered, while B's responses maintained consistent focus on the proposition's scheduling condition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 2 and closing on CON's admission of deliberate prime-time restrictions to favor PLF margins.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO established that multiplex scheduling is zero-sum and that theaters deliberately restrict standard prime-time slots to maximize PLF margins—an admission CON effectively made in Rebuttal 2. While CON offered a coherent economic defense about cross-subsidization, PRO successfully framed it as unproven trickle-down theory that does not help moviegoers who cannot access convenient affordable screenings. PRO's pressure questions exposed a damaging contradiction in CON's demand story, and PRO's Closing crystallized the clash by showing that CON's own description of deliberate restriction proves the proposition's harm. CON's reliance on unsupported precise statistics and a clipped final rebuttal further undermined its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question Q1 and subsequent rebuttals exposing the contradiction in CON's demand narrative—claiming standard prime slots simultaneously sell out yet would produce empty seats if expanded—which PRO leveraged to prove artificial scarcity and validate the proposition's core harm.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins, though not cleanly. The proposition is normative—about what scheduling "should" do—and Side A stays closer to that frame throughout: the harm is concrete (price exclusion plus reduced seat availability), the fix is proportionate (standard options alongside PLF, not elimination of PLF), and the long-term consequence (casual-viewer exodus permanently shifting to streaming) is plausible and arguably more serious than a short-term margin squeeze. Side B's economic survival argument is real and consistent, but it never fully reckons with Side A's strongest counter: that the 60% casual-viewer base is what sustains theaters year-round, so pricing them out is a slower but surer path to bankruptcy than capping PLF at peak. Side B's best move was exposing Side A's flawed 2-vs-4-showings math, and correctly calling out the invented "inter-show calibration" defense as fiction—this is a real credibility hit. However, the underlying seat-count argument (120-seat PLF vs. 200-seat standard = fewer affordable tickets even at equal daily runs) survives that challenge and was stated clearly enough in A4.1. Side B's "wait for week two or a matinee" solution is repeatedly deployed but never adequately answers Side A's point about rigid work and childcare schedules, or the time-sensitivity of blockbuster cultural participation. Side B's "sole first-run access point" distinction was a reasonable answer to the yield-management analogy. On balance, Side A built the more coherent path to the proposition as written; Side B's economic argument, while strong, answers a slightly different question than the one on the table.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was Side B's pressure question challenging the 4-vs-2-showings math, followed by Side A's "calibration time" defense that Side B correctly identified as invented. This exchange genuinely damaged Side A's credibility, but Side A partially recovered in A4.1 by restating the seat-count disparity (120 PLF seats vs. 200 standard) as the real mechanism—which holds even without the calibration fiction. Side B won the narrow math skirmish but failed to fully close out the underlying access argument it exposed.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A built the stronger case by showing PLF allocation reflects actual peak demand and occupancy patterns that sustain theaters against streaming; Side B never neutralized the survival argument or the point that price-sensitive viewers accept staggered access, making economic viability the decisive comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A rebuts B's rigged four-vs-two scheduling claim and rejects the public-utility framing in Rebuttal 2, directly answering pressure questions while crystallizing yield-management stakes.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins because it successfully narrowed the debate to a specific, defensible claim: not a ban on PLF, but opposition to scheduling dominance that eliminates standard prime-time options entirely. Side B repeatedly straw-manned this as throttling premium formats and never squarely justified why theaters must allocate the vast majority of prime opening-weekend slots to PLF rather than maintaining a balance. A’s rebuttal of B’s analogies was decisive—movie theaters are uniquely time-sensitive first-run cultural access points, making wait-until-week-two or airline-yield-management comparisons inapt. B’s “survival” claim remained largely asserted rather than demonstrated with concrete mechanics, and A effectively exposed that chasing short-term PLF margins at the cost of excluding mass casual audiences undermines the long-term audience base that sustains theaters. The pressure question exchange favored A, as B never provided a concrete basis for its math challenge or survival assertion, whereas A recovered cleanly from the 2-vs-4 showing challenge by shifting to a 4-vs-4 seat-capacity comparison that preserved its core harm. B’s closing recapitulated earlier points without resolving these gaps, while A crystallized the clash as a long-term viability versus short-term margin trade-off that the proposition correctly resolves.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal answers to Side B’s pressure questions, where A clarified that even equating daily showings (4 vs 4) still leaves 480 PLF seats against 800 standard seats, locking out hundreds of budget viewers, and where A distinguished theaters from airlines/hotels/books by emphasizing the unique, time-sensitive, spoiler-vulnerable nature of first-run blockbuster access—effectively neutralizing B’s core analogies and reframing the debate around actual exclusion rather than mere inconvenience.
