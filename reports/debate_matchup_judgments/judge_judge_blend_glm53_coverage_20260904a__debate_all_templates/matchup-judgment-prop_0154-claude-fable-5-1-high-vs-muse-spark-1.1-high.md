### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the decisive clash on whether copyright should monetize being learned from and out-competed, then showing PRO’s scheme fails its own freelancer and competition goals. After A framed harm as replacement via learning, B locked that as monetizing an unprotected interest (Christie/novelist parallel), attacked the size/commercial “server copy” distinction as a tax on capability, and showed exemptions plus pool/floor/quality-tier design route money to catalogues, administrators, and frontier indemnity buyers rather than freelancers. A kept historical compulsory-license and intermediate-copying pressure live and answered Qs more cleanly in format, but never neutralized the principle or arrival-of-payment weighing. B’s clipped R2 was a real cost, yet the sustained comparison still made voting CON easier.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s capture of A’s “learned from you / upstream value” line and follow-through that copyright never paid for being out-competed by learning, paired with the pool/floor arithmetic dismantling freelancers’ real recovery.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON successfully undermined PRO's compulsory licensing scheme by demonstrating that the proposed payment mechanism was arithmetically incoherent—any minimum floor per work multiplied across billions of training tokens would exceed a plausible revenue pool, forcing the scheme to exclude most creators or default to big catalogues and administrators. CON also maintained that training involves non-expressive use akin to Google Books, so the license would invent a right where none existed and create a regulatory moat rather than lower barriers. PRO never repaired the math trap or fully justified why scale alone should convert lawful machine reading into infringement.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's arithmetic dismantling of PRO's payment formula (B4.5/B5.2), showing that a "minimum floor per matched work" spread across billions of works is fiscally impossible and would route money to administrators and major catalogues rather than the freelancers PRO invoked to justify the scheme.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling the practical mechanics of Side B's compulsory licensing scheme. While Side B relied on the appealing slogan that "fractions that arrive beat principled zero," Side A proved in the later rounds that the fractions would not actually arrive at the freelancer level due to the mathematical impossibility of Side B's proposed distribution formula (a minimum floor across billions of works exceeding the total pool) and the structural bias toward large catalogues. Side A also effectively flipped the "moat" argument, showing that a statutory licensing gate actually entrenches frontier labs who can afford indemnity, whereas a robust fair-use standard for non-expressive server copies leaves the gate open for startups.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of Side B's payment calculation in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, demonstrating that a "minimum floor per matched work" multiplied by billions of registered works would mathematically dwarf the entire revenue pool, exposing the scheme as economically unworkable for the very freelancers it was meant to protect.
