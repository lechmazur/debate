### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): PRO carried two live routes to victory that CON never fully neutralized. First, the status quo critique: hundreds of millions already face opaque, unaccountable exclusion administered by the same local officials CON trusts with paper credentials, so the comparative question is not "surveillance vs. no surveillance" but "transparent, contestable surveillance vs. invisible, unaccountable surveillance." CON's closing claim that "slower, patchier access" is safer never grapples with how lethal and abusive the present opacity already is. Second, PRO's A2.4 contradiction — that portable paper credentials still route through the very local discretion CON condemns — was never cleanly resolved; CON's best answer was that local exclusion is at least spatially contained, which is true but does not rehabilitate paper as a genuine alternative. CON's strongest counter was the enforcement gap: the AU has never sanctioned a member for treaty breaches, and that historical point (B4.2) materially damaged PRO's ZKP/split-key architecture. PRO's answer — AfCFTA conditionality and cryptographic alerts — was creative but speculative, and CON correctly noted that non-compliant states simply opt out. That enforcement hole is real and leaves PRO's safeguards partially hanging. Still, CON's "off-switch" argument, while rhetorically effective, overstates the unidirectional risk: a dispersed paper system can also be weaponized by the same local authorities, just more slowly and with less visibility. On balance PRO built a cleaner comparative case — the motion's own language ("even if it increases surveillance risks") already concedes some risk and asks whether service gains justify it, and PRO's account of those gains against a bleak status quo is more fully developed and less contradicted than CON's alternatives. Both sides formatted Q/A rounds correctly with no execution penalties.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange was PRO's A2.4 identification that paper/sector credentials still rely on the same discretionary local officials CON treats as safer. CON's reply in B4.1 — that local exclusion is at least contestable by neighbors — partially salvaged the alternative but never explained why neighbor pressure succeeds where digital accountability supposedly fails, leaving a durable contradiction at the heart of CON's alternative-to-the-motion case.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A wins by effectively grounding the debate in the political realities of the African Union, successfully arguing that technical safeguards (like PRO's cryptographic locks) mean nothing without a credible enforcement mechanism. Side B committed a major strategic error in the Pressure Questions by accidentally asking A to explain why AU enforcement should be trusted—a burden that belonged to B. Side A capitalized on this beautifully, using B's own question to prove that the AU lacks the political will to prevent a unified digital ID from becoming a universal "off-switch" for service denial.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.2) decisively capitalized on B's confused pressure question, pointing out that B was asking A to solve B's own burden regarding AU enforcement, and using it to cement A's core argument.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A established concrete service-delivery gains and a credible technical safeguard architecture—split-key custody, zero-knowledge proofs, and AfCFTA-linked conditionalities—that directly answered Side B's core worry about unenforceable oversight. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): The decisive clash was whether the service-delivery gains are unique to a continent-wide credential or already achievable through lighter mechanisms. CON won that clash more concretely: naming M-Pesa's cross-border reach, bilateral health-record sharing, and country-level agricultural permit negotiation in direct answer to PRO's pressure question. PRO's closing assertion that "bilateral deals have left gaps intact for decades" is plausible but was not backed by specifics that could rebut CON's named examples. On the surveillance side, CON's single-point-of-failure logic was sound, and the Kenya, Ethiopia, and Uganda examples gave it operational weight that PRO's design-choice responses (federated storage, breach notification) never fully neutralised. PRO's strongest moment was the conditional-financing mechanism answering B's Q1 — genuinely creative and structurally responsive — but CON's counter that AU history shows it cannot actually deny financing was reasonable and PRO did not rebut it in the closing. CON's "separation is the protection" framing was clean and memorable, and the "paper shield" characterisation of PRO's enforcement answer landed effectively. PRO's argument quality was real but remained more abstract throughout; CON progressively sharpened the key comparison — irreversible structural risk versus services already delivered by lighter tools — and never lost hold of it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 [B4.1–B4.2] directly answering PRO's pressure question by naming M-Pesa cross-border reach and bilateral health agreements was the most damaging exchange. It concretely undermined PRO's core claim that a unified credential is the only path to cross-border services, and PRO's closing never supplied specifics to recover from it.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): Side A won by cleanly neutralizing Side B's two main mechanisms: that the AU could enforce privacy through 'conditional financing' and that cross-border services uniquely require a continent-wide ID. Side A's concrete examples of existing bilateral agreements and the structural logic of a 'single point of failure' effectively dismantled Side B's case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal directly answered both of Side B's pressure questions with concrete examples of cross-border services (M-Pesa, East African health agreements) and a devastating logical counter that 'separation is the protection,' fundamentally breaking Side B's uniqueness and privacy claims.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side B won by successfully undermining the uniqueness of PRO's service-delivery claims and neutralizing the conditional-access enforcement mechanism. B demonstrated that cross-border services already function through bilateral agreements and mobile-money platforms, while persuasively arguing that a continent-wide ID creates an irreversible, structural surveillance risk that weak AU governance cannot contain. A's case was plausible but could not recover from B's sharp weighing that fragmentation limits harm while centralization creates a catastrophic single point of failure.
