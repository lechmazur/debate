### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Both sides ran coherent, well-structured cases, but CON's central mechanism—that a moratorium removes only the transparent, cooperative actors while leaving military and private programs untouched—was never fully neutralized by PRO. CON's pressure question on the Outer Space Treaty timeline exposed a real weakness in PRO's core historical analogy (treaties following demonstrated capability, not preceding it), and PRO's answer (A4.1) largely evaded the chronological challenge rather than resolving it. CON also successfully reframed PRO's "fatal concession" (that no trial can disprove causing distant harm) as cutting the other way: evidence-based suspicion beats no-evidence suspicion. PRO's closing reasserted "fatal concessions" without engaging CON's reframing, while CON's closing ("the choice is open vs. secret, not moratorium vs. no testing") gave a cleaner, more decisive frame for resolving the clash. CON's use of a specific-but-inaccurate historical date ("three years after Sputnik") is a real grounding flaw and was discounted accordingly, but it did not collapse CON's underlying point, which survived the exchange. On balance, CON did more work engaging and dismantling PRO's live arguments, while PRO's rebuttals tended to restate rather than resolve the key tensions CON raised.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1 attack on the Outer Space Treaty analogy (treaties follow demonstrated capability, not precede it) combined with PRO's evasive A4.1 answer to the matching pressure question was the most decisive exchange, since it undercut PRO's central historical justification for "governance before testing" without a real rejoinder.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won a competitive debate by showing that imperfect prohibition can still create legal clarity, sanctions, and diplomatic leverage while reducing normalization and capability-building. Side B persuasively defended empirical research and transparency, but repeatedly relied on the false choice that testing must be either openly permitted or wholly secret, without adequately answering deterrence, legal cover, or technological lock-in. Its incorrectly precise Outer Space Treaty timeline also weakened its grounding.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s strongest response was that prohibition need not eliminate every clandestine actor to matter: it can strip away academic camouflage, delegitimize violations, and establish grounds for enforcement. This directly undercut Side B’s recurring claim that any surviving secret testing makes a moratorium useless.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A built a much stronger case by effectively weaponizing Side B's concession on the attribution problem. Side A demonstrated that because field trials cannot definitively prove they did not cause a distant climate disaster, unregulated testing would inevitably lead to geopolitical escalation and blame, neutralizing Side B's claim that transparency builds trust. Side A also provided stronger analogies, such as the Chemical Weapons Convention, to counter the "drives it underground" argument, while Side B's closing relied on repetitive rhetoric rather than resolving the core clashes.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but A's throughline—no binding rules exist, cross-border effects create a consent problem regardless of scale, and precedent/data-control asymmetries favor first movers—survived the exchange more intact. Critically, when B pressed the catch-22 (diplomats can't set thresholds without data scientists are banned from gathering), A directly conceded the point but reframed it: minimal consent/notification/liability rules do not require dispersion physics and must precede any release, leaving room for calibrated data-gathering once that baseline exists. B never squarely answered this distinction, instead repeating the catch-22 as if unaddressed. B's strongest move—exposing A's opening scale hyperbole (small trials vs. monsoon disruption)—landed and forced A to retreat to procedural/consent grounds, but B's own answers to pressure questions were shakier: citing a vague 'customary international law no-harm rule' while simultaneously arguing the trials cause 'zero measurable transboundary impact' undercuts the claim that this rule provides any real stopping mechanism, leaving A's Q2 (what mechanism lets a nation halt an unwanted trial) effectively unanswered. A's closing crystallized the debate more cleanly ('you don't let someone fire a gun over your head, then let them write the gun laws'), while B's closing largely restated the catch-22 and urgency framing without resolving A's rebuttal to it. The result is a close, competitive debate with a clear but not dominant edge to A.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to B's catch-22 (A4.3–A4.4: minimal consent/liability rules don't require dispersion-physics precision, so the moratorium and rule-writing can proceed in parallel) was the most decisive exchange, because B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing never directly engaged this distinction and instead repeated the original catch-22 framing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side B won by exposing the proposition's failure to distinguish negligible sub-scale experiments from climate-altering deployment. Side A persuasively identified governance gaps and defended basic procedural rules, but its opening exaggerated the effects of small tests, its two-to-three-year timeline was unsupported, and its analogies repeatedly treated trace research like poisoning or gunfire. Side B supplied the stronger proportionality comparison: a treaty-conditioned blanket moratorium risks indefinite loss of information needed for both governance and future emergency decisions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's strongest exchange was its response to Side A's pesticide and fire-extinguisher analogies: it reframed small field trials as calibration rather than deployment, directly undermining Side A's catastrophic-risk premise while reinforcing the empirical-information Catch-22.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully dismantling Side B's core "Catch-22" argument. Side B argued that international governance requires empirical field data to set safety thresholds, but Side A effectively countered that procedural rules (consent, transparency, prior warning, liability) do not require physical test data to be established. Side A also handled the pressure questions much better, exposing Side B's reliance on unenforceable customary international law and domestic statutes to manage transboundary risks. Side A's rhetorical framing of the status quo as a first-mover power grab rather than prudent science was highly persuasive and decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3-A4.4), where they dismantle the Catch-22 by explaining that diplomats do not need to measure exact aerosol dispersion physics to establish basic procedural ground rules like cross-border notification and compensation.
