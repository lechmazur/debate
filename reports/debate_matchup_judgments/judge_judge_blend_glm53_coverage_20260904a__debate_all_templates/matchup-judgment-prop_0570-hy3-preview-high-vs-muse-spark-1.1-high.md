### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (A) ran a bold but coherent inversion of the motion's intuitive mapping: AI is dangerous as "main" for mastery because it can certify procedural correctness while a hidden misconception (the multiply-by-ten example) fossilizes unseen, whereas writing has no "green check" to game, so high-volume AI iteration is generative rather than corrosive, with a human able to arrive as final editor. This throughline was reinforced across every speech and reached a sharp payoff in the closing, where A directly weaponized B's own "math is objective, writing is subjective" distinction against it. PRO (B) defended the conventional allocation competently, but conceded a costly point in the pressure round ("click-chasing leads to correct procedural execution" in math) that A immediately exploited as proof that objective-answer domains are exactly where fossilized wrong-logic mastery is most dangerous. B also never squarely quantified how many real revision cycles a human-main model delivers under a 150-student load, a gap A repeatedly pressed and used to undercut B's "solvable scarcity" claim. Execution costs matter too: B's rebuttal 2 and closing were both clipped before reaching their full argument, weakening exactly the speeches meant to crystallize the case, while A was only clipped once, early, with ample room afterward to develop and land its thesis.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 turning B's own admission that "click-chasing leads to correct procedural execution" in math into evidence that objective domains are the most dangerous place for AI-driven mastery (since wrong logic can still produce a green check), combined with A's exploitation of B's evasive, non-quantified answer to the revision-cycle pressure question.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by demonstrating that the proposition's division is backwards: AI mastery practice risks fossilizing hidden misconceptions behind correct answers, while writing feedback is structurally scarce and benefits from the volume AI enables. PRO never overcame the classroom scarcity argument or the turn that objective math is where broken logic hides.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's turn in Rebuttal 2 and Closing on PRO's "math is objective" concession, showing that objective correctness is precisely where AI is most dangerous—certifying mastery while broken reasoning calcifies—while writing lacks a "green check" to game.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by executing a brilliant logical flip in the final rounds. While Side A argued that math's objective nature makes it safe for AI and writing's subjective nature requires humans, Side B convincingly countered that math's single right answer allows AI to reinforce hidden misconceptions (fossilizing errors via hint-harvesting), whereas writing's generative nature prevents "gaming" a green checkmark. Side B also dominated the practical clash, exposing Side A's reliance on "solvable scarcity" (smaller classes) as a structural fantasy, while effectively defending the necessity of high-volume AI iteration for writing practice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing argument flipping Side A's objective/subjective distinction, demonstrating that objective math answers allow AI to certify mastery for the wrong underlying logic, while subjective writing prompts force the student to supply actual truth without a "green check" to harvest.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): B (PRO) built a cleaner, more mechanism-driven case: mastery practice as AI's natural domain, writing feedback as interpretive/relational work, and a specific institutional-incentive argument (procurement logic) explaining why declaring AI the "main source" of writing feedback would let districts cut human time rather than expand it. B's pressure questions exposed a real internal tension in A's case—A claimed mechanics and voice can't be separated, yet also wanted AI as main structural grader, which B used to ask how AI would avoid penalizing risky, culturally specific voice. A's answer ("prompt engineering and hybrid models") was vague and didn't resolve the tension. A's core equity argument (silence vs. flawed feedback) was met head-on by B's "delay is recoverable, miseducation is not" framing and a synthesis (AI for mastery frees teacher time for human writing feedback) that A never convincingly rebutted with specifics. A also suffered real execution costs: both its Opening and Rebuttal 1 were clipped for exceeding word limits, cutting off developing arguments and weakening its throughput. B's rhetoric was more vivid and consistent across rounds, closing on memorable, clarifying lines that crystallized the clash, while A's closing largely reasserted earlier points without resolving B's live procurement/incentive argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question exploiting A's claim that mechanics and voice "collapse under scrutiny" (Q2), combined with B's Rebuttal 2 institutional-logic argument (A1) explaining why AI-as-mastery-tool and AI-as-main-writing-feedback have opposite incentive effects on district staffing—A's reply that this was merely "speculative" never substantively answered the procurement mechanism.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by drawing a durable distinction between the institutional logic of mastery practice and writing feedback. They showed that offloading mastery work to AI naturally frees scarce teacher hours, whereas making AI the main source of writing feedback creates an incentive for districts to treat algorithmic turnaround as sufficient coverage, effectively formalizing neglect. Side A (CON) powerfully described current feedback starvation but never squarely answered this procurement dynamic, instead relying on optimistic assertions that districts would expand human review and on thin references to "prompt engineering" that lacked grounding. PRO also turned CON's equity argument by demonstrating that algorithmic main readers standardize voice and punish culturally specific risk-taking, whereas their model preserves human interpretive mentorship while still democratizing drill-based equity. CON's execution was further hampered by clipped opening and first-rebuttal speeches.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7), where PRO answered CON's pressure questions by distinguishing how replacing mastery busywork expands human capacity while replacing core writing judgment invites cuts, and by weighing active algorithmic miseducation against mere delayed feedback.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A wins by providing a much stronger institutional analysis of how schools actually operate. Side A successfully argued that using AI for mastery practice replaces grading busywork and frees teacher time, whereas making AI the main source for writing feedback replaces core instructional coverage, incentivizing districts to increase class sizes and formalize neglect. Side A also won the crucial pressure question exchange by contrasting the harm of delay with active miseducation, while Side B offered weak dismissals of the administrative incentive problem. Side A's rhetoric was sharper, more grounded, and more memorable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, specifically the A1 and A2 answers to the pressure questions, where it dismantled Side B's assumption that AI writing feedback would not lead to administrative cuts, and effectively framed the choice as recovering from delay versus unlearning algorithmic compliance.
