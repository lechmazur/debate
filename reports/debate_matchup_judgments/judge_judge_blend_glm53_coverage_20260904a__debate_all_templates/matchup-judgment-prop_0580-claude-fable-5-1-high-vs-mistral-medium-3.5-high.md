PRO’s rebuttals were sharper, the crystallization in closing was more decisive, and the core mechanism—cash directly fixes an income shortfall with near-full delivery—survived the exchange intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s durability challenge and Quebec turnaround in Rebuttals 1 and 2: PRO showed that CON’s “structural change” argument assumed subsidies would be renewed while cash was cancelled, and that CON’s own Quebec example was universal and produced only single-digit employment gains, meaning most subsidy dollars did not change poverty status.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the motion’s per-dollar poverty-reduction test sharper than A’s delivery/admin story. From the opening, B separated ease of sending cash from targeting leakage and the earnings lever; A never cleanly resolved defending 2021 universal evidence while also claiming means-testing, nor why a pure dollar of income should beat a conditional paycheck under A’s own “per dollar now” frame. A’s exclusion and overhead points stayed live but were narrowed into minority cases and secondary costs, while B’s bucket-vs-lever weighing and pressure on whose hands receive the money decided the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Q1/Q2 on which CTC design A actually defends and whether a unlocked paycheck counts under A’s own per-dollar-now standard; A’s “mechanism” answer and conditional concession left the lever on the board.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO)): CON built the stronger comparative case by proving cash credits leak aid to non-poor households and deliver only 1:1 income support, whereas childcare subsidies leverage earnings and long-term mobility. PRO never resolved the contradiction between its universal 2021 evidence and its later means-testing defense, and arbitrarily narrowed cost-effectiveness to exclude paycheck gains. CON’s framing was sharper, its rebuttals more damaging, and its pressure questions exposed burdens PRO could not discharge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Turn 8 rebuttal (A4), where it answered PRO’s pressure questions, turned the deadweight-loss charge back on cash to middle-income families, and showed that once PRO conceded a childcare dollar “may enable a job,” the earnings multiplier put childcare ahead on any sensible per-dollar poverty-reduction metric.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON won the per-dollar comparison. PRO defined efficiency as pass-through and immediacy, but CON showed that targeting matters: universal CTC spends heavily on never-poor families while childcare subsidies land where poverty is, and its earnings lever multiplies the dollar via paychecks. PRO's answers that CTC "can be means-tested" contradicted its 2021 evidence and invoked phase-outs it had called cliffs, and its "per dollar now" concession let CON frame durability as part of cost-effectiveness. Lever vs bucket framing persisted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1 + Pressure Q1 exposed the universal-vs-means-tested contradiction and forced A4.1 to answer "the mechanism" without resolving targeting, while Q2 forced the concession that a childcare dollar "may enable a job" – collapsing PRO's claim that cash always wins per dollar.
