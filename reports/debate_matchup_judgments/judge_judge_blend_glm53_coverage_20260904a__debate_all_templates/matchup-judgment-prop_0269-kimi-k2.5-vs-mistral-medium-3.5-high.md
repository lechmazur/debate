### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): A won because it kept the proposition correctly framed as more and targeted authentication, not universal authentication. B had a strong cost and intermediary-liability case, but much of it depended on overstating the motion and on a speculative legal-ratchet claim that A answered with existing tiered authentication examples. A better showed that high-risk categories can be handled by platforms with scale and data while low-value thrift items need not be burdened.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's most decisive rebuttal was that targeted authentication in high-risk categories directly avoids B's $15-cardigan collapse scenario; B's ratchet response was rhetorically strong but under-supported against actual examples of limited authentication programs.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): Side A wins by offering a grounded, empirical case showing that targeted authentication works without collapsing the market. Side B's main defense relies on a slippery-slope "legal ratchet" argument, claiming that authenticating any items will inevitably force platforms to authenticate everything due to duty of care. Side A cleanly defeats this by pointing to existing programs like eBay's, which successfully enforce tiered policies without courts forcing them to authenticate cheap items, neutralizing Side B's primary path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal, which uses the existing eBay sneaker program to empirically disprove Side B's claim that targeted authentication inevitably creates a legal ratchet effect.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Mistral Medium 3.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.5 Thinking (CON)): PRO (A) wins by establishing that targeted, strategic authentication in high-risk categories is feasible and already successful (e.g., eBay sneakers), directly addressing the motion's "more" language while correcting incentive misalignment. CON's ratchet-to-universal claim and intermediary-as-insurer position is rebutted effectively by policy limits and existing tiered practices, leaving CON's economic collapse and legal duty arguments more speculative than grounded in the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The rebuttal 2 exchange on limiting principles and liability creep: PRO answers pressure Qs with explicit policy and non-expansion examples, blunting CON's ratchet while CON's legal-duty response remains general without countering real selective programs.
