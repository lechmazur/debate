### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued at a high level with clear structure, honest concessions, and no evidentiary overreach, but PRO (B) built the more decisive strategic arc. B explicitly owned the motion's stated cost from the opening and built three concrete mechanisms (time horizons, proposal overhead, risk-aversion) that CON never fully dismantled point-by-point. B's pivotal move—turning CON's "better cycles, not abolishing cycles" concession into "you're defending an imaginary fix agencies have failed to build for decades" —reframed the debate in B's favor and CON's answer to that pressure question (Q1 in Turn 5) was comparatively thin, gesturing at "public accountability" without explaining why the same political pressures wouldn't also block CON's proposed reform. CON's outsider-door and political-legitimacy arguments were strong and forced B to answer with institutional-level contestability (hiring, tenure, migration), which B did competently, but CON never fully exploited the tension in B's own answer—that block-grant selection reintroduces a competitive layer, partially undercutting B's stark dichotomy. Both closings crystallized cleanly, but B's "real trade vs imaginary fix" framing proved more memorable and harder for CON to fully neutralize than CON's "fix the cycles, keep the outside door" counter-frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 turn ("if the remedy were as simple as 'choose longer horizons,' the disease would already be cured") combined with its answer to A's pressure question in Rebuttal 2, which reframed CON's entire alternative as hypothetical and unbuilt despite decades of opportunity—a framing CON's subsequent answer did not fully rebut.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by effectively weaponizing Side B's concession on accountability into a vulnerability regarding political capture and austerity, and by decisively winning the clash over which system better serves outsiders. Side A's argument that block grants replace multiple external doors with a single local gatekeeper neutralized Side B's strongest mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing exposed the flaw in Side B's alternative for outsiders, pointing out that relying on institutional migration forces researchers to escape a captured system before they can even test their ideas, whereas external panels offer repeatable avenues for appeal.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that the accountability cost of block grants is not merely coasting but structural capture that closes the outside door to outsiders and early-career researchers while undermining the political legitimacy needed to defend science budgets. PROs real trade versus imaginary fix frame was powerful, but never fully solved the problem that block grants force unknown researchers to win a local gatekeepers favor before they can test an idea, whereas competitive project grants mitigate this through appealable, repeatable external contests and early-career awards. CONs crystallization that a system that must be escaped before it can be tested is not contestable gave the cleaner comparative reason to vote.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The outsider-access exchange, where CON demonstrated that block grants concentrate power in institutional gatekeepers and that PROs alternatives of hiring and migration require outsiders to already have resources or leave, whereas competitive grants allow repeated appeals to external panels and early-career pilot funds.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): CON (A) built the more decisive structural argument: every safeguard PRO proposed to prevent capture (independent boards, external chairs, protected seed funds, outside appeals) and every enforcement mechanism PRO offered (sunset clauses, budget cuts, leadership replacement) was shown to simply reconstitute contestability inside a slower, more politically insulated institutional shell. CON's "engine vs. gearbox" framing and its close reading of PRO's own answers ("PRO's enforcement list carefully avoids the word 'close'") landed as genuine, unanswered pressure — PRO's closing reasserted the epistemic case (unpredictability justifies less accountability) but never squarely refuted the charge that its remedies quietly readmit competition while losing the annual, impersonal correction that project grants already deliver. PRO's opening and epistemic framing were strong and well-grounded in plausible mechanism, and its closing correctly leaned into the motion's explicit tradeoff language, but it left CON's central "you've rebuilt what you claim to replace" critique substantially live. CON's opening being clipped by the word-limit format note is a real, if modest, execution cost, but CON's subsequent rounds more than compensated with sharper, more surgical rebuttals and a cleaner final frame for the judge to apply.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.5–A4.6), which took PRO's own governance and enforcement answers from the Pressure Questions round and reframed them as concessions that block-grant safeguards are just competition rebuilt internally, slower and with weaker exit options for challengers.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A won by successfully trapping Side B into defending mitigations for block grants that mirrored the very competitive mechanisms Side B was trying to abolish. Despite a clipped Opening, Side A dominated the later rounds with sharp, decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 was devastating. When Side B defended block grants by suggesting internal seed funds, external chairs, and budget cuts, Side A immediately pointed out that this was just rebuilding competition inside a monopoly at a slower, coarser level.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins because it exposed that PRO’s block-grant system cannot solve selection, capture, or accountability without re-importing competitive mechanisms—external review, contestable renewal, and consequences for underperformance—turning the proposal into a slower, coarser imitation of the very system it seeks to abolish. CON’s “engine versus gearbox” reframe and the demonstration that PRO spent two rounds rebuilding competition inside a monopoly made the decisive clash clear. PRO’s conceptual “tax on uncertainty” case was strong, but CON showed the pathologies were tunable features of competitive granting, not inherent flaws, and that block grants would relocate rather than remove prestige bias.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 and Closing showing that PRO’s anti-capture and accountability mechanisms—independent boards, external chairs, budget cuts, leadership replacement—are merely competitive selection rebuilt inside institutions, making the motion’s “rather than” replacement self-undermining.
