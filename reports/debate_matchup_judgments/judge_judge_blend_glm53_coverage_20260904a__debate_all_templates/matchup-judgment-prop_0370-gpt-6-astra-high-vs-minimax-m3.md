### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO's early clarification—that the motion targets "routine condition of employment," not an outright ban—reset the debate on favorable terms and exposed CON's opening as partly attacking a strawman (universal company phones vs. universal BYOD). PRO then built a consistent property/responsibility framework: employers may require the function (dispatch, messaging) but must supply the tool if a worker declines personal-device use, preserving voluntary convenience for willing workers. CON's strongest material was the logistical/cost burden of accommodating non-consenting workers at scale, and the "essential equipment" analogy (personal phone = company truck/radio). But PRO's Rebuttal 2 and Closing decisively punctured that analogy: a driver using an employer-provided device to run the same dispatch app is not refusing to do the job, only declining to supply personal property—so CON's justification for employment penalties collapses into penalizing property refusal, not work refusal. CON's closing pivoted back to cost/burden framing without directly repairing the analogy, leaving that central clash unresolved in its favor. CON's pressure question forced a bold but risky concession ("Yes, employment consequences are legitimate") that PRO successfully weaponized. Both sides argued cleanly without fake sourcing, but PRO ended with the tighter, more decisive path to the resolution as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Closing (A5.2) directly refuting CON's "company truck" analogy by noting that a driver using an employer-supplied device for the same dispatch app is not refusing the job—only declining to supply personal property—undercutting CON's justification (B4.1) for employment penalties.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A wins dominantly by maintaining a clear, consistent stance that neutralizes Side B's core offense. Side B repeatedly attacks a strawman—claiming Side A forces all workers to carry a second phone—despite Side A clarifying multiple times that voluntary personal use remains allowed. Side A also cleanly dismantles Side B's defense of employment penalties by distinguishing between refusing work and refusing to provide private property.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing turn of Side B's "company truck" analogy, which perfectly illustrated that refusing to install an app on a personal device is not a refusal to do the job.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won by establishing and defending a decisive logical distinction: refusing to supply personal property for work is not the same as refusing to do the work. Side B repeatedly misrepresented Side A's model as forcing all workers to carry company phones, ignoring Side A's explicit and repeated preservation of voluntary personal use. In the final exchange, Side A brilliantly capitalized on Side B's pressure question answer, exposing the flaw in Side B's conflation of essential job duties with the requisition of private assets. Side A's case was structurally sound, responsive, and free of the strawman arguments that undermined Side B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing [A5.1-A5.3] dismantling Side B's "company truck" analogy by pointing out that a worker using an employer-provided device is fully performing the job, merely declining to convert their personal phone into company infrastructure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): The debate turned on whether "routine" and "bounded/regulated" are mutually exclusive. A's entire case rested on collapsing B's safeguards into "not routine, therefore a concession," but never explained why a mandatory, standard-for-the-role requirement can't also be subject to enforceable limits — an equivocation B named directly ("that is a definition, not an argument"). B's pressure question forced A to defend rejecting even a reimbursed, independently audited, non-off-duty-monitoring scheduling app, and A's answer retreated into slippery-slope and "self-certification" claims without engaging the concrete external-enforcement mechanism (independent review, worker complaints, regulator remedies) B had already proposed. B, by contrast, answered A's audit-enforcement question concretely and rebutted the scope-creep worry by distinguishing initial grants from later expansions. A's vivid property/surveillance framing and concrete worker examples were rhetorically effective and never fully neutralized as background concerns, but they didn't resolve the load-bearing definitional clash. A also suffered two clipped turns (Rebuttal 1 and Closing) from exceeding word limits, an execution cost that cut off argument development at key moments. B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly and left A's "automatic veto regardless of access or compensation" characterization unanswered.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A2), where B answers A's "who enforces" pressure question with a concrete independent-review/external-enforcement/retaliation-remedy structure, directly defusing A's "self-certification" attack that A had leaned on throughout.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully defending the concept of a 'bounded but routine' requirement, effectively dismantling Side A's core strategy of defining any regulated or safe practice as a 'non-routine exception.' Side B's pressure questions forced Side A to bite a tough bullet on opposing even perfectly audited apps, which B capitalized on to show Side A's absolute veto was unjustified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4's A1 and A2 dismantling PRO's semantic argument by explaining that external regulations constrain a routine practice but do not magically make it 'non-routine.'
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully dismantling Side A's core semantic trap. Side A attempted to argue that any app installation with sufficient safeguards, audits, and accommodations is inherently a "bounded exception" rather than a "routine" condition. Side B clearly and persuasively countered that "routine" simply means a standard, compulsory requirement for a defined role, which can absolutely be tightly bounded and legally restricted. Side B also provided a more practical and technically grounded defense, noting that modern operating systems sandbox apps, and that physical separation via a second company phone does not inherently prevent off-duty messaging abuses. While Side A's rhetorical flair was strong and its warnings about surveillance were resonant, Side B's logical precision, superior handling of the pressure questions, and cleaner normative framework made it the decisive winner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it decisively refuted Side A's claim that "routine" and "bounded" are mutually exclusive, clarifying that a standard employment requirement can be both compulsory for the worker and enforceably limited for the employer without requiring individual negotiation.
