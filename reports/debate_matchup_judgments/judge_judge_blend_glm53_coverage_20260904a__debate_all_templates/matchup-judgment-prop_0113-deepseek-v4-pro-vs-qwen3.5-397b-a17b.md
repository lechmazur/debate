### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): PRO (Side B) carried the core design-incentive argument throughout: when producers bear end-of-life costs, packaging design improves; when they do not, taxpayers silently subsidise unrecyclable laminates with no environmental return. That logic was never squarely neutralised. PRO's most decisive move came in Rebuttal 1, correctly clarifying that EPR fees do not duplicate local collection infrastructure—the same trucks and routes continue; only the funding source shifts—which collapsed CON's lead bureaucracy-and-fragmentation argument before it could accumulate weight. PRO also consistently outpointed CON on regressivity: the consumption-blind property-tax system forces frugal households to subsidise prolific packaging use, while a visible fee tied to actual consumption is fairer even before exemptions are applied. CON's strongest counter—that property taxes are progressive by asset value—had real merit and kept the regressivity clash alive, but it did not resolve the hidden-subsidy problem PRO identified. CON's food-spoilage argument was repeatedly deployed but remained speculative; PRO correctly noted that safety standards cap marginal spoilage risk and that real EPR jurisdictions show packaging improvements without spoilage crises, and CON never bridged the gap from "fees exist" to "marginal spoilage outweighs packaging savings" with enough causal specificity. CON's capture/lobbying concern was legitimate but speculative and was met with the plausible counter that standard-setting remains with public authorities. PRO's execution was hurt by three clipped speeches, a meaningful format cost, but the visible substance still built the stronger cumulative case. CON's closings and rebuttals largely restated earlier points without materially narrowing the central design-incentive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 (B2.1–B2.2) identifying that EPR does not require producers to dispatch collection crews—the same municipal trucks and routes continue, only the financial burden shifts—was the most decisive single exchange. It directly and correctly collapsed CON's lead claim that shifting costs upstream fragments local infrastructure, removing that argument as a live route to victory for CON and freeing PRO's design-incentive case to stand largely unchallenged for the remainder of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO)): B won by keeping the debate centered on the proposition: producers should internalize end-of-life costs because they control packaging design. A raised real concerns about regressivity, bureaucracy, and food spoilage, but leaned too heavily on the mistaken premise that producer payment means producer operation of local waste systems. B directly corrected that with an EPR funding model and gave the cleaner incentive story. A's spoilage and checkout-cost arguments made the round competitive, but they remained more speculative than B's central unfair-subsidy and design-incentive case. B's repeated clipping and some unsupported broad claims reduce the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's first rebuttal was decisive in explaining that producer payment need not duplicate municipal trucks or local collection; it can fund existing systems while changing upstream design incentives. That blunted A's main efficiency and fragmentation attack.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (CON)): Side A wins by directly neutralizing B's pressure questions on capture and bureaucracy with public standard-setting examples, then crystallizing the incentive mismatch in closing; B's food-spoilage and regressivity risks stayed speculative while A's polluter-pays logic adapted cleanly across rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 directly answering B's questions on rule-setting and exemptions, showing public oversight and minimal new admin before tying back to ongoing taxpayer subsidy.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): Side B (PRO) builds a more complete and consistently executed case across all rounds. Its core logic—that zero financial liability for producers guarantees zero design incentive, and that "much more of the cost" does not mean producers must drive the trucks—holds up throughout. B's most durable claim, that even an imperfect fee structure internalizes costs that are currently zero, was never squarely neutralized by A. B's closing distinction between who funds and who operates the system was sharp and clarifying. B also answered both pressure questions with direct A1:/A2: labels and genuine comparative reasoning, and its own pressure questions (especially Q1 on what lever currently forces redesign) exposed a real gap in CON's case that A's answer only partially filled with regulatory bans rather than financial incentives. Side A had real ammunition—the regressivity argument, fee-schedule gaming, and the forward-vs.-reverse logistics distinction—but was clipped in three consecutive speeches (Opening, Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2), an execution problem that left arguments visibly incomplete and denied the judge full versions of A's case. A's closing, at only 142 of 220 words, also left significant burden unaddressed. B's "cities cannot redesign bottles; only producers can" framing crystallized the decisive asymmetry: regulatory bans are reactive corrections by parties who lack design control, while producer liability is proactive pressure on the only party that holds it. That comparative was live at the end of the debate and never adequately answered by CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's answer to A's Q1 (how to stop producers from gaming fee classifications): "The status quo allows producers to lobby for zero liability entirely. An imperfect fee structure still internalizes costs that are currently zero. We do not abandon regulations because enforcement is challenging." This reframe—partial gaming vs. no rules at all—was the debate's clearest comparative and A never recovered from it, reverting to repeating the gaming risk without showing why zero liability is the preferable baseline.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO)): B won by making the clearer comparative case that producer liability internalizes currently hidden waste costs and creates design incentives that municipalities cannot create after the fact. A’s regressivity argument was the strongest CON route, but B plausibly answered that taxpayers already pay, that fees can be modulated by wastefulness, and that imperfect liability is better than zero corporate responsibility. A also leaned too heavily on a logistics objection that overstated the motion as requiring producers to run brand-specific collection systems, while B clarified that funding and operating are distinct. A’s repeated clipped turns also hurt execution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s most decisive rebuttal was against A’s reverse-logistics claim: B reframed the issue as producer funding and design liability, not producers personally driving garbage trucks, which neutralized a major CON objection while preserving PRO’s leverage argument.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen3.5-397B-A17B (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by building a clearer path to victory around incentive alignment and proactive design leverage, directly countering B's pressure questions with workable mechanisms like modulated fees and oversight while reframing regressivity as hidden current costs versus visible consumption-linked ones. B's regressivity and logistics critiques had merit but were weakened by repeated truncations that left key rebuttals incomplete and a closing too narrowly focused.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's A1/A2 responses to B's PQ on lobbying/gaming and uniform hikes, showing imperfect modulated fees plus tax relief outperform zero-liability status quo and enable shopper choice via differentiated pricing.
