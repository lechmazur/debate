### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON)): PRO built a more durable structural case and answered the live pressures more cleanly. The core move—distinguishing a production company's commercial editing room from a grieving mother's voice, and from public-interest journalism—held up under repeated CON pressure. PRO's analogy to existing consent norms in medicine, advertising, and journalism was coherent and never dismantled; CON's best counter (B4.1, that right-of-publicity covers endorsements not factual storytelling) was accurate but left the broader ethical-rules arm of the proposition untouched. CON's strongest persistent line was the chilling-effect/implementation concern and the estranged-relative veto hypothetical, both of which created real pressure, but PRO's A2 response—that consent requirements apply to corporate commercial packaging, not to individual speakers—was adequate and direct. CON kept recycling the cold-cases-and-justice argument after PRO correctly noted these are not mutually exclusive, and the "who holds the microphone" framing in closing repeated rather than sharpened CON's prior points. PRO's "markets maximize engagement, not dignity" crystallization was memorable and answered the market-competition defense directly. The decisive clash favored PRO: CON offered no protective mechanism for routine exploitation except audience choice, and PRO correctly identified that as insufficient accountability for an industry monetizing real human suffering at scale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's Q2 pressure (A4.3–A4.4): the concise distinction that a production company's editing room is not a victim's mouthpiece directly exposed CON's persistent conflation of corporate commercial interests with victim agency, and CON never squarely answered it thereafter.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side A won by effectively operationalizing the practical dangers of PRO's proposed regulations. By showing that mandatory consent rules would create an 'estranged relative veto' and strip marginalized victims of the distribution networks they need to solve cases, Side A successfully turned PRO's core argument about victim protection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A2 in Rebuttal 2, which demonstrated that regulating a streaming platform effectively silences a grieving parent because the parent relies on the platform's massive distribution network to reach a national audience.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side A won by more convincingly exposing the practical veto and chilling effects of regulation on the very families PRO claims to protect, while consistently defending public-domain principles and agency without evasion. B's distinction between journalism and entertainment held conceptually but was less decisive against A's concrete burdens on distribution and marginalized voices.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Both sides ran competent, progressive debates, but CON (B) edged the final exchange on the three arguments that were still live at the close. First, CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's medical-consent analogy (A) was the sharpest single rebuttal in the debate: a living patient is a single legally identified party, whereas a deceased victim's "family" is an amorphous, legally unranked cluster whose conflicting claims turn consultation into a litigation trap. PRO introduced this analogy in the pressure round as its best counter to the chilling-effect argument and then never answered the statutory-hierarchy point in the closing, leaving it fully intact. Second, CON's ex ante/ex post distinction—administrative consent mandates before publication versus privacy-tort litigation after the fact—was a genuine and pointed response to PRO's claim that courts already handle these lines. PRO's closing ignored it. Third, CON's existing-legal-tools argument (defamation, right of publicity, privacy torts already provide recourse) was present from the opening and never directly engaged by PRO in any later round, which is a meaningful gap on the "regulation is necessary" burden. PRO's core moral case—corporate broadcasting of 911 calls without consent is genuinely harmful, financial incentives prevent self-regulation—is compelling and the consent-as-empowerment move in Rebuttal 1 is clever. But PRO's path to victory requires establishing both that harm is routine (evidenced mainly by one Dahmer example) and that proposed remedies are workable, and CON damaged both legs without a clean answer coming back. CON's closing is somewhat repetitive but crystallizes the decisive trade-off: PRO's rule leaves large studios intact while killing independent accountability journalism, making the cure worse than the ailment. That comparison is cleaner and less contested at the end of the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.1 answer to PRO's medical-consent pressure question: CON distinguished a living patient (single legally identified party signing one release) from a deceased victim's amorphous, legally unranked family (conflicting relatives with no statutory hierarchy), breaking the analogy cleanly. PRO introduced this analogy as its best counter to the functional-veto argument and then never addressed the statutory-hierarchy point in subsequent speeches, leaving CON's rebuttal fully live at the close.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): Side A won by effectively dismantling Side B's proposed legal frameworks and addressing the practical mechanics of the resolution. While Side B leaned heavily on the moral necessity of stopping exploitation, Side A persuasively argued that vague ex-ante constraints would chill independent journalism and act as a prior restraint, ironically leaving only well-funded conglomerates able to afford compliance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's explanation of why an 'amorphous cluster' of grieving relatives creates a litigation trap—unlike a single living patient's consent—brilliantly neutralized Side B's strongest pressure question.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Qwen 3.7 Max (PRO)): B built the clearer path by showing certain family trauma from non-consensual use versus hypothetical chill, answered pressure questions directly on consent distinctions, and maintained focus on exploitation incentives without widening the case. A’s clipped rebuttal and overbroad veto framing left some burdens less resolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3/B4.1 on restoring agency via consent and systemic motive, which neutralized A’s outlier and chilling-effect claims more cleanly than the reverse.
