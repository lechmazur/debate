### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases, but B executed the sharper tactical debate. B caught a genuine tension in A's case (emergency response/tax administration framed as intolerably critical in the Opening, then reclassified as "non-critical burst" capacity in Rebuttal 1), and pressed it effectively through Pressure Questions and Rebuttal 2, forcing A into a baseline/surge distinction that B then exploited by reversing A's own hospital analogy ("hospitals rent ventilators, buy oxygen, pay the utility—the box is useless without the flows"), which landed as the debate's most memorable moment and went effectively unanswered in A's closing. B also sustained a consistent "managed dependence" framework (diversification, procurement enforcement, portability) that adapted well across rounds. A's central asymmetry claim (gradual, self-managed decay vs. instant foreign revocation) was a strong structural argument, but B's final closing directly challenged it ("decay is not a timetable you set; it is a stoppage with no failover"), and A had no further speech to answer. A did land a real hit late—that B's "domestic providers under friendly jurisdiction" solution still constitutes exactly the private-cloud dependence the motion warns against, enforced only through trust in an external entity—but this arrived late and B's closing offered at least a plausible rebuttal via the utility/bank regulation analogy. A's opening was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost. On balance, B's rebuttal execution, contradiction-hunting, and closing rhetoric gave it a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1/Pressure Questions exposing the contradiction between A's "emergency response is intolerable to lose" (Opening) and "census/disaster spikes are non-critical burst capacity" (Rebuttal 1), culminating in B's closing reversal of A's hospital/ICU analogy ("hospitals rent ventilators")—this exchange most damaged A's framing and went without a fully satisfying response.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): A won a clear but competitive debate by defending the motion’s limited claim: governments should own the critical baseline while using private providers for overflow. B offered credible diversification, regulation, cost, and security arguments, but repeatedly treated sovereign capacity as a single autarkic datacenter and never justified why owning a baseline becomes wasteful merely because peaks are rented. A’s comparison between gradual, manageable hardware degradation and immediate third-party revocation provided the cleaner residual reason to prefer sovereign capacity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 most decisively answered B’s pressure: it separated standing emergency functions from temporary surge demand, dissolving the alleged contradiction, then showed that foreign supply dependence affects both models while private cloud adds a separate off-switch risk.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a decisive asymmetry in failure modes. While Side B effectively proved that sovereign datacenters still rely on foreign chips, Side A absorbed this by demonstrating that hardware supply cuts lead to gradual, manageable decay, whereas cloud dependence risks instant deplatforming. Side A also successfully resolved Side B's pressure on the workload contradiction by clearly distinguishing standing critical cores from burstable peaks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized Side B's strongest point by conceding hardware dependence but proving that physical ownership still strictly dominates cloud leasing on the axis of sudden, catastrophic failure.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): CON built the more coherent, self-reinforcing case. Its chokepoint argument (chips, spares, software updates all still foreign regardless of ownership) forced PRO into the concession that sovereign capacity "cannot erase" upstream dependence, and CON kept returning to that concession as fatal. CON's pressure questions were sharply targeted and exposed a real internal tension in PRO's case: PRO answered that models must be regularly patched/updated (A1) yet also relied on a "frozen, audited serving stack" surviving a rupture for "months, even years" (A2) — a contradiction CON explicitly caught in Rebuttal 2 and never let go of. CON's domestic-commercial-provider-plus-statutory-priority alternative also turned PRO's own mechanism (statutory priority) into a symmetry argument that PRO could not fully neutralize; PRO's late attempt ("penalties don't conjure spare silicon") was answered by CON's closing observation that idle-capacity costs are symmetric whether owned or rented. PRO's analogies (submarines, petroleum reserves) were memorable but effectively undercut (mature vs. fast-depreciating tech; oil doesn't rot like GPUs age). By the close, CON had the cleaner, more unified narrative ("your architecture is our architecture wearing their flag"), while PRO's closing mostly reasserted claims without resolving the frozen/patched contradiction or the statutory-symmetry point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 catch that PRO's "frozen, audited serving stack" (meant to survive rupture) directly contradicts PRO's own Pressure-Question answer that models must be continually patched against new vulnerabilities — a genuine internal inconsistency PRO never fully resolved.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B won a competitive debate by identifying the stronger middle option: diversified domestic providers subject to statutory priority and surge obligations. A persuasively distinguished upstream chip dependence from operational control, but never established why ownership uniquely provides that control once domestic law can compel providers. B also kept depreciation, utilization, procurement, and budget fragility live. A's hybrid concession that frontier capacity could remain rented further narrowed the benefit of ownership, while its closing used erroneous reference IDs and did not fully answer mandated private surge capacity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.4 was decisive: B directly answered both pressure questions, showing that domestic law can impose public priority without ownership and that renting specifically avoids the refresh, staffing, and procurement risks created by a state-owned fleet.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully leveraging the rapid depreciation cycle of AI hardware (the 'treadmill') to prove that sovereign compute is an unsustainable strategy. By introducing regulated domestic commercial providers as a middle ground, Side B neutralized Side A's strongest jurisdictional points and forced Side A to defend the massive costs of maintaining idle, rapidly aging hardware for crisis surges.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal decisively dismantled the submarine analogy by contrasting the 50-year lifespan of mature tech with the 3-year depreciation cycle of compute, establishing the 'treadmill' as a central, unanswered burden.
