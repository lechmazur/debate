- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that depletion follows withdrawal volume, not title, and that Side B's public-contract model could preserve the same pumping and profit incentives. B offered strong institutional-risk weighing, but relied too heavily on a vested thirty-year permit that A had not proposed and never fully showed why a statutory revocable permit would be harder to administer than public leases.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's strongest response was that B manufactured a compensable long-term right despite A proposing a cuttable permit with no guaranteed dry-year volume.   Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly dismantled Side A's reliance on perfectly drafted regulatory caps by pointing out the asymmetry in legal resources and drafting power, explaining why historical permits always favor the firm.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A won the central policy comparison: aquifer harm depends on withdrawal volume, while the ban selectively targets bottling and leaves larger or substitutable uses untouched. Side B compellingly framed water as a civic reserve and closed the nearby-drilling loophole, but never established why enforceable caps could govern farms and factories yet not bottlers. A's emergency and scale claims were somewhat sweeping, but its alternative more directly protected households and aquifers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's decisive turn was that Side B relied on volumetric caps for farms, breweries, and factories while claiming those same caps could not restrain bottlers. That exposed the cargo-versus-input distinction as morally evocative but hydrologically weak, and also undermined B's regulatory-capture argument.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Side A systematically dismantled Side B's case by exposing contradictions in Side B's enforcement mechanisms. Side A showed that if regulators are too captured to enforce volumetric caps on bottlers, they are also too captured to police sham product reformulations, and successfully demonstrated that Side B's framework arbitrarily trusts caps for major agricultural and industrial users but not bottlers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal exposed Side B's fatal concession: by admitting that volumetric caps would apply to a bottler that transitions to another industry, Side B undermined their own core premise that caps are unenforceable due to regulatory capture.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON wins on outcome - litres, not logos. B shows PRO wavers between narrow ban [A1.8] and total aquifer ban [A2.1], then fails to answer substitution: soda/brewery/chip fab pumping same aquifer is untouched, and policing "sham reformulation" [A4.3] reintroduces the discretionary enforcement PRO calls doomed. B's usufructuary answer to [A3.1] neutralizes the owned-well scare, and the virtual-water close collapses PRO's input vs cargo distinction. PRO's local-harm point is vivid but never solves that the biggest drawdown remains legal under the ban.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2 + B4 A1/A2: trap on lawful-source escape hatch, usufructuary permits require no court order to curtail, and concession that caps are trusted for agriculture/brewery/fab but distrusted only for bottling = theory of villainy, not hydrology.
