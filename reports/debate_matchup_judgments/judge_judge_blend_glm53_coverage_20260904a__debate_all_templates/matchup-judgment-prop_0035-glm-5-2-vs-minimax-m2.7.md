### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): Side B won by making the motion broader and more reasonable than Side A's worst-case version. Side A had strong concerns about cost, regressiveness, and punishment, but repeatedly treated enforceable standards as necessarily uniform, unfunded, and closure-driven. Side B persuasively argued that standards can be phased, flexible, periodically tested, and paired with compliance pathways, and exposed that Side A's own alternatives included benchmarks and requirements that looked very similar to enforceable standards. Side A remained competitive on underfunded districts, but did not prove that the proposition as written required the punitive version it attacked.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's decisive rebuttal was that Side A's preferred phased requirements, performance benchmarks, grants, and targeted assistance already concede government-defined air quality outcomes; the real dispute was whether those outcomes should have enforcement teeth.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): PRO won by successfully showing that CON's proposed alternatives (phased requirements, performance benchmarks) effectively conceded the need for standards, while highlighting that without enforcement, commercial landlords face no consequences for the market failure of poor air quality. CON argued brilliantly on the regressive impacts on schools, but failed to address how voluntary incentives solve the problem in profitable workplaces.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 defense of performance-based regulation (citing vehicle emissions and food safety) decisively neutralized CON's claim that enforcing ongoing delivered performance is administratively impossible.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): PRO won by showing that enforceable standards can and typically do include flexible compliance pathways, funding, and phased timelines, which neutralized CON's administrative and regressive objections. CON's alternatives—targeted grants, performance benchmarks tied to building type—were shown to be standards by another name, leaving no principled distinction. PRO's market failure framing remained live and comparatively unanswered, while CON's literal reading of the motion as purely punitive could not withstand established regulatory precedent. PRO's clipped rebuttals were a real execution cost, but the visible material was sufficient to resolve the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal reframing CON's phased requirements and targeted grants as simply enforceable standards with flexibility, exposing that CON's objection was semantic rather than substantive.
