### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases, but A's "consent cannot be retroactive" framing—that disclosure prevents harm before emotional investment occurs, whereas B's outcome-based/self-correction model only catches deception after trust and time are already spent—was raised in Rebuttal 1, sharpened via pressure questions, and never squarely answered in B's final rebuttal or closing. B's strongest points (stigma on vulnerable users, blurred line between predictive text and substantive generation, ghostwriter parity) were real and forced concessions from A (notably conceding human ghostwriting is "also misleading"), but A recovered with a workable substantive-generation threshold and reframed disclosure as a light informed-consent mechanism rather than a ban or surveillance regime. Because B's closing reasserted its material-misrepresentation framework without directly rebutting the timing/sunk-cost argument that had gone unanswered since Rebuttal 1, A ends the debate with the clearer live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1 point that "self-correction" cannot restore wasted trust or time once emotional investment has occurred (sharpened in A3.2's pressure question) is the most decisive exchange, since B never directly answered it, leaving A's consent-timing argument standing at the close.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO successfully established that undisclosed AI-generated photos and substantive AI-written conversation assistance undermine informed consent in a context where users invest emotional trust based on signals of personal identity. While CON landed strong points on accessibility and line-drawing, PRO’s distinction between curation and fabrication, the enforceability justification for targeting AI, and especially the framing that consent cannot be retroactive and emotional harm occurs before “self-correction,” provided the cleaner route to victory. CON’s reliance on existing fraud rules left the pre-harm deception of synthetic persona-building largely unaddressed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The ghostwriting double-standard exchange (B3.1 → A4.1 → B4.4) and the line-drawing exchange (B3.2 → A4.3 → B4.5), where PRO conceded the moral parallel but justified differential treatment through practical detectability and limited the rule to substantive generation, while CON countered with surveillance concerns.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO kept the strongest live route: an undisclosed AI face unlike the user is conceded deception, and sustained AI-composed charm spends trust that self-correction cannot restore. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued with real substance, but B built a cleaner, more precise path to victory. B's core move—distinguishing "material misrepresentation" (already covered by catfishing/false-identity rules) from mere "AI touch" (which the motion's actual text sweeps in)—forced A into a genuine dilemma in the Pressure Questions exchange. A's answers there (A4.1–A4.2) either bit the bullet by conceding that even a single AI-suggested opener requires disclosure (confirming B's overbreadth charge) or offered a "generative synthesis alters facial geometry" line that B plausibly attacked as unworkable since the same tools perform both trivial and transformative edits. B's closing crystallized this into a clean, decisive frame: PRO's strongest cases are already redundant with existing rules, and the motion's actual bite falls on accessibility-adjacent, non-deceptive assistance. A never squarely answered the "adds nothing / overreaches" dichotomy in its closing, instead retreating to restated framing about consent and authenticity without resolving the line-drawing problem B exposed. A's Rebuttal 1 in Turn 4 was clipped for length, a real execution cost, but the substance delivered before the cut still carried the decisive points, so this cost B only modestly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Pressure Questions exchange and its answers (B3.1/B3.2 forcing A4.1/A4.2, then B4.4 seizing on A's admission that even a single suggested opener would require disclosure) was the decisive clash — it exposed that A's proposed line either collapses into redundancy with existing misrepresentation rules or bites an overbreadth bullet that A never recovered from.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Con wins by showing the motion overreaches: its broad text captures benign AI assistance (photo sharpening, suggested sentences) that involves no material falsehood, while the genuinely harmful cases Pro identifies—synthetic identities and fully outsourced personas—are already punishable as material misrepresentation under existing rules. Pro’s narrowing to “generative synthesis” strains the motion’s wording and concedes the core enforcement ground, whereas Con’s “target the lie, not the tool” offers a cleaner standard that protects users without burdening vulnerable groups or manufacturing suspicion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Con’s exposure of Pro’s retreat to a narrower harm than the motion’s text actually covers, demonstrating that AI-enhanced real photos and minor conversation assistance would be swept into the deceptive category without actually deceiving anyone about identity.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): B wins on the breadth-and-targeting clash. A proved that wholly synthetic faces and fully scripted personas mislead, but B showed those are already material misrepresentation reachable as catfishing without a blanket AI-disclosure rule. B's pressure questions exposed the proposition's overbreadth, and A in A4.2 conceded even a single AI-suggested opener would need a deceptive label, confirming CON's claim that the rule punishes ordinary editing and chills vulnerable users. A's enforcement-gap argument was answered directly by B's workable standard: punish images that don't represent the user, not every AI touch.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B R2 A1/A2 - answered A's catfishing Q with a material-misrepresentation test for novel AI faces and used its own Q to force A to admit one-sentence assistance would be branded deceptive, crystallizing the lie-vs-tool distinction.
