### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): B narrowly won by separating legible, enforceable limits from an individual opt-in veto. A compellingly established the personal and durable nature of synthetic voices, but repeatedly treated itemization, payment, auditing, and deletion as benefits unique to opt-in. B showed those protections can exist in collectively bargained standard terms, while opt-in at the same take-it-or-leave-it hiring session does little for day players and mainly adds clearance and holdout risks. A's upfront-timing response weakened B's most dramatic hostage framing, but did not resolve the central coercion objection.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's permanence response was decisive: an opted-in model remains reusable on a server too, so deletion, custody, and purpose limits address that risk more directly than a separate signature. This sharpened B's broader distinction between clarity and veto, which A never fully overcame.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully separating the desire for "clarity and limits" (which can be achieved through union-negotiated standard contracts) from the mechanism of an "individual opt-in veto." A effectively proves that for vulnerable day players, an individual veto provides no leverage, while for studios, it creates a holdout problem that threatens localization. B's arguments about permanence and legibility are sharply neutralized by A's distinction between custody rules (deletion duties) and property rules (vetoes).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, specifically the argument that "an opt-in model doesn't go home either... A veto doesn't delete a file," which perfectly neutralized B's core claim about the unique harm of AI permanence.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) successfully split the debate into two issues: legible scope and the individual veto. By conceding that scope should be itemized and enforceable within a collective standard, but showing that an individual opt-in veto primarily empowers stars and estates to hold productions hostage, Side A neutralized Side B's appeal to consent. Side A's practical harms—market friction killing indie and non-English dubs, shrinking residuals, and pushing studios toward fully synthetic voices that owe performers nothing—were concrete and went largely unanswered. Side B's principled claim that a right without refusal is merely a tariff was eloquent, but never overcame Side A's demonstration that the refusal right is illusory for coerced day players and destructive for the working ensembles Side B claimed to protect.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 separation of itemization from veto, showing that precise, auditable scope can live inside a collectively bargained standard without converting every performer's signature into a holdout right that blocks the dub.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by distinguishing conventional dubbing from synthetic impersonation and showing that compensation, disclosure, and model-retention rules do not answer the autonomy or reputational harm of an unauthorized cloned performance. Side B created serious pressure around retaliation, bargaining inequality, and localization costs, but overstated that collective protections make individual consent redundant. A's combination of a non-waivable ban on employment bundling, later use-specific consent, union floors, and human-dub fallback preserved the proposition while answering the leverage trap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's A1/A2 response was decisive: it rejected B's hire-time waiver dilemma by separating a non-waivable anti-bundling rule from the performer's later, specific opt-in. B correctly challenged enforcement, but its reply never established why collective terms alone should authorize cloning after an express refusal.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by demonstrating that an individual opt-in right is ineffective and risky for actors without bargaining power. While Side B makes a strong principled case against 'paid impersonation,' Side A's Rebuttal 2 systematically dismantles B's practical defense, showing that anti-retaliation laws cannot protect against subjective casting decisions and that hybrid pipelines would economically punish holdouts. Side A brilliantly frames Side B's reliance on a 'bundling ban' as a concession that collective rules, rather than individual consent, do the real protective work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's R2 argument distinguishing casting from standard employment. By pointing out that casting is discretionary, subjective, and leaves no vacancy record, A decisively neutralized B's anti-retaliation enforcement mechanism.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that opt-in consent is individual theater that the powerless cannot safely exercise. CON absorbed PRO's non-waivable bundling ban as a collective instrument, proving the "individual yes" adds no protection beyond what stars already possess. While PRO's identity-harm and separability arguments were sharp, CON's casting-retaliation critique and the demonstration that refusal would be punished by future hiring—without discoverable evidence—undermined the workability of opt-in. The decisive clash resolved in favor of binding collective terms over a formal right that would be waived by the weak and wielded by the strong.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turn that PRO's non-waivable bundling ban is itself a collective regulatory instrument, rendering the individual opt-in merely a visibility layer that protects no new performers, combined with the critique that anti-retaliation law fails in discretionary casting contexts.
