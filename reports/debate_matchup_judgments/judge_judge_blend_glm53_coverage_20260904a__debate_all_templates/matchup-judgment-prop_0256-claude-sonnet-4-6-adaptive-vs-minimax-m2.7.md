### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A wins narrowly because the proposition is modest: parents should have more power than app makers currently allow, not that toggles alone solve child safety. Side B made strong points about technical ambiguity, liability laundering, and design accountability, but too often treated direct platform regulation as mutually exclusive with parental opt-out power. Side A effectively answered that integrated controls on main platforms are different from separate kids products and can be a second line of defense while preserving company accountability. Side A’s repeated clipping was an execution cost, and Side B had the cleaner rhetoric, but Side B did not fully negate the core claim that parents should have more meaningful ability to block or delay engagement-driven recommendation features.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s first rebuttal against technical incoherence was decisive: the motion requires more parental power over recommendation features, not a perfectly neutral “algorithm-free” internet. That blunted Side B’s strongest attempt to make the proposal conceptually impossible.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by consistently driving home the technical reality that turning off personalization leaves children with chronological or viral feeds, which are arguably more dangerous. Side B masterfully turned Side A's arguments against them, especially by using Side A's admission about the main platform's content pool to prove that a sorting toggle doesn't fix the underlying danger. Side A also suffered from repeated execution errors, having speeches clipped in four out of five rounds.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 smoothly answered the Pressure Questions by distinguishing adult impulse control from children's, and brilliantly weaponized Side A's own logic about the content pool to show why a toggle fails.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): A wins by exploiting PRO's concession on content pools being the core problem and showing parental toggles cannot address design-level harm, while keeping focus on company accountability instead of shifting burden to parents. PRO's mandate-incentive claim remains speculative by comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 directly answers chronological-feed pressure and leverages the content-pool concession to neutralize PRO's toggle mechanism.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by showing the proposition mislocates the problem. B turned A's own concession that curated products are a "different proposition entirely" into the decisive content-pool argument: if the main platform's library and design logic are built for engagement, a sorting-layer toggle cannot protect children from what is in the pool. B's "liability laundering" and "more control over a harmful machine" framing held up through the exchange, while A's market-forcing counter remained speculative and was undermined by severe execution costs from four clipped speeches that left key burdens unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing conversion of A's "separate products" admission into the content-pool argument, proving that the toggle cannot solve the design logic of the main platform ecosystem.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M2.7 (PRO)): CON wins by successfully decoupling "more parental control" from "better child safety," demonstrating that disabling recommendation algorithms often defaults to chronological or popularity feeds that can expose children to more dangerous, unfiltered viral content. CON also effectively turned PRO's regulatory mandate argument, noting that if regulators can force toggles, they can directly mandate safer design without outsourcing risk to parents. While PRO offered a pragmatic market-pressure mechanism, CON's "pool vs. sorting layer" frame and sharper handling of liability laundering provided a cleaner, more decisive reason to reject the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2, which answered PRO's pressure questions by distinguishing adult vs. child impulse control to defend the chronological feed danger, then turned PRO's admission about separate content pools to argue that a toggle doesn't fix the underlying harmful ecosystem. The pivot to "if you can mandate toggles, you can mandate safe design directly" decisively undercut PRO's mechanism.
