### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A won primarily by landing and sustaining the "cap in practice" argument: if verified accommodation requires confirmed beds before offers, then when beds run out, offers are refused—which is a cap in substance regardless of what you call it. CON never fully escaped this. Their best response was "we apply it universally, not by nationality," but that reframe doesn't neutralize the underlying logic; it only shifts which cohorts face the limit. CON's revenue argument was the most persistent counter—cuts to international tuition starve construction budgets—but PRO answered it cleanly by labeling the arrangement a "bridge loan" on current renters and arguing that public institutions cannot justify present housing harm by pointing to hypothetical future supply. CON repeated the revenue point across multiple rounds without meaningfully engaging PRO's specific framing of who carries the cost during the gap years. CON's Q2 pressure (domestic backfill eliminates net relief) was the sharpest challenge and PRO's answer ("that's an evasion of policy's purpose") was the weakest moment in PRO's case, but it didn't collapse the broader argument. CON's "nationality-based freeze" objection had real traction but was only partially answered; the motion's specificity to international recruitment remains a genuine awkwardness. PRO also suffered real execution costs from three clipped speeches, though the visible arguments were well-formed. On balance, PRO built the cleaner comparative path: make the capacity limit explicit, enforceable, and applied before harm, rather than depending on informal mechanisms that either reproduce the cap quietly or allow continuing harm.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's "cap in practice" move in Rebuttal 1 (A2.4) was the most decisive exchange. PRO argued that CON's own preferred tools—verified accommodation, guaranteed first-year housing, offers tied to available beds—already constitute a cap in substance; PRO merely makes it explicit and public-interest-driven rather than leaving it as an informal private scramble. CON's subsequent responses across Rebuttal 2 and the closing tried to distinguish "universal capacity check" from a nationality freeze, but this distinction, while real, never resolved the logical core of PRO's point: that any genuine commitment to verified beds produces the same limiting outcome when supply is insufficient.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by cleanly differentiating their 'universal capacity check' from Side B's demographic-specific cap, demonstrating that capping only international students fails to reduce aggregate demand if domestic students backfill, while simultaneously gutting the revenue needed to build new housing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 6 and Turn 8 pressure question exchange decisively exposed that Side B's international-only cap could simply result in domestic substitution, failing to solve the housing shortage while destroying the construction budget.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): CON showed that nationality-specific caps arbitrarily single out one group without reliably reducing aggregate housing demand, while undermining the revenue streams necessary to finance new supply. PRO’s attempt to collapse CON’s verified-accommodation alternative into a cap in practice was effectively distinguished as universal demand management rather than demographic discrimination. PRO’s weak answer to the domestic-backfill problem and its failure to negate CON’s fiscal and supply-side mechanisms left CON with the cleaner live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s rebuttal distinguishing universal verified-bed enrollment management from a nationality-specific cap, and demonstrating that slashing international tuition defunds the very construction needed to escape the shortage, materially damaged PRO’s live route to victory.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): CON (Side B) won on the decisive targeting clash. PRO's institutional mandate argument—that international recruitment is the elastic, revenue-driven margin universities can cut without violating statutory obligations—explained why it is administratively convenient to cap international students, but never established why doing so actually reduces housing pressure better than cutting any other net off-campus demand. CON's consistent "wrong wheel" framing (a capped international student in a residence hall harms nothing; an uncapped out-of-state domestic student renting locally harms plenty) was never squarely demolished. PRO's best answer—backfilling is "structurally limited"—was thin and conceded that if the cap doesn't materially reduce housing demand, it only excludes an easily excluded group. CON's Rebuttal 2 A2 response was the most decisive single exchange: it answered PRO's immediacy pressure by showing the housing-neutral admissions freeze is also applied at enrollment, not downstream construction, giving it the same real-time bite as PRO's circuit breaker while actually targeting rental bids rather than nationality. PRO's "unconstrained capacity with better paperwork" line was rhetorically strong but CON's specific mechanism (no new off-campus demand unless beds, leases, or staggered starts are guaranteed) survived that charge. PRO's framework remained compelling as a civic-mandate argument, but CON held the more important ground: proportionality to the actual harm. Format penalties for CON's clipped Opening and Rebuttal 1 limited CON's first-half impact, narrowing the margin, but CON's later-round consistency and precision across the pressure-question exchange preserved the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 A2 answer: responding to PRO's challenge that CON's alternative lags the crisis by years, CON clarified that the housing-neutral admissions freeze operates at the same enrollment decision point as PRO's cap—no additional cohort unless beds, non-displacing leases, or staggered starts are in place. This directly undercut PRO's strongest card (immediacy) while preserving CON's core advantage (targeting actual rental demand rather than nationality), and PRO's closing never addressed this specific reframing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): Side A won by successfully proving a mismatch between the proposed policy (capping by nationality) and the stated harm (off-campus housing demand). Side A's alternative—an admissions freeze on net new off-campus demand—neutralized Side B's argument that only an international cap could act as an immediate 'circuit breaker.' Side B never adequately resolved the flaw that their cap would penalize dorm-housed international students while missing out-of-state domestic renters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Turn 8 (Side A Rebuttal 2) decisively answered Side B's pressure regarding immediacy by explaining that an off-campus demand cap operates as an immediate freeze at admissions, while sharply crystallizing the flaw in Side B's targeting.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): CON won by exposing a persistent mismatch between PRO’s policy and the housing harm it claimed to address. PRO argued international recruitment is the discretionary, elastic margin and that a nationality-based cap provides an immediate circuit breaker. CON effectively showed that housing scarcity is driven by net rental demand, not passport status, and that PRO’s cap would block international students in dedicated housing while missing nonlocal domestic students who actually bid for scarce apartments. CON’s double-bind—if backfilling is limited the cap barely eases housing; if it is large it starves the university of housing-construction revenue—further weakened PRO’s effectiveness claim. PRO’s concession that the cap is a demand brake “not a housing assignment tool” conceded too much of the displacement rationale. CON answered PRO’s timing and feasibility pressure by offering immediate admissions-level constraints on net off-campus demand that operate without construction lag, undercutting PRO’s claim that only an international cap could act in real time. Across the exchange, CON sharpened the ballot question to whether the policy caps housing pressure or merely caps a category of people, and PRO never escaped that frame.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s “brake attached to the wrong wheel” exchange, which showed PRO’s cap catches international students in university beds while missing nonlocal domestic renters in private apartments—combined with PRO’s concession that the cap need not route units to locals—materially undermined PRO’s emergency-rationale by proving the mechanism targeted the wrong variable.
