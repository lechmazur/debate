- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) showed that licensing already delivers transparency and debris control without the irreversible harms of premature title recognition, and that recognizing output before a full treaty would create vested legal claims and incompatible national blessings at scarce lunar sites that no future allocation could unwind. B’s pressure questions exposed real gaps in cross-border certainty and enforcement, and B’s closing cleanly crystallized that the motion adds only risk, not value, when licensing already suffices.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 1 distinction that “one is a leash; the other is a deed,” and the subsequent dilemma that recognition is either unilateral (contested paper) or reciprocal (the treaty PRO says we cannot wait for), which undermined PRO’s central trade-off and anchored CON’s winning weighing in the final speeches.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A consistently neutralized Side B's offense by separating conduct rules (which can be enforced via launch licensing) from property rights. Side A's explanation that "clouded" title pressures investors to seek a genuine treaty was a decisive winning mechanism that Side B failed to overcome.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's final response to the "captive buyer" flip: clarifying that while captive space agencies might ignore clouded title, the investors and insurers funding the missions will not, perfectly preserving A's mechanism for incentivizing a future treaty.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON wins by separating the motion into two distinct elements—conduct regulation and property title—and showing that the transparency and debris conditions PRO prizes are already enforceable through launch licensing, while the motion's distinctive and controversial ingredient (internationally portable title) creates grandfathered vested interests and preempts equitable allocation before any treaty exists. PRO's "buyer enforcement" mechanism was dismantled by CON's captive-buyer observation, and PRO never recovered from CON's central demonstration that obligations do not hinge on title recognition but on launch authority. CON's closing crystallization—that the motion "notarizes its winners" rather than governing the scramble—accurately captures the live asymmetry after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B2.1/B4.4 distinction between conduct regulation and title grant, showing that debris and transparency duties are conditions of launch authority rather than a trade requiring legal sale recognition, which dissolved PRO's core framing that conditioned legality is the only path to enforceable obligations.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Claude Fable 5 (high) (PRO)): CON won the leverage clash. PRO's core hinge was that title must be legal to attach transparency/debris duties [B1.3][B4.5]. CON showed duties attach to launch licensing regardless of sale legality [A2.1][A4.4], so recognition adds only portable title, not governance. CON then pressed unanswered costs: portable title entrenches prime-site first movers [A1.5], safety zones equal de facto exclusion [A4.6], and bloc reciprocal recognition [B4.1] formalizes rival legalities [A4.5] and kills incentive for allocation, with seabed history supporting grandfathering risk [A2.7]. B's buyer-enforcement and cloud critique was turned by A's investor/insurer audience shift.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.4 + A4.2/A4.3: Debris/disclosure duties are conditions of launch (comply or don't fly), not a bargain for title, so CON's withholding produces a cloud on investors/insurers that preserves pressure for allocation, directly undercutting PRO's claim that obligations only bind because sale is lawful.
