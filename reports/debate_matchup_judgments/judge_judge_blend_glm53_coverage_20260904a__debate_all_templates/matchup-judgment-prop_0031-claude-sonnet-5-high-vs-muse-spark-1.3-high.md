### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that broad legalization creates far more viable sites, distributes growth more fairly, and cannot plausibly cause an immediate infrastructure surge when redevelopment is naturally gradual. Side A never substantiated its crucial claim that bonuses would produce the same housing at the same speed, and overstated the choice by treating bonus conditions as the only possible source of infrastructure funding. B also answered the durability challenge well: vested projects survive under either policy, while broader rights create a larger constituency and avoid the quiet throttling of uptake through conditions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was turning A’s labor-limited, gradual-growth concession against the bonus model: slow turnover answers the infrastructure-surge concern, while legalizing many more parcels gives constrained builders more viable opportunities. A’s response that modest fees cost essentially nothing in output was asserted rather than demonstrated.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully proving that bonus systems structurally throttle housing supply by artificially limiting eligible lots, whereas a ban provides vastly more "shots on goal" for a limited labor pool. Side A's case relied heavily on the premise that both policies would yield the "identical pace of construction," which Side B effectively dismantled. While Side A made an excellent defensive point that bans can also be administratively hollowed out, Side B's argument that regular property taxes fund infrastructure just fine—while bonus exactions often kill projects entirely—remained the more credible path to actual housing production.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal directly destroyed Side A's premise of "identical output" by pointing out that even with a limited labor pool, having 50,000 eligible lots provides vastly more viable projects than rationing development to 50 bonus parcels.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): B kept the decisive clash on the default rule and eligibility: exclusion as the norm yields tiny uptake and a two-tier map, while broad by-right permission multiplies shots on goal under the same labor constraint both sides conceded. A’s durability-and-funding dilemma was sharp and well-repeated, but it never overcame B’s replies that vested rights plus many new owners make rollback costly, that quiet administrative choking kills bonuses without a vote, and that programs which produce almost nothing cannot fund capacity either. B made the choice easier: same gradual pace, but only a ban ends legal exclusion and spreads growth.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 A1/A2: vested permits protect either way, but a ban creates thousands of defending owners while bonuses die by quiet condition-tightening; value is recaptured via diluted land costs and a broader tax base, not fees that starve the very projects that would fund sewers.
