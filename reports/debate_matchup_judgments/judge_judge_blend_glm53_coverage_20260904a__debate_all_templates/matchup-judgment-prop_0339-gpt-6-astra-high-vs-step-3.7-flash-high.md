### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the decisive clash on the motion as written: a required store category toggle is not the same as targeted, reliable child access control. B repeatedly showed PRO claiming usability simplicity while also claiming enforcement, accurate “high-risk social” sorting, and activity-level protection that app labels do not deliver. A’s parental-accessibility frame had force, but A overreached on existing subcategories, stretched “block or hide” into fuller OS control, and never neutralized overbreadth or shared-device handover problems. Multiple A clippings also weakened late weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s sustained rebuttal that PRO sells precise access-control benefits while defending only a blunt whole-app category switch—especially the app-vs-activity and hide-vs-installed-app distinction.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won the central classification and enforcement clash. PRO needed to show a store category toggle can target "high-risk social" without overbreadth and that "block or hide" actually blocks installed apps. B's pressure Q1 exposed the missing threshold; A's answer invented granular subcategories and asserted "or" means both, which B rebutted. Combined with conceded existing free controls and repeated clipping costs for A, CON's narrow weighing beat PRO's broad harm claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2/B4 line that app-level category filtering removes both school chats and harmful recommendations inside same app, and that "or" does not require both hide and block, collapsing PRO's benefit claim into blunt filtering vs full profile management.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B wins decisively by exposing the technical and logical flaws in Side A's proposal. Side A conflated app store search categories with OS-level access controls, falsely claiming that blocking an app store category could disable specific "risky features" inside an installed app. Side B cleanly dismantled this by explaining that app-level blocks remove all functionality (e.g., school chats alongside harmful feeds) and that hiding a store listing does not block an already installed app. Furthermore, Side A suffered severe execution costs, being clipped for exceeding the word limit in three separate turns (Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2, and Closing), which left key arguments incomplete and violated the format constraints. Side B maintained strict epistemic discipline, answered pressure questions directly, and controlled the clash throughout without relying on fake precision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where it systematically dismantled Side A's claim that parents could use app store "subcategories" to block only the risky features of an app, correctly pointing out that an app store label cannot selectively disable internal app functionalities.
