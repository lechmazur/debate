### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): PRO built a stronger cumulative path to victory across the exchange. The harms narrative—weak-tie erosion, health costs of isolation, class-based exclusion from pay-to-enter venues—was largely uncontested; CON conceded some decline and market limits by Rebuttal 2. PRO's parks counterexample was the most decisive single move: parks are publicly funded, zero-cost, purpose-agnostic gathering spaces that people use specifically to linger and socialize, which directly punctures CON's "functional anchor" theory. CON's response—that parks work because people come for exercise and play—is only partially convincing, since many park users come purely to sit and socialize. The library analogy also survived: CON tried to distinguish it as utility-anchored, but PRO's "participatory design plus local stewardship" rebuttal was at least plausible as a design solution rather than a structural impossibility. CON's strongest live argument—the rent-spiral concern and the deregulation-vs.-mandates contradiction—was answered adequately if not definitively. PRO's "complementary scalpel" framing (remove parking minimums while adding only a small amenity requirement in large developments) held together, even if the rent math was asserted rather than proven. CON's "you cannot mandate organic community" line is rhetorically strong and captured a genuine concern, but it was never fully reconciled with the fact that publicly funded parks and libraries already produce organic community at scale. The private-substitutes dispute ultimately favored PRO: churches, volunteer groups, and barbershops are real but curated and conditional, and do not serve the financially marginal user PRO consistently foregrounded. CON's original "functional anchor" theory was genuinely interesting and the strongest intellectual contribution of the debate, but it was not enough to neutralize PRO's accumulated case on access, market failure, and existing successful public-space precedents.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's parks pressure question (Q1 in Round 5) and follow-up in Rebuttal 2 was the most decisive exchange. By pointing out that public parks thrive as pure gathering and lingering spaces with no consumption requirement, PRO directly falsified CON's core "functional anchor" axiom—the claim that government-funded spaces only succeed when they offer a primary utility beyond socializing. CON's answer that parks work because of exercise and play was undercut by the plain reality that many park users simply sit, chat, and linger without any fitness purpose, which PRO crystallized clearly. This exchange left CON's central structural argument materially damaged for the remainder of the debate.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A narrowly wins because it preserved the broad motion: governments can both undo exclusionary zoning and fund proven public third-place infrastructure like libraries, pools, parks, and mixed-use public amenities. Side B’s best move was to narrow the case to sterile “community living rooms” and costly mandates, but that did not fully answer A’s wider package of deregulation plus public-goods funding. A also effectively answered the “government caused it, so government cannot fix it” point as fatalism, and showed that private substitutes are often paywalled or socially selective. B had sharper rebuttal rhetoric and a strong cost/organic-community critique, but it leaned too heavily on excluding successful public examples from the proposition.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s functional-anchor rebuttal to libraries and parks was the strongest exchange, but A’s response that these public spaces still prove government can create low-cost third places kept the pro route alive.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side B prevailed by keeping the live clash on policy efficacy: they differentiated parks/libraries (purpose-driven) from mandated "social rooms," showed mandates/grants risk rent hikes and sterility, and answered PRO's questions with concrete zero-cost alternatives while PRO's analogies and "scalpels" failed to neutralize those risks. PRO built a clear problem but could not prove the proposed fix would work without side effects.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 directly answered both pressure Qs, then crystallized why pure-social grants lack functional draw and replicate the original cost pressures.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO built a structurally stronger path across the full debate. Its most decisive moves were: (1) reframing current zoning regimes as the prior intervention, so removing parking minimums and single-use mandates is deregulation—not new mandates—which cut CON's affordability objection at the root; (2) the positive-externality argument, which CON never genuinely neutralized (responding only that externalities don't justify a "blank check," not that the market-failure logic is wrong); and (3) turning CON's own concessions—built environment distorted, zoning reform wise, loneliness real—into affirmative support. The framing that "adaptation to scarcity is not preference" is the sharpest single line in the debate and accurately names the burden CON never satisfied: explaining what mechanism, under their alternative of mere deregulation, actually reverses the documented decline. CON's strongest moment was correctly distinguishing active funding from deregulation and pressing that the motion requires more than rule-removal, and its "walkable cities still lonely" empirical point created genuine pressure. PRO's response (degraded supply drives low use) was only partially answered; CON's counter (declining demand drives degraded supply, not the reverse) remains somewhat live. But CON never supplied a complete causal story for how loneliness gets reversed under their preferred path, and "let genuine demand determine" is too thin given the documented structural failure PRO named. CON's closing was the only unclipped final speech, a small execution credit; PRO's clipped closing cost it some crystallization. On net, PRO's theoretical foundation, sharper rebuttals, and stronger comparative framing deliver a clear but competitive win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 move reframing current single-use zoning and parking minimums as the active distortion already in place—so removing them is deregulation, not new mandates. This turned CON's affordability concern against them and repositioned PRO as the anti-intervention side, forcing CON to spend significant later speech time clarifying that the motion requires active spending beyond deregulation rather than building new offense.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins a clear but competitive debate. B’s best move was distinguishing deregulation from active funding and pressing the mechanism gap between spaces and reduced loneliness. But A more convincingly tied the decline of third places to government-created zoning distortions and market underprovision of positive externalities, then defended funding through familiar public goods like libraries, parks, plazas, and community centers. B’s tradeoff argument was weakened by treating libraries and parks as competing priorities even though they are central examples of third places, and its demand-shift claims remained mostly asserted rather than proven. A’s case better fit the motion as written: fund and zone for revival, not merely hope voluntary actors solve the externality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s strongest rebuttal was that “revealed preference” for isolation is not real preference when people inherit car-dependent, commercially optimized environments with few viable gathering spaces. That answer undercut B’s demand-side framing and made zoning reform plus targeted public investment look corrective rather than paternalistic.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO built the stronger overall case by grounding the motion in market externalities and government's prior role in distorting the built environment, then using CON's own concessions on zoning reform, the real loneliness crisis, and built-environment effects to show that mere deregulation repeats the original failure; PRO answered pressure questions directly with plausible mechanisms while CON's responses stayed at the level of speculation about demand shifts without neutralizing the externality argument.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1/A2 answers to CON pressure questions on healthcare mechanisms and funding-risk design, which turned CON's concessions into a live burden that the closing crystallized.
