### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) ran the cleaner, more coherent case throughout. The decisive line was the asymmetry of harms argument: accidental amplification of outrage reshapes a user's worldview without consent, while accidental suppression merely leaves a user uninformed about something they never requested—with other discovery avenues intact. PRO established this frame early (B3.2) and developed it fully in Rebuttal 2 (B4.4), while CON only managed to construct a competing version of the same asymmetry after it had already been framed against them. PRO also answered the pressure questions directly with proper A1/A2 labeling and offered the strongest single inversion in the debate: the status quo has already abandoned the civic sphere—it's trading civic content for engagement-bait and calling the result "shared reality." CON's strongest objections (definitional fuzziness, casual users won't opt in) were addressed adequately: PRO distinguished algorithmic push from other discovery pathways, and noted that the same definitional fuzziness currently amplifies partisan outrage rather than suppressing it. CON's "zero incentive to fix rage-bait" closing point was genuinely interesting but arrived too late to shift the overall weight of the exchange, and CON's Rebuttal 2 was clipped before completion—an execution cost that prevented its full comparative case from landing. PRO's closings, though also clipped, had crystallized the key decision rule more completely by that point.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.4) responding to CON's definitional and civic-content concerns: PRO turned the fuzziness argument against CON by pointing out that the same definitional uncertainty currently causing platforms to misclassify content is amplifying partisan rage rather than suppressing it under the status quo. This directly neutralized CON's strongest rebuttal and reframed the comparison: accidental suppression of unrequested content is a smaller harm than accidental amplification of content designed to enrage.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON wins on the strength of a single structural argument that PRO never satisfactorily resolved: the "knowledge trap." Disengaged users who most need incidental civic exposure—voting deadlines, local elections, policy changes—are precisely those who will never opt in, because opting in requires knowing you're missing something. PRO repeatedly offered the counter that users "can search or follow community accounts," but this is circular: it presupposes awareness of what to seek. PRO also never clearly distinguished the motion's broad reach (covering all political content, including legitimate civic material) from the narrower harm of misinformation bait, which allowed CON to effectively characterize the proposal as overkill that punishes local journalism and civic organizations alongside engagement-farming. CON's early identification of the contradiction in PRO's framing—buried opt-out harms vulnerable users, so will a buried opt-in—was only partially addressed by PRO's cookie-consent analogy, which was introduced late and never developed into a full enforcement mechanism. PRO's opening grandmother scenario was vivid and established strong initial stakes, but PRO's later speeches became increasingly repetitive, cycling back to misinformation harms without engaging the structural civic-invisibility problem. PRO's closing was clipped for exceeding word limits, a real execution cost at the round's most important weighing moment. CON's closing crystallized neatly around one clean unanswered question, framed the civic-abandonment consequence memorably, and advanced the comparison rather than merely recapping. The margin is real but not dominant because PRO's core intuition—that forced outrage-bait is a genuine harm—was never refuted, only outweighed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to PRO's Q2 was the most decisive exchange. CON argued that the disengaged users PRO claims to protect cannot "search for what they don't know exists"—and that followed accounts only reach those already following them, making the suggestion circular. PRO's response in the same round conceded the misinformation-versus-civic-info tradeoff framing rather than answering the structural discovery problem directly, leaving CON's knowledge-trap argument standing at the end of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side B won by identifying a fatal structural flaw in Side A's case: users cannot search for or opt into civic information they do not know exists. Side A was forced to concede the loss of incidental exposure, relying entirely on the claim that avoiding misinformation outweighed this loss, which Side B successfully defeated by arguing that hiding all political content is an overbroad solution that creates civic abandonment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal decisively dismantled Side A's "users can just search for it" defense by explaining the basic information theory principle that disengaged users cannot seek out what they don't know exists.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.1 (CON)): Side B wins because their core route—that disengaged users never learn what they do not know exists without default exposure—survives unanswered after the full exchange, including the pressure round, while A repeatedly retreats to already-admitted outrage harms without resolving the knowledge trap or the enforcement inconsistency on opt-in friction.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.3–B2.4 and B4.3–B4.5, which directly pressed the structural barrier for unaware users and were not neutralized in A’s A1/A2 answers or clipped closing.
