### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): CON won the decisive comparative: forecasting a priced endpoint is not decision-relevant causal analysis, and subsidized formal status adds manipulation and contract-design problems without self-enforcing official accountability. PRO was sharp on incentives, visibility vs committee capture, and "formal input not override," but under pressure retreated to documentation mandates and expert interpretation—exactly the expert-led machinery CON argued can be strengthened without privileging betting. CON answered pressure cleanly and made the motion's institutional shift fail on causality, influence, and who actually bears the cost of error.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2/closing: conditional narrowing still fails endogeneity, and a moved price does not force officials to revise—PRO's case still depends on the governance rules it treats as mere paperwork.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): CON preserved two live routes PRO never neutralized: (1) prediction markets forecast outcomes but do not isolate policy causation, and narrowing conditional contracts still leaves endogeneity unaddressed; (2) financial discipline fails when traders can profit more from swaying policy than from betting accuracy, while visibility alone does not diagnose intent. CON also showed that the governance mechanisms PRO relied on to give markets teeth can be applied directly to expert review without subsidizing a trading venue. PRO’s incentive story and rhetoric were strong, but never adequately bridged the forecasting-causation gap or closed the manipulation loophole, so CON’s case against formally elevating market prices over mainly expert-led analysis remained the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s repeated demonstration that conditional contracts and granularity do not solve the causal inference problem—showing that a price conditioned on an observable like unemployment still confounds the policy effect with the conditions that triggered the policy, and that simply multiplying contracts divides liquidity without creating valid counterfactuals.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively exposing the epistemic limits of prediction markets for policy-making, particularly the endogeneity problem in conditional contracts (correlation vs. causation) and the fact that a trader's policy payoff can easily outweigh their market losses. Side A also brilliantly separated the market mechanism from the administrative governance rules required to make it work, showing that CON's preferred reforms could achieve PRO's accountability goals without the flaws of subsidized betting. Side B's reliance on "narrowing the conditioning" failed to resolve the causal inference problem, leaving PRO's core mechanism unjustified for major policy decisions.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 on the causality problem, explaining that narrowing observed conditions (e.g., unemployment > 6%) does not eliminate the endogeneity of political decision-making, thereby dismantling PRO's defense of conditional contracts.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the decisive structural clash live: self-referential/corruptible policy markets, the thin-market-manipulation problem, and the theater-vs-teeth trap around "reviewable reasons." A had a clean formal-input theory and answered format pressure directly, but kept qualifying the mechanism (conflict bars, quality discounts, no compulsory deference) in ways that either hollowed its benefit or left discretion to ignore inconvenient prices. B's interim-window point—that manipulation only needs to move the live price during the live decision, not win at settlement—and the "who checks the checker" critique of agency-authored dismissal criteria were never squarely neutralized. B made the choice easier: the proposed check creates a subsidized attack surface judged by the same institutions it is meant to constrain.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R2 (and earlier R1 trap): interim price moves force officials to spend capital rebutting manufactured doubt before settlement, while "reviewable reasons" remain self-graded agency paperwork—exposing A's accountability model as either toothless theater or a binding manipulable number.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON won by exposing two structural flaws in PRO's mechanism that PRO never resolved. First, CON showed that manipulation does not need to succeed at settlement to damage policy; it only needs to move the live price during the live decision window, forcing officials to spend capital rebutting manufactured doubt. Second, CON demonstrated that PRO's "reviewable reasons" framework is accountability theater: the same agency embarrassed by an inconvenient price authors the criteria and writes the memo explaining why it can safely ignore that price. PRO's responses about preannounced quality criteria and post-hoc oversight did not establish an independent referee, so CON's "self-graded homework" critique survived the exchange. Because the mechanism PRO defended is either toothless or dangerously manipulable at the moment decisions are actually made, CON built the stronger path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing crystallized the "self-graded homework" dilemma, showing that PRO's reviewable-reasons model leaves the monitored agency in control of both the market's credibility standards and the divergence memo, undermining the independence PRO promised.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively dismantling Side B's core mechanism. Side B relied on "reviewable reasons" to balance market input with government discretion, but Side A effectively showed this amounts to "self-graded homework" where the agency uses its own criteria to dismiss inconvenient market prices without an independent referee. Side A also won the manipulation clash by highlighting that political damage and manufactured doubt occur during the live decision window, rendering Side B's settlement-based safeguards and subsidy designs largely irrelevant to the actual policy impact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8), specifically the A1 and A2 answers, which sharply exposed that market manipulation achieves its political goal before settlement and that "reviewable reasons" lack an independent referee, collapsing Side B's accountability mechanism.
