### Judge Notes

- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON won by establishing a durable comparative burden that PRO never discharged. CON’s core case—that vouchers bundle distinct transactional risks (bureaucratic timing, inspection dependencies, renewal uncertainty)—survived PRO’s rebuttals, especially once PRO conceded that “ordinary processing uncertainty must be accommodated,” effectively admitting landlords could no longer screen for that specific risk. The decisive turn was CON’s supply asymmetry: a mandate can permanently remove rental units from the market via small-landlord exit, while an incentive-based approach can only fail to add them. PRO repeatedly treated this as a mere possibility demanding quantification rather than engaging the directional imbalance, which left CON’s burden-of-proof argument live at the end. Although CON’s opening was clipped—an execution cost—later speeches recovered and sharpened the clash. PRO’s moral framing against categorical exclusion was competent, but its failure to neutralize the “who decides” and supply-risk lines gave CON the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second-rebuttal answer A2 ([A4.3]), which introduced the supply asymmetry that mandates risk irreversible unit loss while incentives merely fail to add units. PRO never answered this beyond demanding unquantified proof, leaving the point live and ultimately decisive.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): PRO kept the proposition narrow - rent-covered, otherwise qualified, verification allowed - and won the comparison: bounded paperwork/timing vs categorical loss of housing. CON's best path, third-party-contract risk and permanent-exit asymmetry, was neutralized when PRO showed wage tenants also lack guarantees, rent caps only matter if rent isn't covered, and an "available" unit that bans vouchers is not actually available to voucher holders. CON also incurred execution cost with clipped Opening and repetition of the contradiction charge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R1/R2 handling of rent caps and timing - if rent is covered the cap in other deals is irrelevant, and verified subsidy plus documented inability is still a legitimate screen; ordinary uncertainty must be accommodated. That turned CON's core transactional-difference claim into a manageable burden rather than a justification for a blanket veto, and set up the effective supply counter.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by maintaining a tighter, more resilient core argument. A successfully framed the proposition as a ban on categorical exclusion rather than a ban on individualized screening, which blunted B's "transactional risk" and "bundle" arguments. While B introduced a rhetorically strong "asymmetry" argument late in the debate (claiming mandates risk permanent supply loss while incentives do not), A decisively countered it in the closing by pointing out that B treats the status quo harm of categorical exclusion as costless. B also suffered an execution cost with a clipped opening speech. A's rebuttals were sharper, answered pressure questions more directly, and controlled the central clash throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing response to Side B's "asymmetry" argument, pointing out that an existing home categorically excluding voucher holders is already unavailable to those households, exposing the flaw in treating the status quo exclusion as a costless baseline.
