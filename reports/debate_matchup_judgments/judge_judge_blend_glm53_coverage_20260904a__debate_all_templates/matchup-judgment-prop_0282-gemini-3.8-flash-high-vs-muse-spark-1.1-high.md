### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A built an emotionally forceful case anchored almost entirely on the Dahmer example, but its burden was "routinely" exploits, and when B's pressure question directly asked for evidence that most commercial true-crime is made over family objection rather than invitation, A's reply (A4.1) just re-asserted "structural reality" without new support—leaving the breadth claim unproven. B exploited this gap and also caught A's key strategic move: redefining "exploitation" to mean any dramatization without prior consultation, which B correctly showed collapses A's own distinction between legitimate journalism and predatory entertainment (B4.3, "turns journalism inside out"). B's mechanism critique—that mandatory notice functionally becomes a veto when family members disagree, abandoning the relative who wants publicity—was raised twice and never squarely resolved by A beyond citing a next-of-kin hierarchy that doesn't settle intra-family splits (e.g., two children disagreeing). A's rebuttals were competent but largely reasserted the same framing rather than closing these specific holes. B's opening was clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost, but B's later speeches were tightly argued, directly engaged A's strongest points, and ended with the cleaner comparative frame: existing harassment/defamation law versus a vague, chilling "sensationalizing" standard.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.3), showing A's own definition of "exploitation as any dramatization without consultation" turns ordinary public-interest journalism into exploitation by A's own logic, directly undercutting A's attempt to distinguish "legitimate journalism" from entertainment.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): B won by better handling the motion's decisive burdens: whether exploitation is routine enough, and whether stronger rules/constraints are justified once family agency and speech tradeoffs are weighed. A built a forceful moral frame around commodification and externalized harm, and scored with the Dahmer-profit critique of self-correction, but leaned too hard on an outlier and a journalism/entertainment split that B collapsed by noting cold-case and wrongful-conviction work is itself commercial entertainment. B repeatedly showed that "notice" and anti-sensationalizing duties become practical chill/vetoes when families disagree, and that consent/invitation plus targeted harassment law better fit the live clash. A kept reasserting "ethics or law" and "notice is not a veto," but never neutralized B's workability and agency counterweights. Clipped opening for B was a minor execution cost, not decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's collapse of A's journalism-vs-entertainment split (B2.1-B2.2) plus the notice-as-practical-veto weighing (B2.5-B2.6 / B4.3-B4.4), which forced A's later speeches into definitional expansion rather than a cleaner remedy defense.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by effectively leveraging the "ethical rules or legal constraints" disjunction in the motion to neutralize Side B's prior restraint and veto arguments. Side A maintained a sharper distinction between commercial entertainment and public-interest journalism, and successfully argued that market self-correction (via PR protocols after massive profits) does not undo the routine exploitation of unconsenting families. Side B's opening was clipped, and its defense of market correction conceded the initial financial windfall of exploitation, weakening its core claim that the market naturally deters the behavior.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's "notice equals veto" argument by pointing to standard legal definitions of next-of-kin and emphasizing that enforceable industry ethical codes do not constitute prior restraint, cleanly escaping Side B's First Amendment trap.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A built a more complete case: concrete examples, a structural economic mechanism (dead have no life-rights, so grief is free content) that B never directly rebutted, and a narrowed conduct-based test (identifiable victim + graphic intimate recreation + scripted profit) that tried to answer the censorship worry rather than dodge it. Crucially, A's pressure question forced B to admit that "tasteless, callous dramatizations certainly exist" but must be tolerated as free speech — a concession A exploited devastatingly in closing, framing B as accepting the premise of routine exploitation while merely disputing the remedy. B's case stayed strong on principle (public record, prior restraint, "who decides" vagueness) but never engaged A's market-failure evidence (Dahmer's billion viewed hours despite family objections) or the economic structural claim, and B's closing largely restated earlier censorship warnings rather than resolving the concession or the unanswered economic point. A also handled the disjunctive nature of the motion (ethical rules OR legal constraints) better by offering softer non-legal remedies (consent efforts, victim funds) that blunt B's censorship attack, while B's rebuttal focused almost entirely on legal/state-censorship dangers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Pressure Question forcing B to concede that "tasteless, callous dramatizations certainly exist" but that "moral disgust does not justify legal censorship" — A then used this admission in closing to argue B had conceded the factual premise of the motion while only disputing the remedy, a move B's closing never fully neutralized.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the motion narrow: routine structural exploitation of identifiable victims plus workable constraints on scripted graphic reenactment, not a ban on public facts or investigation. A repeatedly forced the journalism/entertainment distinction, clarified that remedies leave news and trials free, and used B’s concession that callous dramatizations exist as proof that stronger rules are warranted. B mounted a serious prior-restraint and line-drawing case (Thin Blue Line; narrative needed for scrutiny) but overweighted absolutism and repeatedly treated A’s limited conduct rule as an editorial veto over history, which A had already denied. After full exchange, A’s comparative path remained cleaner and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s Rebuttal 2 answers (A4.1–A4.3): objective conduct test + no veto on open-court facts, only on graphic scripted humiliation; neutralized B’s Thin Blue Line/prior-restraint pressure while preserving the live route.

While Side B relied heavily on First Amendment slippery-slope arguments and false equivalencies (equating Netflix dramatizations with documentaries like The Thin Blue Line), Side A effectively neutralized these attacks. In Rebuttal 2, Side A provided a precise, objective three-part test for restricted conduct and clarified that proposed consent rules apply only to the simulated humiliation of graphic violence, not to the reporting of public facts. Side A's grounding was exceptional, using strong legal analogies (autopsy photo restrictions, rape-shield laws) to prove that limiting exploitative dramatization is consistent with existing legal traditions. Side B was rhetorically polished but ultimately conceded the existence of "tasteless, callous" exploitation while failing to overcome Side A's targeted, conduct-based remedies.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they cleanly answer the pressure questions by establishing a narrow, objective three-part test for restricted conduct (identifiable victim, graphic intimate recreation, scripted profit) and clarifying that the family veto applies only to simulated humiliation, not public facts, effectively neutralizing CON's prior restraint and slippery slope arguments.
