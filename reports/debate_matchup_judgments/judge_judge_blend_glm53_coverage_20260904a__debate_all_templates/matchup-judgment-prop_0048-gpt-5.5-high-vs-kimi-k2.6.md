### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a clear but competitive debate for three reasons. First, the "unsafe open bed is not real access to safe hospital care" reframe genuinely turns CON's best argument. CON insisted closed beds are the harm of a mandate; PRO responded that a bed staffed below safe levels is also not real access — it is "access to risk wearing a hospital bracelet." CON never escapes this comparison; its closing merely repeats the access objection without squarely answering whether an overloaded nurse is a better outcome than a diverted patient. Second, PRO's timing argument — outcome enforcement arrives after the fall, after the missed deterioration — holds up throughout. CON's A4.1 alternative (regulators reviewing acuity-based plans before the shift) is the most substantive response and is actually creative, but it quietly concedes that proactive intervention is necessary, which is PRO's core premise; and CON never explains why a regulator reviewing a plan without a hard minimum would be more resistant to budget pressure than the legal minimum itself. The "who says no?" question in PRO's closing remains the sharpest unanswered pressure in the debate. Third, PRO's safety-code analogy (restaurants, fire exits) is never substantively rebutted; CON only contends that ratios are too rigid, not that the category of safety-code regulation is inapplicable to hospitals. CON's best live argument — the floor-to-ceiling contradiction (PRO calls it a floor but insists it must be a statute because guidelines lack force) — is a genuinely sharp pressure question and a real tension, but PRO's response (proper law design with acuity tiers, continued liability above the floor) is adequate. The margin stays at 1.0 because CON's shortage argument has real force, its detailed alternative in the second rebuttal is thoughtful, and the debate never becomes a rout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive exchange is PRO's comparative frame in B2.5 — "CON treats closed beds as the harm; PRO identifies unsafe open beds as the hidden harm" — combined with CON's failure across subsequent rounds to establish that an overloaded-nurse scenario is safer than diversion. CON never successfully neutralizes the distinction between nominal access and safe access, which is ultimately the hinge of the whole debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A won by effectively exposing the flaw in Side B's outcome-based accountability model (it only punishes after harm occurs) and cleanly neutralizing the 'access to care' argument by framing unsafe open beds as a hidden harm rather than genuine access.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 takedown of the access argument, defining an unstaffed bed not as real access but as a 'sign on the door masking a hollow service' and 'access to risk wearing a hospital bracelet.'
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): A wins by establishing a clear, enforceable pre-harm baseline that directly counters incentive misalignment and invisible risk, while answering pressure questions with concrete mechanisms for exceptions and statutory force. B highlights real risks of rigidity and access loss but fails to supply a comparably enforceable pre-harm alternative, leaving its professional-oversight model vulnerable to the same failures it attributes to current practice. Clash sharpened cleanly in later rounds and closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 opening with labeled A1/A2 responses to B's pressure questions, which directly reinforced the floor concept and auditing, then carried that comparison into the closing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the cleaner comparative path across the debate. Its central logic—that administrators cannot be trusted to voluntarily protect staffing, so an enforceable red line is necessary—held up better than CON's alternative of acuity-based plans and nurse committees. PRO's most effective pressure was exposing the trust paradox: if administrators are too self-interested to exceed a simple statutory floor, they are even less trustworthy with the far more complex and gameable discretionary systems CON proposes. "A political line can be amended; a manipulated acuity score is buried in a chart" was the debate's sharpest weighing line and remained unanswered. PRO also correctly identified that CON's alternative still requires trusting managers to design, fund, and comply—neutralizing CON's main attack on administrator good faith. CON landed real damage with the bed-closure concession: PRO embraced it honestly, but CON's "visibility is not treatment" response carried genuine weight, and the point that a patient deteriorating in an ED queue is not saved by a compliant ward ratio was never fully resolved. However, PRO reframed bed closures credibly as a transparency mechanism forcing systemic investment rather than hiding harm. CON's argument that ratios measure the wrong variable was intellectually strong but was weakened by the repeated clipping penalty (three truncated turns) and by the logical gap in its own alternative: the acuity-based framework also depends on administrators, yet the proposal assumed enforcement mechanisms that would require just as much, if not more, institutional good faith. PRO's rebuttals were consistently sharper, its format compliance was better (clear A1/A2 in Rebuttal 2 vs. CON's embedded labels), and its closing crystallized the live clash cleanly without late evidence dumps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2, particularly the answer to CON's Q1: PRO directly exposed that CON's entire alternative framework—acuity tools, nurse committees, audits—still runs through the same administrators whose bad faith is the core premise of the debate. The line "flexibility for administrators, danger for patients" crystallized why CON's complexity-based alternative does not escape the trust problem it claims to solve. This was the exchange that most decisively damaged CON's live route to victory.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully reframing Side A's strongest concession—that rigid ratios force bed closures during shortages—as an active harm to patients. While Side A argued this transparency forces systemic accountability, Side B persuasively countered that "visibility is not treatment," showing how ratios merely shift danger from the ward to the emergency room queue.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's attack on Side A's willingness to accept bed closures, culminating in the observation that "a patient deteriorating in an emergency queue is not saved by the fact that the ward's ratio looks compliant."
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by establishing an unanswerable incentive problem with administrator discretion, using the California example and pilot/speed-limit analogies to show a statutory floor as a non-negotiable backstop; Side B's rigidity and access concerns were addressed directly in rebuttals by reframing bed closures as visibility and accountability that forces hiring, exposing that B's alternatives still rely on the same untrusted managers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answers to B's pressure questions on bed closures and number validity, which turned the "rationing" concession into a feature by arguing it exposes hidden dangers in the status quo.
