### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won the decisive mechanism clash: a parity rule can forbid a high-margin shape but cannot summon cars if operators shrink, delay, or skip. PRO’s best attack—vouchers presuppose presence—was answered by margin-raising procurement and real ridehail contracting; when pressed, PRO’s citywide-denominator and expiring-waiver replies walked into CON’s predicted fork and undercut the durability contrast. Early clipped CON turns cost polish, but later weighing was cleaner and easier to vote on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON A4.5–A4.7: turned PRO’s “jurisdiction is the denominator” answer into proof of the everywhere-or-shrink fork and reframed graduated waivers as unfunded discretion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built the stronger comparative case by showing that PRO's parity mandate would deter entry and expansion—either functioning as a jurisdiction-wide obligation that operators avoid entirely or imposing costly low-density requirements at every margin—while offering no enforcement mechanism that doesn't harm the intended beneficiaries or collapse into toothless waivers. CON's alternative of direct subsidies and procurement contracts provided a more credible, competition-driven path to actual vehicle presence, and CON successfully turned PRO's license-condition framing against it by demonstrating that a license can forbid a service shape but cannot conjure a car onto a block where none is profitable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON in Rebuttal 2 [A4.5]-[A4.6] turning PRO's answer to Q1—that parity is measured against the whole jurisdiction—into a concession that the mandate effectively forces jurisdiction-wide service, and exposing PRO's "graduated remedies" as unfunded discretionary administration.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): PRO kept a live path that CON never fully neutralized: privileged citywide permit justifies a coverage floor, and market geometry predicts clustering without it. PRO's strongest turn [A2.3] – vouchers subsidize price not presence – forced CON to answer why ridehail hasn't blanketed deserts. CON's answer [B4.1] – nobody paid – helped but conceded market won't go without payment, while its two clipped turns cost execution. CON's geometry/fork pressure [B2.2][B3.1][B4.5] was sharp and A partly walked into whole-jurisdiction measurement, but A's phase-in + graduated remedies + durability-on-return [A4.3][A5.5] answered the immediate “serve everywhere at once” charge better than CON answered the durability question. Weighing who must act, license floor vs perpetual appropriation, PRO gave the cleaner reason to vote for the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Exchange on [A2.3] vouchers buy nothing if no car comes vs [B2.3] subsidy raises revenue so presence follows margin vs [B4.1] where CON says agencies that paid did get cars – decisive for whether mandate or payment creates durable presence.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): CON built the cleaner decisive path: the missing monopoly wallet, hidden fares on the same low-income riders, litigation/gaming risk of any "similar" threshold, and early-stage rollout throttle. PRO's public-franchise and durability frames were strong but never fully neutralized the cost-bearer and transparency comparisons after CON forced the profitable-core admission and showed both instruments are political. Clipping on PRO turns and the late metric specifics slightly diluted its force.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2 price-machine turn: per-trip credits rewire the exact routing algorithm PRO feared, converting a supposed supply-only problem into a priced incentive without freezing network economics.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won by making durability the decisive clash. CON conceded that under its subsidy model, if funding is trimmed, operators have no legal duty to serve low-income neighborhoods. PRO turned this into a powerful "default coverage versus default abandonment" frame, showing that a permit condition survives political inaction while a line item dies from it. CON's counter that permits can also be waived was blunted by PRO's asymmetry argument—waivers require affirmative action, while appropriations lapse through silence. CON's fleet-shrinkage and hidden-tax arguments were plausible but remained speculative, and PRO successfully identified the wallet while rebutting the litigation fear with concrete, administratively familiar benchmarks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question Q1 and CON's concession in Rebuttal 2 that "the operator has no obligation — correct" if the credit is trimmed, which PRO then weaponized in Closing to frame the choice as default coverage versus default abandonment.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 4.8 (high) (CON)): PRO won the instrument comparison by answering vagueness with a concrete 1.5x median / ~20min 90th percentile permit benchmark and by exposing CON's fatal concession that with no appropriation 'the operator has no obligation.' The durability asymmetry — silence kills a subsidy but preserves a permit condition — gave a clean risk-allocation reason to prefer required reliability over leaving low-income service contingent on annual budgets, despite CON's strong price-machine and hidden-tax counters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4 naming explicit reliability thresholds and adjudication by the permitting agency directly defused CON's litigation vagueness attack from B3, while CON's B2 reframing of subsidies as supply through price was its sharpest hit but was undercut by CON's own admission in B4.1 that coverage disappears when funding does.
