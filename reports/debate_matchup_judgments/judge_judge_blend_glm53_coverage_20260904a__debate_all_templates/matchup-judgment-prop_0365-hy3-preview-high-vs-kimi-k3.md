### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): PRO opened strongly with a concrete access story and cost figures, but its case never fully escaped the "manufactured demand" problem: CON's point that contestable, razor-margin exhibition markets would arbitrage away any unclaimed standard-price crowd went largely unanswered beyond assertion. CON's cross-subsidy/price-discrimination framework (airline analogy, premium slots funding cheap matinees) was pressed effectively and, crucially, CON's pressure question forced PRO to concretize its remedy (a specific threshold and a 1:2 slot ratio). That concretization backfired: CON's Rebuttal 2 showed the arithmetic contradiction—PRO's mandate cannot both convert premium slots to standard price and "preserve all PLF upcharge revenue," since the revenue lives in those very slots. This "moot or harmful" fork (either the trigger never fires, or it fires and bleeds the cross-subsidy) was never squarely rebutted by PRO, whose closing retreated to reasserting accessibility without engaging the arithmetic, and even inadvertently conceded that revealed demand alone would prompt theaters to add slots—undercutting the need for a mandate. CON's closing crystallized this decisively. CON's questions were also sharper and more damaging than PRO's, and CON's answers to PRO's questions were direct and complete.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 exposing that PRO's remedy cannot simultaneously convert PLF slots to standard price and "preserve all PLF upcharge revenue," crystallized as the "moot or harmful" fork that PRO never escaped.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B won the core economic clash. PRO's accessibility harm was real but CON showed revealed demand, contestable scheduling, and time-based pricing, then locked in the moot-or-harmful fork: either standard prime shows already exist all week so rule never fires, or converting sold-out premium slots destroys the cross-subsidy that funds matinees. A's R2 threshold answer contradicted itself by claiming to preserve all PLF revenue while mandating conversion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3-B4.4 arithmetic fork — converting a $20 Dolby 7 PM show to $12 cannot preserve premium revenue, so either the zero-standard-prime trigger is phantom or it bleeds the subsidy that funds the affordable shows PRO defends.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A won by establishing a decisive "moot or harmful" fork regarding the proposition's scheduling mandate. Side A effectively demonstrated that forcing theaters to convert prime-time PLF slots to standard pricing would either be moot (as standard slots already exist at other times) or harmful (destroying the premium cross-subsidy while simply creating a standard slot that sells out instantly to fast clickers). Side B struggled to defend the arithmetic of their mandate, falsely claiming it would preserve PLF revenue while simultaneously requiring the conversion of premium slots, and failed to adequately answer Side A's pressure regarding the "fastest clicker" problem.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side B's claim that the mandate "preserves all PLF upcharge revenue" by pointing out the arithmetic impossibility of keeping the upcharge when converting a sold-out premium slot to a standard one, cementing the "moot or harmful" trap.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): A built a clear causal mechanism (front-loaded demand plus scarce prime standard slots equals rationing that never registers as lost demand) and then, crucially, used the Pressure Questions round to expose a genuine internal tension in B's case: B's "self-correction happens fast" claim and B's "capping PLF causes a closure death spiral" claim cannot both be true without contradiction. B's answer (distinguishing "tactical" auditorium shifts from "systemic caps") was a plausible but under-explained dodge that A exploited effectively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, arguing that if tactical rebalancing already achieves the motion's floor, enacting that floor causes no new death spiral. B never cleanly resolved this, instead repeating the survival/closure framing without engaging why the same "tactical" adjustments it praises wouldn't satisfy the motion's requirement. A also handled the "define too few" pressure reasonably by analogizing to ordinary legal/policy standards, while B's demand for numeric precision was somewhat answered but not decisively turned into a knockout. Overall A progressed the debate more, sharpened the central contradiction, and closed with a cleaner, more resolved narrative, while B's closing largely restated prior points without directly dismantling A's contradiction framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.4-A4.6), which turned B's own concession about "tactical" schedule shifts into proof that the motion's requirement is already achievable, thereby exposing that B's self-correction and closure-risk arguments undercut each other.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO wins on the conditional reading: CON concedes a brief opening-weekend squeeze, which is exactly the motion's trigger, and then relies on two incompatible pillars - market self-correction by adding standard slots and survival requiring never pulling premium. A exposed that adding a standard slot at fixed capacity must pull a premium one, making the self-correction B touts impossible under its own death-spiral logic. B's tactical vs systemic reply did not resolve that, leaving PRO's front-loaded, invisible lost-customer account as the cleaner path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Pressure Q1 and Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 where A shows B cannot both claim theaters easily add standard slots by Saturday and claim any cap on PLF triggers closures - adding a standard prime slot must displace a premium one, so self-correction is blocked by B's own survival argument.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B wins by decisively exposing a fatal contradiction in Side A's case. Side A argued simultaneously that theaters naturally self-correct by shifting premium screens to standard when demand dictates, and that forcing theaters to limit premium screens would cause a financial death spiral. Side B effectively trapped Side A: if shifting screens is easy and profitable, a mandate to do so wouldn't cause closures; if premium screens are truly untouchable for survival, the natural self-correction Side A relies on couldn't exist. Side B also handled the pressure questions excellently, turning Side A's "sell-out" signal argument into proof of rationing that harms the casual viewer. Side A's distinction between tactical shifts and systemic caps was unconvincing, leaving Side B with the cleaner, more logically cohesive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it crystallized the contradiction between Side A's "self-correction" mechanism and its "closure/death spiral" warning, effectively neutralizing Side A's two main pillars.
