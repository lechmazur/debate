### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): CON built a sharper, more structurally damaging case around a single asymmetry: cross-bans strip the most from players with the least to lose value (legitimate libraries, reputations) while barely touching the target population (serial cheaters/harassers who already churn disposable accounts, spoofed hardware, and VPNs). This point was introduced early, repeated with increasing precision, and never squarely neutralized by PRO. When pressed (Q1/B3.1) on who verifies cross-bans and what independent appeal exists, PRO's answer ("appeals handled by the platform that holds the original evidence") effectively conceded CON's core worry — the same company that convicts also judges the appeal, with no neutral reviewer. CON exploited this concession directly in Rebuttal 2, turning PRO's own answer into evidence for its "judge, jury, executioner" framing. PRO's responses relied heavily on restating the "99% vs 1%" framing and asserting that raising costs "prices out" abusers, without engaging the mechanical reason CON gave for why disposable-identity abusers specifically evade that cost while ordinary players absorb it. PRO's rebuttal to "exit as due process" (calling it "surrender") was rhetorically forceful but did not answer CON's sharper point that exit is the only escape hatch for falsely flagged players, and that cross-bans eliminate exactly that option for the people cross-bans wrongly catch. Both sides argued the motion as written and accepted the due-process tradeoff framing, but CON did more work showing why that tradeoff fails on its own terms, while PRO mostly reasserted that the tradeoff was necessary without resolving the asymmetry or appeal-neutrality problems raised against it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4.2-B4.3), which used PRO's own pressure-question answer about appeals being handled by "the platform that holds the original evidence" to argue PRO had conceded a judge-jury-executioner enforcement model, directly undercutting PRO's deterrence and due-process-tradeoff claims.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by showing that cross-ecosystem punishment creates an asymmetric burden: disposable-account abusers can evade it, while invested innocent players bear catastrophic errors without independent review. Side B established real protective and deterrent benefits, but repeatedly asserted that false positives were rare and that added friction would work without adequately grounding either comparison. It also continued to mischaracterize A’s exit argument after A clarified that local bans protect victims while ecosystem exit is recourse against failed moderation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both pressure questions directly, distinguished local removal from victim flight, and turned PRO’s deterrence mechanism by asking whether the added friction actually falls on serial abusers or on legitimate players contesting secret evidence.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A wins by decisively exposing the asymmetry of cross-ecosystem bans. A effectively argues that serious abusers use disposable identities and hardware spoofers, meaning the real cost of cross-bans falls on ordinary players who lose years of paid libraries to unappealable false positives. A also brilliantly clarifies that "exit" is not about victims fleeing abusers (since local bans already remove the abuser from the specific game), but about the falsely accused escaping a broken or biased moderation system. Side B's reliance on the original platform handling its own appeals and the claim that cross-bans "exhaust" dedicated trolls failed to overcome A's targeted, logically cohesive rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's clarification in Rebuttal 2 that local bans already protect victims, making "exit" the necessary due process for the falsely accused, completely neutralizing Side B's pressure question about forcing victims to flee.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases and engaged the motion's explicit due-process tradeoff directly, making this a genuinely close debate. PRO's strongest move was narrowing the proposal in Rebuttal 2 to a reversible, issuer-owned, multiplayer-only hold, which neutralized much of CON's "contagion" framing and exposed a real tension in CON's stance (demanding human review while also insisting even high-confidence, human-verified bans should never be shared). But CON's pressure question about Xbox/PSN account-level lockouts forced exactly this concession, and CON's follow-up—that platform authentication architecture ties single-player library access to overarching network bans, not matchmaking alone—directly undercut PRO's "contained to multiplayer" mitigation. PRO's closing reasserted the narrow scope but never rebutted the authentication point, leaving CON's most damaging rebuttal unanswered at the moment it mattered most. CON also used its last-speech position effectively to reinforce this unresolved technical objection alongside the property-rights framing. PRO's case remains emotionally and causally strong (repeat offenders, victim harm, banking/doping analogies), but its core due-process mitigation strategy was the decisive battleground, and CON's practical rebuttal there went unrefuted, giving CON the cleaner path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's B4.5/B5.2 point that PSN/Xbox-style account authentication severs whole-library access, not just multiplayer, directly undermined PRO's A4.2 attempt to scope shared bans to "the multiplayer commons only"—and PRO never answered this in closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending a narrow, high-confidence, multiplayer-focused system rather than the permanent industry-wide account deletion Side A repeatedly attacked. B directly weighed reversible false-positive harm against repeated victimization caused by easy platform hopping. Side A raised legitimate concerns about automation, appeals, and corporate coordination, but often assumed that shared enforcement must revoke entire digital libraries and did not establish why targeted restrictions were impossible. Its answer that each new platform should simply improve security also failed to address identity evasion and relocation of proven offenders.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal was decisive: it assigned appeal responsibility to the issuing company, limited derivative enforcement to multiplayer access, and distinguished local automated sanctions from shared human-reviewed serious findings. Side A challenged whether companies would implement that model well, but largely returned to the broader library-seizure scenario B had expressly rejected.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins because it successfully navigates the "even if" clause of the motion by providing a strong comparative weighing: the certain, compounding harm of predators and cheaters recycling victims across ecosystems outweighs the due-process risks of false positives, especially when limited to high-confidence, severe infractions. Side A lands a very sharp practical rebuttal regarding console DRM (showing that a platform-level ban often severs single-player library access), which damages B's attempt to narrow the collateral damage. However, A's alternative—relying on individual platforms to catch cross-ecosystem hoppers independently—fails to solve the core problem of predators evading consequences, effectively biting the bullet on victim recycling. B's rhetoric ("containment is complicity," "cheating stops being a rental fee") and cleaner impact calculus secure the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 point [A4.5] exposing that console ecosystem bans (like PSN/Xbox Live) sever overarching account authentication, effectively destroying the single-player library despite PRO's attempt to narrow the ban to the "multiplayer commons."
