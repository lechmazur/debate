### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): PRO won a close debate by keeping the decisive comparison clearer: lower cost floors are not the same as lower consumer prices, and lost hard-to-replace rivalry is harder to rebuild than forgone non-merger-exclusive scale. CON’s scale/exit story was vivid and sticky, and it leveraged PRO’s “nothing guarantees” line hard, but it overclaimed that as a knockout and never fully resolved the tension PRO exposed between formidable scale barriers and reliable post-merger discipline. PRO’s answers under pressure were more precise, its merger-specific-efficiencies nuance better fit the proposition’s weighing, and its closings crystallized ability versus incentive without late dump or evasion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO Rebuttal 2 (A4.1/A4.5): answers CON’s subscale trap while turning CON’s own scale-barrier claim against its entry/pass-through story.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by maintaining the decisive distinction between a lower cost floor and a lower consumer price, showing that eliminating a close rival weakens the competitive pressure needed to pass savings through. A also exposed a damaging, unresolved tension in B's case: if scale barriers are so high that independent firms cannot survive, then entry cannot credibly discipline merged firms. B overread a supposed concession from A and failed to show that remaining scaled rivals fully replace the competitive constraint of the acquired entity. A's closing crystallized the clash cleanly, while B's closing relied on a mischaracterization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A4.5 observation that CON cannot simultaneously claim scale barriers doom independent firms and that entry will discipline merged firms; this tension was never resolved and materially weakened CON's core tradeoff.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A won by grounding the debate in the concrete realities of cost floors and scale economies, effectively arguing that preserving subscale, high-cost rivals does not protect consumers because they literally cannot afford to undercut a scaled operator. Side A brilliantly weaponized Side B's concession in Rebuttal 2 ("Nothing guarantees that two genuinely subscale firms will underprice an integrated firm") to collapse Side B's core argument that preserving the rival maintains a pricing constraint. Side A's crystallization of the clash as a choice between a "certain ability to charge less" and a "theory about willingness to charge more" was rhetorically devastating and decisively resolved the weighing mechanism in its favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it capitalized on Side B's admission about subscale firms to prove that preserving high-cost rivals fails to lower consumer prices, while contrasting the concrete savings of scale against speculative theories of margin expansion.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the motion’s real burden live: stricter blocking of additional deals must beat selective review on net prices/innovation versus lost productivity and scale. A’s openings and closings were vivid on airlines, hospitals, shelving, and pass-through, but often proved that some mergers are harmful—cases B also would block—rather than showing extra prohibitions improve the tradeoff. B consistently separated double-marginalization/commercialization benefits from foreclosure risks, refused to treat contracts and internal build as free substitutes, and correctly rejected A’s move to count only efficiencies passed through as a goalpost shift against the motion’s productivity language. A’s filter answer (4-to-3/ability-incentive) narrowed the dispute but never converted rank/presumption into a net-benefit case once B showed remaining rivalry and capability combinations still matter at the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 (esp. B4.1–B4.2/B4.6): pass-through failure and competitor-pipeline shelving justify selective blocks, not stricter presumptions, and “temporary paperwork” erases permanent lost financing/development windows.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B wins because Side A never proved that the additional mergers blocked by stricter enforcement would be net harmful rather than efficiency-creating. Side B successfully exposed that Side A treated competitive harm as certain while dismissing productivity and scale losses that the motion explicitly requires weighing. Side B’s argument that 4-to-3 concentration is a warning sign rather than proof of net harm, and that mistaken blocking can permanently destroy innovation windows just as mistaken clearance destroys rivals, left Side A’s comparative burden unmet after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Rebuttal 2 argument that the motion explicitly counts lost productivity and economies of scale, so Side A cannot dismiss merger benefits merely because they do not immediately pass through to consumers as lower prices ([B4.4]–[B4.5]).
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully framing the debate around the marginal deals caught by stricter enforcement and exposing Side B's overly optimistic assumptions about frictionless alternatives like contracts, internal growth, and non-rival buyers. Side A also correctly held Side B to the motion's explicit text regarding "productivity and economies of scale," preventing B from dismissing unrealized efficiencies. Side A's answers to the pressure questions were highly nuanced and effectively weaponized in later speeches to dismantle Side B's claimed asymmetry between permanent merger harms and temporary blocking harms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's assumption that blocking harms are merely "temporary delays" while merger harms are permanent, pointing out that a blocked startup might fail to secure alternative funding and never reach the market, thus collapsing Side B's asymmetry argument.
