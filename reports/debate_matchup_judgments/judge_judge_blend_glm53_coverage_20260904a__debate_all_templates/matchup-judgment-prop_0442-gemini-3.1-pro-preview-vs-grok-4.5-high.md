### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by sustaining the stronger coordination and enforcement case: a future-dated global mandate creates investable demand and counters the inertia and flag-shopping that voluntary measures permit. Side A effectively distinguished managed carbon pricing from unmanaged freight shocks and exposed distributional harms, but never established that a sensibly phased timetable must outrun infrastructure or why its alternatives would provide equal certainty and enforcement. B therefore retained the cleaner route from binding policy to actual decarbonization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s answer that a timetable can phase in over years directly weakened A’s assumption that a mandate necessarily creates immediate scarcity. A continued to posit infrastructure lag, but did not show that such lag is unavoidable under an IMO-set schedule designed to induce investment before compliance dates.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (A) kept the scarcity/physical-supply and regressive bulk-cost routes alive while answering PRO's key pressure. PRO's chicken-and-egg certainty thesis was damaged by A's correct point that a fuel-use mandate (not an outcome) excludes wind-assist/efficiency [A2.5-A2.6], and PRO never resolved that mandating demand cannot conjure renewable electricity and bunkering. A's differentiation of gradual carbon pricing with revenue recycling for essentials blunted B's strongest contradiction punch from [B3.1], leaving CON with the clearer comparative path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.5-A2.6 / A4.1 – A shows the resolution requires fuel use, so PRO's claim that wind/efficiency can still comply is false, proving the mandate picks winners and crowds out safer, cheaper cuts.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A won by sharply distinguishing between the physical bottlenecks of green fuel production (renewable energy capacity, port infrastructure) and mere regulatory demand, effectively arguing that a rigid fuel mandate manufactures scarcity rather than solving the chicken-and-egg problem. Side A also successfully defended a superior alternative (technology-neutral carbon pricing with revenue recycling) that internalizes costs without locking in immature fuels or causing unmanageable shocks. Side B relied too heavily on the mantra that "certainty unlocks capital" without adequately answering the physical supply constraints, and conflated the binding nature of IMO enforcement with the specific choice of a fuel mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's dismantling of the "pennies" argument by distinguishing between containerized consumer goods and bulk shipping (food, fertilizer), followed by the defense of revenue recycling to protect the Global South from carbon pricing shocks.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by establishing that a phased global mandate solves the free-rider and investment-certainty problems that voluntary supply-side support leaves intact. Side A raised serious concerns about bulk freight, fuel availability, and ammonia safety, but repeatedly escalated them into guaranteed starvation, quadrupled costs, and total breakdown without adequately grounding those magnitudes. Side B also correctly emphasized that a timetable can phase targets while inducing fuel, port, and safety investment in parallel, undercutting A’s portrayal of an immediate inflexible switch. B’s incorrect C-reference was an execution flaw, but not decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s A1/A2 response was decisive: it answered the claim that mandates cannot create physical infrastructure by explaining how phased deadlines create bankable demand and coordinated investment, while treating safety systems as part of the required build-out. Side A identified genuine bottlenecks but did not prove they necessarily become the catastrophic shortages its case required.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO won the central coordination clash. CON's strongest live routes were immediate food-security pain from bulk freight and absolute physical bottlenecks for green fuel and ammonia safety. PRO answered with phased targets turning future scarcity into bankable offtake that funds grids and codes, LNG precedent, and markets having absorbed bunker spikes. CON never solved free-riding: phased levies for R&D still leave residual oil cheaper forever, and its A1 dodged the 3% fractional-sector slippery slope exposed in B's pressure questions, while B gave clean A1/A2.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1-B4.3 turning CON's "fuel does not exist" and "physical limits cannot be legislated" into investment barriers that only a binding timetable makes bankable, plus A2 on toxic-cargo codes already handling similar hazards.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins because it successfully defended the core necessity of a binding timetable to solve the free-rider problem, exposing the weakness of Side A's supply-side alternatives. While Side A made a compelling case regarding physical bottlenecks and the immediate humanitarian risks to bulk shipping, Side B effectively weighed manageable, planned freight rises against permanent, unplanned climate shocks. Side B also capitalized on Side A's strategic misstep of dismissing shipping's 3% emissions as "fractional," providing a cleaner comparative path to victory in the final crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal to the Global South food security argument, effectively contrasting phased, predictable freight cost increases with the compounding and unplanned harvest failures caused by unchecked climate change.
