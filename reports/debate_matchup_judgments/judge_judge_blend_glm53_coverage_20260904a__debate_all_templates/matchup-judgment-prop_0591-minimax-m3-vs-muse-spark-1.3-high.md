### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Both sides built genuinely strong, well-adapted cases, but PRO's mechanism survived scrutiny more intact. CON's strongest weapon—that transparency hands smugglers a "how-to-evade manual"—was persuasive against individual case-by-case explanations, but PRO successfully carved out a distinction (individual explanations stay confidential to claimant/counsel; only aggregate, weight-free audit findings are disclosed) that CON never fully dismantled. CON's counter that pooled confidential letters still leak via smuggler debriefing was clever but speculative, and PRO's closing jiu-jitsu ("if smugglers already extract this via debriefing, the marginal disclosure cost is small while the marginal benefit of catching systemic bias is large") landed hard; CON's rebuttal ("state letters are ground truth, not guesses") answered it but didn't fully restore the original force of the security argument against the audit-body mechanism specifically. Meanwhile, CON's internal-review answer to the pressure question ("supervisors, inspectors, judges track patterns nationally") was asserted rather than defended against PRO's sharp charge that this is structurally identical to the status quo that already produces undetected bias — a real, mostly unanswered gap. CON did land a strong, under-addressed point of its own (that no sovereign state will actually cede audit control, making the "floor" hollow), which PRO's closing never returned to — but on balance PRO's core due-process/bias-detection logic proved more resilient across the exchange, giving it the cleaner path to the decision.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing move turning CON's "smugglers already debrief" concession into a cost-benefit asymmetry (marginal disclosure risk vs. marginal bias-detection benefit), and CON's partial but incomplete comeback distinguishing "ground truth" state letters from smuggler guesses, was the most decisive exchange of the debate.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): PRO edged a high-quality debate by forcing the live clash onto whether internal human review can detect and contest systemic algorithmic error. CON’s adversarial-filter and aggregation attacks were strong and sustained, but PRO’s pressure on unfalsifiable rubber-stamping, plus the closing that proving one story ≠ proving the system misweighted dialect/nationality, left CON’s preferred safeguard looking structurally incomplete. Cost asymmetry (wrongful return vs. coaching friction) and the floor-not-ceiling sovereignty answer gave the cleaner residual reason to affirm the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure + closing: internal “second look” cannot see cross-case over-flagging, so factual re-interview does not let a wrongly rejected refugee prove algorithmic error.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won by decisively distinguishing between individual factual review and systemic algorithmic auditing. While Side B mounted a strong, highly readable defense regarding adversarial evasion and the practical limits of transparency, Side A effectively demonstrated that an individual officer conducting a re-interview cannot detect systemic biases (such as over-flagging a specific accent or nationality) across thousands of cases. Side A's closing crystallized this clash perfectly, proving that internal human review is structurally insufficient to catch underlying algorithmic harm, thereby securing the necessity of the transparency floor without falling into the trap of demanding raw source code.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side B's reliance on internal human review by proving that an individual appeal only tests the claimant's specific story, not the algorithm's underlying systemic weighting, rendering internal review structurally incapable of catching demographic bias.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, escalating cases, but PRO (B) sustained a clearer through-line: transparency defined from the opening as notice/factors/challenge/audit, defended consistently rather than truly retreating, and reinforced with apt analogies (wiretaps, informants, published judicial reasons) that directly neutralized CON's "gaming manual" and "theater" objections. PRO also answered the pressure questions cleanly with explicit A1/A2 labels and used the "which is worse" framing to blunt CON's strong hegemony/donor-leverage critique, even if not perfectly. CON (A) landed real hits — the definitional-slippage charge and the donor-leverage/hegemony reframe were genuinely original and exposed a real tension in PRO's enforcement story — but CON's rebuttals to the security-disclosure analogy stayed somewhat abstract ("consistency makes it legible to adversaries") without fully answering why judicial disclosure of human reasoning hasn't collapsed security. Decisively, CON's closing was clipped for exceeding the word limit, cutting off its final synthesis and vote rationale — a real execution cost per the judging frame — while PRO's closing was complete, tightly argued, and rhetorically the most memorable moment of the debate ("no secret sorting of human lives"). That combination of steadier framing, better-handled pressure exchange, and a clean closing versus a truncated one gives PRO the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 response to the "leak vector" and adversarial-gaming objections — analogizing tiered disclosure to how courts already handle wiretaps and informants, and noting that reasoned human refusals haven't collapsed border security — most damaged CON's central security argument, especially since CON's counter (consistency at scale = easier gaming) remained assertive rather than fully worked through.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): PRO kept a clearer live route: no secret sorting where stakes are liberty and return, with a floor (notice, factors, challenge, audit) that already limited source-code dumps in the opening. CON overplayed “retreat” when later tiering mostly spelled out A1.3, and never fully neutralized the human-reasons parallel or the transnational-vendor scaling point. CON’s best pressure—machine consistency aids gaming, global floor is hegemonic and redundant with domestic oversight—was real and competitive, and the slightly clipped closing cost a bit, but PRO answered the decisive security and enforcement clashes more directly and crystallized stakes better for a third-party reader.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.2/A3.1–A4.1 on “security proves too much”: human visa refusals already give reasons without collapsing borders; tiered access (person + cleared auditors/courts) is ordinary safeguard practice, not a public gaming manual. CON’s scale/consistency reply (B4.1) was sharp but did not restore the absolute secrecy premise.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A wins by providing a compelling justification for the global nature of the standard—specifically, that transnational vendors sell black-box tools to weak states, and a global floor provides the necessary leverage to demand accountability. Side A also successfully defends "tiered transparency" as the standard, legally grounded definition of administrative transparency, neutralizing Side B's security objections. Side B's arguments on sovereignty and gaming were strong, but Side A's comparative weighing on vendor leverage and the reality of automated scale proved more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal in Turn 7, which flips Side B's hegemonic enforcement argument by pointing out that without a global floor, weak states are forced to buy untestable black boxes from powerful vendors, making the global standard a protection for the weak rather than a burden.
