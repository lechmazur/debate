### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): A wins because it more clearly attacked both halves of the proposition: the claim of an underacknowledged urgent crisis and the blanket remedy of tripling maintenance before any megaproject. A's stable deficiency-rate argument, replacement-versus-repair framing, and opportunity-cost comparison made the motion look overbroad and poorly targeted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's pressure on the missing underacknowledgment metric was decisive: B's answer that the stable 7 percent figure itself proved underacknowledgment was weak, while A used that exchange to reinforce that the crisis was managed rather than hidden.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.3 (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side B won by successfully dismantling both core burdens of the prompt. B used the stable 7% deficiency rate to neutralize the 'urgent, hidden crisis' claim, and effectively argued that the proposed sequencing (tripling maintenance before megaprojects) would delay safety-enhancing replacements and mode-shifting projects like high-speed rail. Side A's labor-pool constraint argument was clever, but A struggled to explain why pausing megaprojects wouldn't also pause critical risk-reducing replacements.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal cleanly exposed the flaw in Side A's sequencing, pointing out that many megaprojects are themselves replacements or bypasses, and delaying them forces governments to waste money patching near-end-of-life assets.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Grok 4.3 (PRO)): CON won the factual premise of the motion. PRO never established that the crisis is “more urgent than governments publicly acknowledge”; CON used the stable 7 percent deficiency rate and rare fatal collapses to show governments’ public posture matches reality, and PRO’s interpretation of that same statistic as hidden urgency was strained. On the policy prong, CON successfully showed that many megaprojects are themselves replacements that bypass the worst spans, so an absolute tripling-before-any-new-project rule would delay the very safety gains it claims to seek. While PRO’s execution-capacity and sequencing argument remained live, it did not overcome CON’s combined pressure on opportunity cost, diminishing returns of repair versus replacement, and institutional waste. CON’s synthesis—targeted increases plus reform beats a blunt triple—provided the cleaner path to victory after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 1 argument that megaprojects replace rather than depend on aging spans, and the ensuing clash over opportunity cost versus sequencing, deflated PRO’s core dependency narrative and shifted the debate toward waste and diminishing returns.
