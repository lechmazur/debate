### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON)): Side A won on the most decisive clash: the parental blindfold problem. PRO's Q1 pressure—how does a daily cap protect a child from cyberbullying or toxic content during allowed hours?—was never convincingly answered. CON's best response was that "caps reduce the attack surface," which concedes the point and only argues for a smaller exposure window rather than protection from content harms. This left a live, damaging gap at the center of CON's case. PRO also landed the more memorable and mechanically honest comparative: a timer schedules access without filtering its quality, while a quality-focused parent can ban manipulative platforms altogether. CON's strongest argument—displacement of sleep and play—was real, but PRO's response (structural contextual rules like no bedroom devices and outdoor-play-first policies) was at least partially responsive, and CON never closed the loop on why caps are specifically superior to those alternatives. CON's physiological uniformity claim (FaceTime and doomscrolling identical because both "displace equally") was visibly the weakest move in the debate; A2 in Rebuttal 2 sidestepped the cognitive-impact question by collapsing it into displacement, which felt evasive given PRO's direct challenge. PRO also correctly read the motion: it does not ask for abolishing limits, only for shifting the primary frame of advice from time to quality and context, giving PRO a more defensible interpretive footing throughout. CON's enforceability and negotiation-fatigue argument had real force but was undermined by PRO's reframe that quality evaluation operates at the format/platform level rather than requiring per-video auditing. Both sides were epistemically disciplined with no fake citations. The margin is clear but not dominant because CON's displacement argument was substantively strong and partially unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question—asking how a daily cap defends against cyberbullying, predatory messaging, or extreme content during "allowed" hours—was the most decisive exchange. CON's A1 response ("caps reduce the attack surface; less time means fewer opportunities for harm") implicitly conceded that the scheduled hours remain unguarded and merely argued for a smaller risk window. PRO exploited this throughout: a blunt cap is a parental blindfold that schedules exposure rather than preventing harmful content, whereas a quality-focused parent can identify and block manipulative platforms entirely. This exchange drove the core comparative against CON's position and was never neutralized.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): B won by better fitting the motion: focusing more on quality and context need not abolish all boundaries, and B made the stronger case that simple caps give false security while ignoring cyberbullying, toxic content, and valuable creative or social uses. A’s displacement and enforceability arguments were strong and kept the debate competitive, but A often overstated that quality focus means unlimited exposure and only partially answered why harms inside the allowed window are acceptable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s “scheduled toxicity” rebuttal was decisive: A answered that caps reduce the attack surface, but never showed how a simple timer deals with harmful content or context during permitted screen time.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won by successfully defending displacement and enforceability as the core harms that quality-based frameworks cannot reliably solve. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): PRO holds a narrow but real edge across the full exchange. The core motion asks what parenting advice should prioritize, and PRO consistently frames the debate on that terrain: quality and context target the actual mechanism of harm (passive, isolated, algorithm-driven consumption), while caps can only count time regardless of what fills it. PRO's sharpest single moment is Pressure Question 2, which catches CON in a genuine internal tension: if all screen minutes equally displace development and disrupt sleep, whitelisting homework hours for unlimited educational use should be equally harmful—yet CON endorses that carve-out. CON's answer ("necessary compromise with modern education") is defensible but never fully resolves the contradiction, and it implicitly concedes that duration alone cannot be the deciding metric. PRO also lands a live route to victory that CON does not squarely neutralize: caps teach compliance, not judgment, so when software locks expire or teens borrow unmonitored devices, children raised on rigid limits face algorithms with no practiced self-regulation. CON's strongest countermoves are real—the automated-software rebuttal substantially undercuts PRO's enforcement-difficulty argument, the prefrontal-cortex development point is plausible, and the riptide/casino rhetorical framing is more vivid than anything PRO deploys. The "weekly check-ins are too slow for millisecond algorithms" criticism also stings PRO's A1 answer. But CON never provides a clean answer to the adulthood judgment gap, and the whitelisting inconsistency leaves the displacement argument weakened. The motion's framing ("focus more on quality and context than on simple caps") does not require eliminating caps, only deprioritizing them—PRO's case fits that standard better than CON's case for prioritizing hard ceilings. Both sides are competent and the clash is genuinely close, but PRO's argument more cleanly answers the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question 2 is the most decisive exchange: catching CON's claim that all screen minutes equally displace physical development against CON's own endorsement of whitelisting unlimited educational screen time. CON's answer—that school already forces screens so leisure time must be capped—is coherent but implicitly concedes that duration alone is insufficient as the governing metric, undercutting the displacement pillar of CON's case.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): B wins narrowly. A made a strong case that caps are simple, enforceable, and protect finite offline time, especially with automated tools and leisure exemptions. But B better matched the proposition: A proved caps can be useful, not that advice should focus on them more than quality and context. B kept a live route that harms differ by content, co-use, isolation, sleep context, and algorithmic design, and that caps are blunt and temporary while contextual habits build durable judgment. A's biological and dopamine claims were rhetorically strong but sometimes overconfident and did not fully answer why context-focused rules like bedroom limits, shared spaces, and creation versus autoplay are more targeted than a stopwatch.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's most decisive exchange was pressing that A's model exempted homework despite claiming duration itself causes physiological harm, while also arguing that caps age out or get bypassed. A had a plausible compromise answer, but it still left B's broader point standing: the advice must teach contextual judgment, not only enforce temporary limits.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Qwen 3.6 Max Preview (PRO)): CON won by proving that time caps are the only practical, enforceable safeguard against algorithmic capture and physiological displacement. PRO's quality framework collapsed under CON's pressure that algorithms operate faster than parental check-ins, making contextual judgment impossible to execute before children are hooked. CON also cleanly resolved PRO's pressure questions about adulthood and homework by framing caps as biological protection for brain development and a necessary boundary around leisure time.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that algorithms work in milliseconds, not weeks, exposing PRO's reliance on weekly check-ins as far too slow to interrupt dopamine loops or distinguish creation from consumption.
