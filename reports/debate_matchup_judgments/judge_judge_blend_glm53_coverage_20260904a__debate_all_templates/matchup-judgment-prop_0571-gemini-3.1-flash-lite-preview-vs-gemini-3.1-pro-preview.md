### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): CON built a more durable path to victory through three interlocking arguments—regulatory capture by large incumbents weaponizing complexity, jurisdictional compliance burdens freezing hiring, and insider-outsider dynamics pricing marginal workers out of entry-level roles. These arguments were coherent, mutually reinforcing, and survived the exchange largely intact. PRO's core economic logic (calibrated wages avoid the cliff-effect that triggers layoffs) was reasonable and never entirely demolished, but PRO consistently failed to mount convincing institutional responses. "Public oversight prevents capture" was thin and hand-wavy. The health/safety analogy was effectively challenged as a false equivalence (mandating gloves vs. layering granular pay tiers, scheduling rules, and job classifications). The small-business protection answer of "can be designed with designated seats" was hypothetical and evasive rather than grounded. PRO's best moment was Q2 (large corporations also lobby for high minimum wages, suggesting capture is not unique to wage boards), but CON's counter—simple transparent cost versus weaponizable complexity—was adequate. CON's sharpest move was turning PRO's own language about "operations, scheduling, or training" negotiations into confirmation that sectoral bargaining dictates entire business models, amplifying the complexity concern with PRO's own words. PRO held the rhetorical high ground with memorable framing ("floor that lifts the bottom without tearing down the roof") but rhetoric reinforcing an argument that was outperformed institutionally still yields CON the cleaner comparative case on the motion's key question of employment losses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's use in Rebuttal 2 of PRO's own opening language—that wage boards negotiate not just wages but "operations, scheduling, or training"—to confirm that sectoral bargaining dictates entire business models. This turned PRO's positive framing into direct evidence for CON's jurisdictional complexity and compliance burden argument, making it structurally more damaging than a simple denial would have been.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): CON won by more effectively grounding the debate in political economy and administrative realism. While PRO established an intuitive "precision beats bluntness" framework, CON successfully reframed that precision as a weaponizable liability—arguing that sectoral complexity, regulatory capture by large incumbents, and rigid insider-outsider dynamics would produce deeper employment losses than a simple universal floor. PRO never fully neutralized the insider-outsider harm or explained how small businesses could realistically prevent capture in practice, and their health-and-safety analogy crumbled under CON's false-equivalence pressure. CON's answers to PRO's pressure questions—especially the distinction between simple, transparent wage floors and complex, targetable sectoral mandates—sharpened the comparative advantage and left CON's route to victory cleaner.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's falsification of PRO's health-and-safety analogy and the subsequent "weaponize complexity" frame, which turned PRO's central "precision" argument into a liability by showing that large incumbents would use granular sectoral rules to bankrupt smaller competitors.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): CON won by successfully flipping PRO's core advantage. While PRO argued that sectoral "precision" tailors wages to industry capacity and saves jobs, CON convincingly demonstrated that this same precision weaponizes complexity, creating compliance labyrinths that crush small businesses and price out labor market outsiders. PRO's defenses relied on idealistic institutional design (public oversight, designated small-business seats, collaborative flexibility) that CON effectively grounded in the reality of regulatory capture and data dominance. CON's consistent comparison of a transparent, predictable floor versus a bureaucratic mandate that dictates business models directly resolved the prompt's employment-loss metric in their favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 turn where they seized PRO's admission that wage boards negotiate "scheduling or training investments" alongside wages. CON used this to prove that sectoral bargaining dictates entire operational models rather than just setting a pay floor, decisively turning PRO's precision argument into a structural job-loss liability for small firms.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): Side A won by consistently anchoring to the comparative burden the resolution actually requires: which system produces fewer employment losses? PRO's "internal braking mechanism" argument — that employer participation structurally caps wage increases below sector-specific breaking points, while a large statutory hike has no such check — is the debate's most decisive structural claim, and CON never fully neutralized it. PRO also executed an effective turning move throughout: whatever harms CON attributed to wage boards (incumbent advantage, barriers to small business entry) apply with equal or greater force to a flat statutory minimum, which instantly forces the same severe cost shock on every firm without any tiering mechanism. CON's core regulatory-capture theme was legitimate and consistently applied, but it remained an attack on wage boards in isolation rather than a comparative defense of the statutory alternative. CON's best answer — that wage boards "institutionalize" price increases through cost-plus collusion — is a real mechanism, but CON never completed the comparative chain to show this produces more employment losses than a blunt economy-wide mandate applied regardless of margin or elasticity. Side A's pressure questions (Turn 5) were notably sharper than Side B's: they forced CON to defend the comparison directly, whereas CON's questions largely restated its own themes. Both sides maintained reasonable epistemic discipline; neither deployed fake precision. The margin is clear but not dominant, because CON's regulatory-capture argument left a genuine residual concern that PRO answered only partially (asserting government conveners mandate small-business representation without developing how this prevents capture).
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 turning of the small-business argument [A2.1–A2.2] was the most decisive exchange. PRO showed that CON's concern about incumbents using wage boards to crush smaller rivals applies with even greater force to a flat statutory minimum, which legally mandates the same maximum cost shock on every firm with no tiering option. CON's eventual response ("rule of law" and equal application) did not explain why equal treatment of structurally unequal firms is better for employment outcomes, leaving the turn standing and undercutting CON's comparative standing throughout the rest of the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): PRO won by keeping the debate anchored to the resolution's comparative metric: which policy raises low-wage pay with fewer employment losses. While CON landed real critiques about regulatory capture and cartelization, PRO successfully neutralized them by showing that a large uniform minimum wage is even more destructive to small and low-margin employers because it offers no calibration mechanism. PRO's causal story—wage boards internalize sector-specific breaking points while statutory mandates blindly impose identical shocks—remained intact after the full exchange. CON never squarely proved that board-induced job losses would exceed those caused by massive across-the-board hikes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's turn in Rebuttal 1 showing that a flat statutory minimum is the true incumbent advantage, forcing an identical cost shock that megacorporations absorb but low-margin small firms cannot, whereas wage boards can negotiate size-based tiers—a comparative point CON never fully defused.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Flash-Lite Preview (CON)): PRO wins by maintaining a tighter focus on the resolution's core metric: comparative employment losses. PRO consistently leveraged the economic mechanism of industry-specific margins and price elasticity to argue that sectoral bargaining acts as an internal braking mechanism, preventing the blunt job destruction of a large flat wage hike. CON mounted a rhetorically strong case around regulatory capture, cartelization, and democratic transparency, but failed to adequately prove that a large across-the-board minimum wage would actually result in fewer employment losses. CON's defense of the alternative relied heavily on predictability and accountability, which, while valid governance concerns, sidestepped the direct economic comparison on job retention. PRO's rebuttals more effectively flipped CON's small business arguments and kept the clash anchored to the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 effectively answered CON's capture concerns by noting government ratification and tiered structures, then sharply pivoted back to the resolution's metric by contrasting the sledgehammer of a flat wage against vulnerable low-margin sectors with the scalpel of sector-specific calibration. This move neutralized CON's governance offensive and re-centered the debate on the employment loss comparison where PRO held the decisive advantage.
