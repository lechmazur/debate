### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): B better exploited the structural weakness in A's absolute "prefer X over Y" framing by forcing the bite-the-bullet question of whether a proven, beneficial mega-event should still be rejected. A's answer (A4.1) dodged this rather than committing, which is a real execution cost under the pressure-question rules. B also sustained a genuine unrebutted mechanism throughout the debate — that many beloved recurring local festivals originated as legacy programming from mega-events, and that recurring local events risk insider capture/stagnation — while A mostly answered with assertion rather than a concrete counter-mechanism. B's closing crystallized this into a memorable "certainty of stagnation" frame that directly weighed against A's "certainty of ownership" frame, leaving the decisive clash clearer for B. A's case was coherent and grounded but grew repetitive and never squarely resolved the seed-funding or insider-capture challenges.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Question ("will you state clearly that public institutions should turn down a well-planned mega-event proven to seed lasting local traditions, solely to abide by this rule?") and A's evasive, non-committal A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 — B then explicitly called out that dodge in B4.4, which was the most damaging unanswered exchange of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A wins by systematically dismantling Side B's two main routes to victory. Side A effectively frames Side B's 'seed funding' argument as a highly risky 'trickle-down' theory, pointing out that institutions could just directly fund the local events instead of hoping a mega-event leaves one behind. Furthermore, Side A wins the clash on 'stagnation' by demonstrating that the corporate barriers to entry for mega-events are vastly higher than any local gatekeeping at a community festival.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 decisively neutralized Side B's core 'seed' argument by asking why an institution would fund a risky one-off event just in the hope it leaves a parade behind, rather than funding the parade directly.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): PRO wins by offering a more coherent and defensible theory of place identity as cumulative, resident-owned practice, while successfully reframing CON's seed theory as a high-risk "trickle-down" gamble. PRO's core question—why bet on a mega-event to maybe leave a parade behind when you can fund the parade directly—remained unanswered at the end of the exchange. CON's stagnation/insider-control argument was adequately rebutted by showing that mega-events impose far higher barriers through corporate bidding and top-down contracting, whereas local recurring events provide low-barrier entry points. CON's closing rhetoric about freezing identity in amber was memorable but could not overcome the structural weakness that its model relies on speculative spillovers rather than guaranteed community ownership.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 reframing of CON's seed theory as "trickle-down identity" and the pointed comparison: "Why fund a one-off event that might—might—leave behind a parade, when you can fund the parade directly and guarantee the community owns it from day one?" This crystallized the efficiency objection and blunted CON's strongest causal narrative.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO built a coherent, resident-centered theory of place identity and sustained it under pressure, most decisively with the counter to Barcelona ("nobody says 1992 when asked what makes Barcelona home"), which CON never squarely rebutted—CON kept reasserting infrastructure's importance without engaging why residents don't cite mega-events as identity markers. PRO's pressure-question answers were concrete (opportunity cost of stadium debt vs. decades of local festivals; conceding non-inclusivity to reframe the debate). CON's answers, by contrast, redefined "identity" mid-debate into "strategic viability," which is a subtle but real drift from the motion's literal frame, and its A1 answer to why residents cite local traditions ("that just proves comfort, not strategic identity") was circular and evasive rather than a direct engagement. CON's case remained plausible and consistently argued but grew repetitive in later rounds, never landing a rebuttal as sharp as PRO's core counters.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 turn on Barcelona ("Nobody disputes the Olympics built waterfront pavement... but ask anyone who actually lives in Barcelona what makes that city feel like home today. They will not mention 1992") — this directly neutralized CON's central example and was never adequately answered in later CON speeches.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A established a highly resonant and consistent framework separating true "lived identity" for residents from mere external branding. Side B made strong economic arguments but suffered a catastrophic execution error in the Pressure Questions round, accidentally asking questions that attacked its own case. Side A easily capitalized on this mistake while maintaining a clean, compelling narrative throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 cleanly neutralized Side B's best example (Barcelona) by pointing out that while the Olympics laid the concrete, local traditions were required to give that infrastructure its actual identity.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): PRO won by keeping the debate anchored to the intuitive meaning of place identity as lived resident belonging, while CON repeatedly drifted toward economic competitiveness and infrastructure. PRO effectively neutralized CON’s Barcelona example by showing that recurring local traditions give meaning to the space the Olympics merely built, cleanly answered CON’s pressure questions about debt and inclusivity, and exposed CON’s reframing of identity as external branding as a shift away from the motion. CON’s assertions that local events are static or insular were left largely unwarranted.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s first rebuttal, which turned CON’s Barcelona Olympics example by arguing that residents remember the recurring neighborhood traditions that filled the new waterfront, not the 1992 event itself—transforming CON’s strongest piece of evidence into support for PRO’s thesis.
