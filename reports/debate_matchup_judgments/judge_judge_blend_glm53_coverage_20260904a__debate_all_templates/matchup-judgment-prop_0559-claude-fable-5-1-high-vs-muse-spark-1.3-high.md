### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): B won by exposing a structural consent problem: the receiving platform must obtain or match contacts' identifiers before those contacts can consent. A answered the questions directly and presented a credible lock-in benefit, but its safeguards narrowed transfer to public, findable handles and therefore weakened its promises concerning changed handles and pseudonymous supporters. B then weighed the remaining convenience gain against compelled disclosure of relationship data and also caught A narrowing the motion from all platforms to gatekeepers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's decisive exchange was the response to A's claim that the API was narrower than a public departure notice. B showed that public posting reveals the mover's address, while graph transfer additionally reveals other people's identifiers and associations before their consent; A never squarely resolved that distinction.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): Side B won by methodically dismantling Side A's consent mechanism. By forcing Side A to explain the exact order of operations, Side B successfully proved that user data (handles) must cross corporate boundaries before consent can be obtained, effectively weaponizing Side A's own concessions to prove the privacy violation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 decisively cornered Side A by using A's own admission about handles crossing first to prove that the mandate forces the creation of a non-consensual "shadow map" on the receiving platform.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON established a durable conceptual dilemma: mandated social-graph transfer either collapses into ordinary re-following or compels third-party data sharing, and under pressure PRO's policy steadily shrank to little more than a notification convenience. CON's "push versus passive" and "shadow map" line showed that the mechanism sends data across boundaries before secondary consent occurs, while their alternative—port your own data, post a public notice, enforce antitrust—preserved user power without conscripting followers' identifiers. PRO's tenancy narrative and phone-portability analogy were resonant, but they never fully resolved the co-ownership problem, and their mid-debate shift to a gatekeepers-only duty weakened fidelity to the motion as written. CON's superior weighing—one click saved versus a standardized, compelled pipeline for others' handles—made the decisive clash easier to resolve.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's "shadow map" and "push versus passive" exchange ([A2.1]–[A2.2], [A4.1]–[A4.2]), which minimized PRO's benefit to "one click instead of an afternoon" and exposed the mandate as a disproportionately heavy mechanism for modest convenience.
