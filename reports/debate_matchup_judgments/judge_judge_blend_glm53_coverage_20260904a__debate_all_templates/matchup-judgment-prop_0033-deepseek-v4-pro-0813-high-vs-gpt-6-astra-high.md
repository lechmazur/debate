### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but B ultimately left A with two unanswered strategic problems going into the close. First, B's "mixed model" dilemma (a retirement ad can trigger both substantive interest and reassurance simultaneously) exposed a real gap in A's proposed enforcement test (distinguishing "policy interest" optimization from "emotional" optimization): either such models escape the ban, undercutting its promised protection, or the ban swallows ordinary interest-based targeting, undercutting its promised narrowness. A never directly answered this in Rebuttal 2 or the closing. Second, B's "proves too much" challenge — that a campaign's superior strategic knowledge of how a message will land is a feature of all persuasion, not proof that psychologically-informed persuasion uniquely defeats agency — went unanswered after B's closing raised it sharply. A's core move, that consent cannot cover "the moment of influence," was repeated across three speeches but never developed beyond assertion, and by the close it looked more like a restated premise than a demonstrated harm. A's structural-harm reframing in the closing (protecting deliberation rather than proving individual impairment) was a smart pivot but did not resolve the concrete enforceability and overbreadth objections still live on the table. B's alternative regulatory framework (disclosure, consent, audits, bans on deception/concealment) gave the debate a clean comparative frame that A's rebuttals chipped at but did not fully dismantle by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over A's functional enforcement test (A4.3) versus B's "mixed models" dilemma (B4.5–B4.6): B showed that any workable line between "policy interest" and "emotional" targeting either lets the feared practice through or bans legitimate interest-based targeting, and A never resolved this tension in the closing.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): CON won the two decisive clashes: informed consent and enforceability. PRO conceded it bans even knowingly authorized, truthful, archived appeals, and CON showed that requires proof receptiveness equals impaired judgment, which PRO never supplied — selection does not determine judgment. On enforceability, PRO's opening claim of a narrow, label-based ban collapsed in Rebuttal 2 when it admitted the ban must cover functional emotional optimization, creating the dilemma CON pressed: mixed relevance/emotion models either escape or drag ordinary interest targeting into the ban. Alternatives (archives, audits, consent) address the accountability story PRO opened with.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A2.1/A4.5 combo: predicting receptiveness is not determining judgment, and a retirement ad can win engagement through relevance and reassurance at once, so PRO's functional emotional-optimization test must either leave fear-optimization alive or swallow permitted interest targeting — undermining both its harm justification and its narrowness claim.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A built a much stronger and more resilient case for why the specific harm of psychological micro-targeting cannot be cured by mere transparency or consent. A's distinction between targeting for policy interest versus affective response provided a workable regulatory boundary, effectively neutralizing B's slipperiness objections. A's rhetorical framing of the "asymmetric moment of influence" decisively defeated B's reliance on post-hoc archives and informed consent, making the structural harm to democratic deliberation clear and compelling.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the consent and transparency arguments, specifically pointing out that "the voter experiences the ad before seeing any archive" and that consent to data collection does not equate to waiving the right to evaluate political appeals by their reasons rather than triggered affect.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): The debate turned on whether the "psychological profile vs. legitimate topic/interest targeting" line could be operationalized, and on whether the ban's costs to underfunded campaigns were justified. B pressed this enforceability problem relentlessly, showing that identical page-view data could be labeled either "housing interest" (permitted) or "anxiety" (prohibited), and A's final answer — that regulators must examine whether an "estimate" of mental state was used [A4.2] — effectively conceded that officials would still be adjudicating hidden psychological categories, confirming rather than resolving B's chilling-inquiry critique. A's harm claim also stayed abstract ("individualized informational asymmetry," "psychological leverage") without ever cashing out into a concrete injury distinguishable from ordinary targeted advertising, whereas B's speech-cost argument (dispersed, self-unidentified persuadable voters lost to challengers) was concrete and went largely unrebutted beyond assertion that it was "marginal." B's disclosure alternative also gave a coherent answer to the opacity concern without the line-drawing problem. A's tu quoque (disclosure requires the same inspection) was a fair point but only partially blunted B's critique. Overall B built the more resilient, better-defended path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange starting with B's pressure question ("what operational test distinguishes them without officials policing inferred emotions?") and A's answer in Rebuttal 2 ("officials must establish that a mental-state estimate was used") was decisive: A's own framing confirmed the chilling, subjective inquiry B had warned about, and B's Rebuttal 2 explicitly capitalized on that concession.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO kept a clean mechanism distinction: topic/context vs. inferred psychological score, and gave an operational test (model objective/output/use). CON collapsed the distinction to 'same readers' but never neutralized that operational difference. B's pressure on disclosure — you need the same inspection you call unworkable — landed, while A answered the small-campaign question but not the control-vs-notice distinction. The accountability/informational asymmetry harm survived truthful-content objections, giving PRO the clearer path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B3 Q1 / B4.1 exchange where CON's disclosure alternative was shown to require the same vendor-input checks it attacked as chilling, plus B2.1-2.3 distinguishing interest-matching from vulnerability-matching.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by successfully defending the operability of its proposed ban and exposing a fatal contradiction in Side B's disclosure alternative. Side A clearly explains that regulators can inspect model objectives, training criteria, and outputs to distinguish psychological profiling from standard issue targeting. When Side B argues that inspecting models is too subjective for a ban but acceptable for a disclosure regime, Side A decisively points out that both require the exact same technical verification, but only the ban actually prevents the harm. Side A's framing of the harm as individualized psychological leverage, rather than relying on strawmen like "mind control," is highly persuasive and keeps the debate grounded in realistic democratic concerns.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.5), where it traps Side B in a contradiction: Side B cannot demand credible disclosure of targeting criteria while simultaneously claiming that inspecting those same targeting systems to enforce a ban is an unworkable, chilling inquiry. Both require verifying what the model actually estimates.
