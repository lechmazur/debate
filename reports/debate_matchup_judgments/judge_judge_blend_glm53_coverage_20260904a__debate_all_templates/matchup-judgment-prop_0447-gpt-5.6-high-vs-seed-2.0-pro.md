### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A won by systematically dismantling Side B's absolutist claims and offering superior comparative weighing. Side B's case suffered from an internal contradiction, arguing simultaneously that regulators would aggressively invalidate paperwork to issue six-figure fines (scaring firms away from small contractors) and that regulators would entirely ignore enforcement to quietly collect revenue. Side A capitalized on this by showing that even under Side B's projected outcomes (firms using large agencies, direct hiring, or paying the surcharge), the policy still successfully funds social protections and improves upon the status quo.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal (A4.5) decisively trapped Side B by explaining that even if firms react exactly as B predicts—defaulting to large agencies, hiring directly, or retaining opaque chains and paying the surcharge—all three outcomes still result in funded social contributions, making them superior to the status quo.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A constructed a more resilient policy model: a supplemental contribution with safe-harbor documentation places predictable liability on the solvent lead firm, preserves separate classification enforcement, and prevents cross-border arbitrage because liability follows the company using the labor. Side B’s central objection—that the rule monetizes precarity and creates perverse state incentives—was rhetorically sharp but remained speculative and unsupported by a concrete legal mechanism; Side A showed that even under Side B’s own feared scenarios the results are better than the status quo. Side B also never recovered from Side A’s argument that large firms cannot casually hide procurement trails to evade the rule entirely.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A, Rebuttal 2: the comparative weighing that lead firms choosing direct hiring, compliant contractors, or opaque chains all produce better outcomes than the status quo, exposing that Side B’s predicted harms do not prove net harm.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO kept the live route: lead-firm liability corrects externalization, credits/safe harbor answer small-firm exclusion, payment does not legally block classification, visibility helps enforcement, EU level prevents routing. CON's core that firms will exclude all small suppliers and regulators will stop enforcing relied on asserted inevitability rather than mechanism, and was directly undermined by B's Qs and R2.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 - safe harbor turning uncertain liability into predictable cost plus classification remains, reframing three firm choices as all better than status quo, directly neutralizing CON's loophole and exclusion claims.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by surgically dismantling Side A's proposed mechanism. When Side A introduced aggregate certificates to solve the data privacy issue, Side B effectively pointed out that aggregate data cannot allocate shortfalls for shared workers across multiple clients, elevating this from a mere administrative complaint to a fatal structural flaw. Side B also successfully maintained the stance that liability should follow control rather than distance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal exposed the logical gap in Side A's aggregate certificate mechanism by pointing out that a contractor serving multiple clients cannot use aggregate payroll to assign a specific liability share to just one purchaser without granular data.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): CON won because it demonstrated that PRO's aggregate certificate could not solve the allocation problem across shared workforces and subcontracting tiers, and that liability should follow control rather than company size. PRO failed to establish a workable causal link or to rebut the 'convenient payer' framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's dismantling of PRO's aggregate certificate defense by showing that shared workforces and multi-tier chains make it impossible to attribute shortfalls to a specific purchaser without granular data, neutralizing PRO's main answer to the administrative burden objection.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): CON wins on implementation and targeting. PRO's "difference only" safeguard depends on an aggregate certificate from direct contractor, but CON shows a shared-workforce contractor can't allocate hours/contributions to one buyer and can't reveal tier-2 failures without worker-level data. That leaves PRO choosing between a certificate too general to calculate liability and an audit too intrusive to be proportionate. The double-counting, privacy, and de-risking toward large intermediaries objections survive, while PRO's repeated "never denied" concession claim misreads CON's attacks on causation and on who should pay. Targeted enforcement following control and earnings remains the cleaner fit.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 takedown of the aggregate certificate — multi-client allocation, no visibility two tiers down, and incentive to exclude small honest suppliers rather than only bad actors.
