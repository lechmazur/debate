### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): CON built a cleaner, more consistent doctrinal frame (agreement/plus-factors vs. mere parallel adoption) and used the Pressure Questions round to devastating effect. CON's Q1 forced PRO to draw a line between "calculators" (public data) and scrutiny-worthy tools (non-public, real-time data), which PRO answered in A4.1 — but this answer conceded a narrower rule than the motion as written ("shared algorithmic rent-setting tools," full stop). CON exploited this concession decisively in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, arguing PRO had effectively adopted CON's own proposed remedy (case-by-case scrutiny of actual data pooling) while still nominally defending a categorical per se rule. PRO never directly answered this scope-collapse charge in its closing, leaving a decisive, un-rebutted line of attack live at the end. CON's second pressure question on "plus factors" also exposed that PRO's core claim ("the agreement is baked into the subscription") was conclusory; PRO's later restatement ("mutual outsourcing is the agreement") was persuasively reframed by CON as "awareness renamed," a point PRO never overcame. CON also sustained a strong policy point (per se rules entrench corporate landlords over small ones) that PRO addressed only partially. PRO's case remained coherent and grounded in a real antitrust theory (hub-and-spoke/substance-over-form), but it lost the exchange on precision, concession-management, and closing-round resolution.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question Q1 in Turn 6, forcing PRO to concede in A4.1 that only non-public/real-time data tools warrant scrutiny — which CON then used in Rebuttal 2 and Closing to show PRO had narrowed the motion to match CON's own proposed remedy, a concession PRO never recovered from.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO); B = Kimi K3 (CON)): B wins the central legal clash: agreement plus-factor. A asserts subscription equals agreement and private data pooling is per se illegal, but B shows parallel adoption is independently profitable, knowledge is not assent, overrides show no agency or enforcement, and even human info-exchange is rule-of-reason absent agreement. B's pressure line forcing A to narrow "shared tools" to only private-data tools concedes the motion's categorical per se scope, making A's final position case-by-case scrutiny which was B's opening remedy. Existing law covering real conspiracies leaves A without a distinct path.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1/B4.3 - Independent profitability defeats plus-factor inference and deviation argument flipped: cheating proves an agreement exists to breach, while ignoring a suggestion with no punishment proves no agreement; this materially damaged A's "baked into subscription" route.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Kimi K3 (CON); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (PRO)): Side A won by exposing the overbreadth of the proposition and forcing Side B to narrow the scope to tools using "non-public, real-time data." This concession effectively collapsed Side B's case into Side A's position: that case-by-case scrutiny of actual anti-competitive data pooling is the correct standard, rather than the categorical per se liability demanded by the motion. Side A also delivered a decisive and logically airtight rebuttal to Side B's "cheating" analogy, proving that independent self-interest and awareness do not constitute the "meeting of the minds" required for antitrust liability.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's Q2 in Rebuttal 2, explaining that cheating on a cartel price presupposes an agreement and an enforcement mechanism, whereas ignoring an algorithm's suggestion carries no punishment because no agreement was ever made.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): PRO built a layered, doctrinally grounded case: hub-and-spoke liability (Apple e-books), knowing participation inferred from conduct, a clear bright-line test (confidential live competitor data feeding a common compliance-tracked engine vs. aggregated/public benchmarking), and real-world grounding in the RealPage litigation without overclaiming its outcome. When CON's strongest and most repeated pressure point landed — that "scrutiny as if coordinating directly" seemingly demands per se treatment yet PRO still allows efficiency defenses — PRO answered with the Broadcast Music ancillary-restraints doctrine, arguing the motion imports the analytical framework (including its established carve-outs) rather than an automatic verdict. CON called this a "surrender" but never actually dismantled the mechanism or explained why ancillary-restraints reasoning doesn't apply; it mostly re-asserted the contradiction rhetorically. CON's core defense (unilateral adoption, no retaliation, non-binding recommendations) was met squarely by PRO's "cartels that sometimes cheat are still cartels" point and by PRO's answer to CON's own pressure question distinguishing benchmarking tools from pooled-secret pricing engines — a distinction CON's Q2 tried to blur but never successfully reopened. Both sides answered pressure questions with proper A1/A2 labeling, but PRO's answers did more work resolving the central legal tension, while CON largely repeated its opening framing across rounds without developing new mechanism-level responses. PRO's opening was clipped for length, a real execution cost, but subsequent rounds recovered and extended the case fully.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's use of the Broadcast Music ancillary-restraints doctrine to answer CON's repeated "per se bars all defenses" pressure point was the pivotal exchange: it directly resolved the apparent contradiction CON hammered throughout, and CON's rebuttal ("this is a surrender") was rhetorically sharp but never substantively rebutted the doctrine itself.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Kimi K3 (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): PRO built a live path: pooled current confidential data + shared pricing engine + compliance tracking = substance of hub-and-spoke agreement, with RealPage as concrete illustration and a clear limiting line. CON's best pressure — per se bars efficiencies — was directly answered by A4's Broadcast Music/ancillary restraints point, while CON never neutralized the discipline-as-product argument or the human-consultant equivalence. A kept scrutiny vs automatic guilt distinct and narrowed the dispute; B repeated independent judgment/no retaliation after it was materially damaged.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2-A4 sequence: cartels also cheat so non-binding overrides don't defeat coordination, compliance dashboards show discipline is the product, and the per se contradiction is resolved because even direct price coordination gets rule-of-reason when ancillary — preserving scrutiny as if coordinating directly without mandating automatic guilt.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = Kimi K3 (PRO)): Side B wins by building a much stronger and more legally nuanced case. B successfully frames the algorithmic tool as an automated hub-and-spoke cartel, using the "human consultant" analogy to expose the flaw in A's formalistic distinction between software and direct coordination. When A attempts to trap B in a legal contradiction by arguing that direct coordination is per se illegal and thus bars efficiency defenses, B defuses this by invoking the Broadcast Music ancillary restraints doctrine, showing that horizontal scrutiny does not automatically mean per se condemnation. A fails to effectively respond to this nuance in later speeches, relying on repetitive assertions that no horizontal agreement exists without dismantling B's hub-and-spoke logic. B's rhetoric is also significantly more memorable and persuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they invoke the Broadcast Music principle to dismantle Side A's "per se" trap, proving that horizontal scrutiny allows for efficiency defenses when ancillary to productive collaboration, thereby neutralizing A's primary legal offensive.
