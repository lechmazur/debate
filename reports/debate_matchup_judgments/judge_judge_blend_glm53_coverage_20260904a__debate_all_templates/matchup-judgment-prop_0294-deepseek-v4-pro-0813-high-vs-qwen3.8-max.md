### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but B landed the more decisive blow. A's central move—reframing review as testing for "evidentiary completeness" rather than adjudicating interpretation—was clever, but B's Rebuttal 2 exposed it as question-begging: deciding which documented alternatives must be included, how prominently, and what counts as suppression is itself the contested interpretive judgment A claimed to avoid. A's closing did not rebut this; it merely conceded that "every textbook selection already answers that," which effectively validated B's critique rather than refuting it. Meanwhile, A's strongest asset—the "timing" argument that political correction arrives too late to undo classroom harm—was met by B with a workable parry: certified narratives also require re-review or replacement to be amended, so the asymmetry in correction speed is less one-sided than A suggested, and open political contestation (hearings, courts, media) can name and challenge euphemism immediately rather than waiting on expert consensus. B's pressure question on "unscholarly" labeling extracted the debate's most consequential concession, and A never fully closed that gap. A's question about who drafts "evidence rules" was answered adequately by B (accountable, transparent bodies), blunting the regress charge. On balance, B's case for contestable, amendable civic authorship of national narratives held up better under scrutiny than A's case that scholarly gatekeeping is a neutral, low-cost checkpoint.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 point [B4.3–B4.4], showing that A's "evidentiary completeness" standard is itself an interpretive command (who decides what must be included, how prominently, what omission counts as suppression), which A's closing conceded rather than answered.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending the proposition as a supplementary evidentiary safeguard rather than the expert finality Side A repeatedly attacked. B showed that public adoption, elections, and competing interpretations can remain while review catches omissions before classroom harm occurs. A raised a serious legitimacy concern about who defines completeness, but never established why visible political control would better protect accuracy or why its accountability mechanisms could not coexist with scholarly review.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions directly, distinguished evidentiary completeness from imposing one interpretation, preserved public selection powers, and then weighed preventive review against correction only after students receive a distorted narrative.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by successfully proving that 'evidentiary completeness' is inherently an interpretive and civic judgment. While Side B landed a strong practical argument about the timing of bad history entering classrooms, Side A's consistent deconstruction of scholarly review as an unaccountable, hidden pre-commitment prevailed on the core question of democratic legitimacy.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 sharply neutralized Side B's standard of 'evidentiary completeness' by pointing out that deciding which alternatives must be included and how prominently is itself an interpretive command.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, competing frames — PRO on evidence-discipline preventing sanitization before harm occurs, CON on democratic removability preventing entrenched, unaccountable error. PRO's pressure-question answers were more concrete (dispersed appointment, arm's-length funding, evidence-based criteria for labeling contested events like "massacre" vs "necessity"), directly engaging CON's "who appoints/who writes the line" challenge. CON's answers were more concessive ("no system guarantees...") and largely repeated the "no routable target" refrain without directly countering PRO's institutional design proposal. CON's strongest and most under-addressed point — that political mobilization (civil rights, Holocaust, Indigenous history) has actually forced textbook correction — was never squarely rebutted by PRO, but PRO's closing crystallized a decisive asymmetry CON never fully answered: democratic accountability only works if voters *want* accurate, uncomfortable history, and the entire premise of the debate is that they often have electoral incentives not to. CON's opening was also clipped for exceeding the word limit, a real execution cost that slightly weakened its foundational case. On balance PRO's case was tighter, better defended under pressure, and closed with a sharper decision rule.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to Q2 (A4.3), laying out evidence-based criteria (intent, proportionality, command directives) for labeling contested events, was the most concrete attempt to bridge the fact/frame divide CON kept pressing — and CON's closing never directly re-engaged with those specific criteria, instead falling back on the general "reviewers choose frames too" assertion.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate. Side A compellingly framed history as morally contested and emphasized democratic corrigibility, but it repeatedly treated stronger review as exclusive, entrenched expert control. Side B preserved democratic control over broad goals while showing why factual narratives should face source-based scrutiny before publication. Its comparison between ex ante prevention and electoral correction after cohorts are harmed, reinforced by politicians' incentives to flatter national identity, remained the strongest live route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's most decisive response was that scholarly fallibility supports disciplined, revisable review rather than electoral narrative control. It paired this with the timing argument: elections may punish sanitization later, while independent review requires evidence before students receive the account. Side A never fully neutralized that incentive-and-prevention comparison.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side B wins by effectively demonstrating that scholarly review provides an evidentiary constraint on historical narratives that political control lacks. Side B's detailed breakdown of how scholars evaluate events successfully dismantles Side A's claim that historical framing is purely a subjective value choice, whereas Side A leaves Side B's point about political incentive structures largely intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal (B4.3) decisively answered Side A's pressure question by explaining the specific methodologies scholars use to distinguish "massacre" from "military necessity," neutralizing Side A's core argument about subjective moral framing.
