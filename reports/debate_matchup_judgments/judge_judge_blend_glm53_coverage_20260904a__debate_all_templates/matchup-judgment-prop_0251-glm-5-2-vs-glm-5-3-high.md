### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides argued sharply, but A won the framing battle that mattered most: the motion asks whether governments *should* mandate stop-work protections, and A consistently anchored the debate to a concrete, high-stakes moment—"who decides at 2PM on a lethal day"—and argued that only a hard trigger removes employer discretion at that instant. B's strongest move was the "auditability" and "bendable inputs" critique, correctly showing that PRO's WBGT/workload/acclimatization trigger still relies on employer-declared inputs, which is a real vulnerability. But B's own alternative (layered duties + inspections + penalties) suffers the identical vulnerability—it also depends on employer compliance with rest cycles, shade, and acclimatization, which B itself admitted get skipped under production pressure. A's closing exploited this directly: "a skipped rest break evaporates too... unlike a stop order, it leaves the worker still on the roof." B never adequately explained why inspections-and-penalties would compel real-time compliance better than an automatic stop, especially once B conceded enforcement failures are pervasive. B's wage-continuity attack ("what treasury?") was a good pressure question, but A's answer (existing paid-leave/workers'-comp precedents, policy-design framing) was adequate to neutralize it as a design detail rather than a fatal flaw, and B's closing did not revisit or press this point hard enough to make it decisive. On the "scaffolding" analogy, B's rebuttal that heat is ambient rather than a fixable hazard was a fair distinguishing point, but it doesn't undermine the core labor-rights logic A built—removing decision-making power from the incentivized party during acute danger. Overall A did the better job resolving the central clash by round's end, even though B's questions forced real concessions and complexity onto A's model. The debate was close and both sides showed strong rebuttal skill, but A's final framing was cleaner and more decisive for a reader trying to resolve the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Q&A exchange on acclimatization verification (B's Q1 in Pressure Questions, A's WBGT/workload-category answer, and B's rebuttal that this still relies on bendable employer declarations) was the most decisive exchange—it exposed the core tension in PRO's case, but PRO's closing successfully reframed the same vulnerability as symmetric to CON's own layered-regime reliance on employer compliance, tipping the exchange back in PRO's favor.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side A won by exposing a fatal contradiction in Side B's case. B argued that stop-work rules are necessary precisely to remove employer discretion, but A cornered B into conceding that the trigger must rely on complex variables like workload and acclimatization. A decisively showed that these inputs still rely on employer declarations, meaning B's policy fails to solve the very problem it identified.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing brilliantly tracked the trajectory of B's case, showing that B's "unbendable stop" had morphed into a complex system of employer-declared inputs that would bend to production exactly like the rest cycles B originally denounced.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): CON won the decisive instrument clash: mandatory stop-work is either too blunt for multi-factor heat risk or, once PRO added WBGT, workload categories, acclimatization schedules, and wage continuity, collapses into a layered regime plus a least-auditable employer-declared trigger. PRO kept a strong labor-rights/power-imbalance frame and timing-gap rhetoric, but never squared the contradiction that sophistication reintroduces the discretion they said already fails. CON’s second rebuttal was clipped (execution cost), yet enough of the auditability and trajectory reply landed to leave the clearer path to vote CON.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON forcing PRO’s ‘self-executing stop’ into employer-declared WBGT/workload/acclimatization inputs, then showing that still hides bendable judgment and is harder to audit than continuous layered duties.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-grounded cases, but PRO ultimately controlled the decisive line of clash: what happens when graduated, site-specific plans fail. CON's Rebuttal 2 answer to this pressure point—delegating stop-work authority to supervisors—was functionally conceded by PRO's closing as "stop-work minus the worker-triggerable feature," turning CON's own fallback into support for PRO's mechanism. CON's strongest sustained argument (that stop-work structurally severs the wage-production link in a way graduated measures do not) was real and never fully answered with textual grounding, but PRO successfully reframed the debate around asymmetry of irreversible harm (death) versus reversible harm (a wage statute needing fixing), and CON's "false choice" rebuttal to that framing came earlier and wasn't renewed forcefully in the clipped closing. PRO also neutralized CON's "single crude threshold" strawman by noting real heat rules layer acclimatization and humidity indices, and used CON's own graduated-mandate framework to force the concession that heat regulation is already a mandatory labor-law matter. Both openings/closings suffered format clipping, slightly more so for PRO (opening and closing both cut), which is a real but minor execution cost, not enough to offset PRO's cleaner handling of the pivotal backstop exchange and closing weighing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's use of CON's own "delegated stop-work authority" concession (B4.3) in the closing (A5.2)—showing that CON's fallback for plan failure is functionally identical to PRO's mechanism, just discretionary rather than a worker-triggerable right—was the most decisive moment, since CON's closing never adequately recovered from having its alternative absorbed into PRO's framework.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by decisively winning the clash over the "backstop." While Side A made a very strong and persistent structural argument about wage loss, Side B successfully forced Side A to explain what happens when graduated plans fail. When Side A conceded that supervisors could be delegated stop-work authority, Side B brilliantly tied this back to their opening argument about power imbalances, proving why the stop-work must be a mandatory right rather than a supervisor's discretion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's closing perfectly capitalized on Side A's A2 response, pointing out that a discretionary stop-work order relies on the same supervisor whose schedule is slipping, proving the exact need for a mandatory trigger.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (CON); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (PRO)): CON kept the motion fixed on the specific tool—mandatory stop-work—and showed PRO’s strongest answers required assuming paid downtime and collapsing graduated heat rules into stoppage. That wage/architecture clash and the false binary with “do nothing” stayed live. PRO was sharper on power imbalance, enforceability, and the day the plan breaks, but those points never fully overcame CON’s tool-specific burdens.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's first rebuttal: paid downtime is not in the proposition, so PRO cannot treat worst-case unpaid stoppage as mere bad drafting while still claiming the motion as written.
