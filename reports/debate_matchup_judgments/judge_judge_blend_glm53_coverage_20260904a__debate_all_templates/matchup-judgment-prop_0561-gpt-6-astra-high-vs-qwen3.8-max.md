### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides built coherent, plausible causal stories, but CON's exclusion-mechanism argument—that fixed compliance/liability costs per worker (not just proration size) will push platforms to screen out or deactivate low-hour accounts—was more concrete and better sustained across rounds. The pivotal exchange was CON's pressure question demanding a threshold/evidence that marginal workers wouldn't be priced out; PRO's answer explicitly conceded it "cannot supply a verified estimate or guarantee every casual worker gains," which CON then exploited decisively in rebuttal and closing. CON also neutralized PRO's strongest concrete example (the delayed courier) with a plausible "delay top-up" transaction-based fix, showing its alternative could handle duration-sensitive harm without full hourly accounting, weakening PRO's claim that time-tracking is indispensable. PRO's structural defenses (proration avoids a cliff, portability, pooled administration) were reasonable but never squarely rebutted the fixed-cost/exclusion mechanism, and PRO's closing pivoted to "your alternative has costs too" rather than directly refuting the exclusion causal chain. CON's closing was tighter and better crystallized the decisive clash, giving it the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question ("what threshold of lost marginal access would make the policy fail, and what evidence shows casual workers will not fall below it?") forced PRO's explicit concession of no threshold or evidence, which CON then leveraged in both Rebuttal 2 and Closing as the decisive unanswered harm.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side B won by successfully exploiting the specific wording of the prompt ('tied to hours worked'), arguing convincingly that an hourly ledger creates fixed compliance costs that disincentivize retaining casual, low-hour workers. Side A argued well structurally, but Side B's distinction between small prorated benefit costs and fixed per-account auditing costs proved decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.3 effectively dismantled A's attempt to equate the two models' administrative burdens by distinguishing between checking timestamps for delayed jobs and continuously auditing overlapping idle time across multiple apps.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): CON wins because its exclusion-mechanism argument remained live after the full exchange: prorated hourly benefits attach compliance and liability costs to every account, giving platforms rational incentives to deactivate, screen, or steer away from small-hour and marginal workers. PRO never squarely neutralized this mechanism; it admitted it could supply no verified estimate or threshold for participation loss, and could not show that net protection for casual workers would survive the cut. CON offered a coherent alternative (transaction-based funding with delay top-ups) that funded protection without converting platforms into hour-auditing employers, giving judges a cleaner path to protect workers while preserving open entry.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4), where it answered PRO's pressure questions with the "delay top-up" design and then demonstrated that hourly accounting creates fixed per-account costs platforms will avoid by excluding marginal workers—a harm PRO could only call "a cost to weigh" without evidence or threshold.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B built and sustained the sharper analytical throughline: mandated benefits do not settle "who pays" merely by legal labeling, since platforms can offset contributions via lower cash pay, meaning the worker may simply finance their own "benefit" in a less liquid form. A's strongest counter—that inadequate current pay proves an existing subsidy—never answered B's clean rebuttal (B4.1) that inadequacy of prior pay does not convert redirected earnings into net additional security. On the hours-measurement front, B's pressure question forced A into a genuine dilemma: choosing "engaged time" as the trigger both abandons protection for the very instability (waiting time) invoked to justify the policy and validates B's rationing concern ("schedule more efficiently" for whom). A conceded ground here without adequately closing the gap. A's closing repeated the moral framing ("who bears the cost of illness...") without directly refuting B's arithmetic point, while B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("Judge their security, not the platform's paperwork") and kept pressure on the unproven claim that pooling/portability outweighs lost cash for heterogeneous workers. B did somewhat dodge A's direct question about an alternative trigger design, which is a minor execution cost, but this was outweighed by B's consistently more rigorous engagement with incidence, heterogeneity, and the operational tensions A never fully resolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's rebuttal 1 exposing the tension in A's "schedule more efficiently" line (B2.3–B2.4), later reinforced by B's pressure question forcing A to choose between excluding waiting time or admitting rationing risk—A's answer in Rebuttal 2 conceded the dilemma rather than resolving it.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully exposing a critical gap in Side A's case regarding cost incidence. Side B proved that mandating a benefit does not prevent platforms from passing the cost back to workers via lower cash compensation, which actively harms those who need rent money or already have insurance. Side A's concession that this might happen, coupled with the claim that it just "exposes a subsidy," failed to prove that workers are actually better off under the mandate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal decisively capitalized on Side A's concessions, pointing out that if pay is cut, the worker funds the benefit regardless of past inadequacy, and that choosing "engaged time" abandons protection for the very instability Side A decried.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins because it established a clearer structural route to victory: platform work currently externalizes essential costs onto workers and taxpayers, and prorated benefits tie protection to the measurable fact of labor while preserving flexibility. Side B’s pass-through and net-security objections were sharp but never fully overturned the race-to-bottom and hidden-subsidy logic; they instead treated the status quo as viable if workers simply keep marginal cash, without adequately answering who bears catastrophic risk now. Side A also more effectively crystallized the clash in closing, framing the question as whether directed labor must generate security rather than offload it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The incidence exchange over pass-through costs (Turns 3–4, 7–8), where Side B pressed that redirected worker pay does not create additional resources, and Side A countered that mandatory pooled accrual replaces an individually unaffordable, risk-laden status quo—leaving the core question of whether the hidden subsidy to platforms was the decisive injustice.
