### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won a clear but competitive debate on several dimensions. First, execution: Side A was clipped in three of five speeches—Opening, Rebuttal 2, and Closing—meaning arguments were literally cut off each time. This is a real cost, not a technicality, because live clash was left unresolved. Second, the boxing case study: PRO introduced boxing as the concrete evidence of paywall-induced cultural decline, and CON's rebuttal that it was "product fragmentation and quality" rather than enclosure felt thin against PRO's timeline argument (the sport retreated behind paywalls and then lost the generation). Third, PRO successfully turned CON's own examples. Every counterexample CON offered—F1 via Drive to Survive on an accessible tier, UFC with free prelims, NFL voluntarily keeping Sunday afternoons on broadcast—actually supported the proposition that blended free access is the sustainable model, not pure enclosure. PRO's "floor not straitjacket" framing consistently neutralized the regulatory-uncertainty objection. Fourth, PRO answered pressure questions with proper A1:/A2: labels and substantively (the geographic access answer—antenna costs once, broadband costs monthly—was clean). CON's strongest live argument was that leagues voluntarily choose free-to-air when it serves them, which is real, but PRO answered it adequately: voluntary choices can still erode under short-term revenue pressure, hence the need for a structural floor. On balance, PRO held the decisive clash—audience-engine causality—with better-grounded examples and cleaner execution throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 boxing answer (A1): rather than accepting CON's framing that boxing declined for product reasons, PRO flipped the timeline—boxing retreated behind paywalls first, then lost a generation of casual viewers. This was the most memorable and causally coherent specific case in the debate, and CON never fully defused it.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B won by keeping the central clash clearer: free-to-air marquee games are an access floor and a long-term audience engine, not charity or an anti-streaming ban. Side A had strong points on voluntary incentives, modern discovery, and regulatory uncertainty, but over-relied on recasting the motion as a government mandate and never fully neutralized the equity problem of fragmented subscriptions. B also handled pressure questions directly and used boxing plus the NFL blend to make the long-term relevance risk more concrete. A was competitive, but clipped turns and a weaker answer on low-income access cost it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response that A was attacking a false binary was decisive: the proposition allows a blend, so examples where leagues thrive with both free exposure and paid depth supported B more than A.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = GLM-5.1 (PRO)): Side B built the stronger path by showing free-to-air marquee games create the audience engine and cultural foundation enabling all revenue (boxing decline after enclosure, counterexamples requiring some free exposure, multiple-sub costs vs. one-time antenna), while directly answering pressure on ignition and existing rules. Side A’s voluntary-market and revenue points were countered by noting that successful enclosures still blend free sparks, and mandates target only a floor. A’s clipped turns reduced completeness and impact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 answers on boxing timeline and geographic reality, which crystallized why PRO’s engine claim holds and voluntary examples actually support the floor.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): PRO (Side B) built the stronger path to victory across the full exchange. Three decisive moments stand out. First, PRO's "both-and" framing was established early and held throughout: the motion requires a meaningful share, not a full ban on subscriptions, so CON's repeated argument that mandating free-to-air "starves leagues of primary revenue" was attacking a position PRO never held. CON never fully escaped this misalignment. Second, on the enforcement question—CON's strongest pressure point—PRO answered with the UK listed-events framework and Australia's analogous system in Rebuttal 2. These are real, grounded pieces of background knowledge, and CON never engaged with them in Rebuttal 2 or the closing. That silence effectively conceded the enforcement burden. Third, the core access comparison broke in PRO's favor: income-based exclusion is structurally larger in scale than geography-based exclusion, CON's proposed remedy of tiered pricing and promotional windows was correctly identified as speculative and discretionary ("a promissory note"), and PRO's turn—that CON's own "imperfect access disqualifies" logic applies more forcefully against paywalls—was sharp and went unanswered. CON had genuine strengths: the streaming portability argument was well-developed, the EPL audience-growth point had real force, and the "symbolic access on a declining platform" framing in the closing was memorable. But CON's revenue argument never accounted for the both-and structure, and the enforcement collapse in Rebuttal 2 was never repaired. Both sides had clipped speeches (execution costs for PRO), but PRO's core arguments were already on the record before the cuts. PRO made the decisive clash cleaner and won the most important comparisons.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to CON's enforcement pressure question (A1): invoking the UK listed-events regime and Australia's equivalent framework converted CON's strongest attack into a live example of the very policy PRO was defending. CON then spent its closing on a "symbolic access vs. real investment" frame without ever engaging the UK/Australia examples, effectively conceding the enforcement burden that had been the centerpiece of CON's Q1.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): B won by making the cleaner comparative case that subscription paywalls exclude by recurring cost, while free-to-air imperfections are less central to the motion. A had a plausible revenue and flexibility argument, but relied too heavily on asserted trickle-down benefits and an overdrawn claim that streaming equals broader access. B also answered the vagueness objection with a workable regulatory comparison and kept emphasizing that the motion allows both free and paid content.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest rebuttal was turning A's access critique: if imperfect access disqualifies free-to-air, recurring subscription paywalls are worse because exclusion by income is built into the model. That exchange undercut A's main framing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = GLM-5.1 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A wins by establishing a clearer comparative advantage on revenue flexibility, access as portable flexibility versus one-time broadcast, and how marquee revenue concretely funds youth programs and community tickets rather than symbolic free access. They neutralized PRO's cultural claim and income-exclusion argument by showing streaming exclusions can be mitigated through existing tiered tools while free-to-air adds geography/schedule/hardware barriers, and by directly answering pressure on enforcement (voluntary beats mandate) and affordability (multi-platform paywalls not the only access path). Side B's strong opening on shared experience and real regulatory examples in rebuttal were undercut by not weighing the primary-revenue hit to leagues or neutralizing the on-demand superiority frame. Later rounds show A adapting to close the clash while B repeated exclusion comparisons without advancing tradeoffs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 directly opened with A1/A2 labels to answer both pressure questions on mandates versus voluntary and multi-subscription affordability, then reframed the entire access metric to portability and revenue-funded services, which damaged PRO's core income-exclusion route more than any other exchange.
