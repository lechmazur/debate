CON's pressure questions forced PRO into a damaging concession (A4.2) that life-saving coastal flood barriers and transit modernization would have to be frozen until the tripled baseline is met, and CON's second rebuttal and closing exploited that concession to show the policy is self-defeating: it risks paralyzing exactly the kind of urgent capital projects PRO's own logic treats as non-deferrable. PRO's attempt to soften this by claiming the budget gate "can clear within one budget cycle" is a plausible patch but sits in tension with PRO's own earlier claim (A2.5) that scaling maintenance requires phased procurement, weakening the rebuttal's coherence. CON's economic feasibility argument (absorptive capacity, procurement inflation) was never fully neutralized, and CON kept the debate anchored on the practical consequences of the rigid sequencing the motion demands, which is the more decisive comparative frame.

Absorptive-capacity and diminishing-returns replies landed cleaner than PRO’s same-cycle workarounds, leaving CON the clearer reason to reject the proposition as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 1–2 assault on B2.7/B4.1: relabeling mega-replacements as remediation either rewrites the motion or admits the freeze cannot hold, while still stranding modernization behind an arbitrary triple.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): A built a vivid, emotionally compelling case around invisible chloride corrosion and the Morandi collapse, and cleverly used CON's "scarce labor" concession to argue for a moratorium. B's closing crystallized this cleanly: PRO's own bottleneck concession undercuts its remedy, turning it into an argument for risk-ranked triage rather than a blanket freeze-and-triple policy. That is the more decisive, unresolved point in the debate.
