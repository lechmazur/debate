### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases and engaged the actual clash rather than talking past each other. B's "tax the demand, not the message" reframe was rhetorically sharp and exposed a real weakness in A's model: the need to classify "routine" vs. exception invites litigation and may push demands into unpaid hidden channels. A answered this credibly by shifting the evidentiary/justification burden onto the employer and noting that compensation claims face the same proof problem. The decisive moment came in the Pressure Questions exchange: A asked whether B's compensation model would still let employers reward off-hours responsiveness in promotions even when paid. B honestly answered "it would not ban" that reward, merely asserting that payment removes the "coercive core." A exploited this concession forcefully in Rebuttal 2 and the closing, showing that pricing availability does not remove the psychological/career pressure to be responsive — the very harm B promised to cure. B never fully rebutted this; its closing reasserted "a wage makes it not free" without explaining why paid responsiveness stops functioning as a competitive signal for promotion. Because B's own concession undercuts its central selling point ("disconnection without career penalty"), while A's entitlement-vs-payment distinction survived scrutiny, A ends the debate with the cleaner, more damaging final position, even though B's litigation/hidden-channel critique remained a live, only partially answered concern.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's exploitation of B's admission ("it would not ban rewarding responsiveness") in Rebuttal 2 and Closing was the most decisive exchange — it turned B's own compensation-based alternative into evidence that the career-pressure arms race persists even when replies are paid, directly undermining B's core promise.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO won a competitive debate by keeping the decisive clash on whether refusal is genuinely safe from career penalty. CON’s pay/schedule alternative and hidden-channel critique were sharp, but PRO exploited CON’s admission that rewarding paid off-hours responsiveness would still be allowed, showing the availability arms race can survive pricing alone. PRO better framed default non-solicitation plus arranged coverage as the cleaner entitlement than after-the-fact wage claims, while both sides stayed disciplined and format-compliant.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO’s closing and R2 use of CON’s “would not ban rewarding responsiveness” answer: pay taxes interruptions but still leaves relative commitment pressure intact unless routine after-hours asking is itself constrained.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by successfully shifting the debate from the desirability of boundaries to the most effective mechanism to enforce them. Side A's alternative of strict compensation and logging proved more legally practical and economically sound than Side B's speech restrictions. Side A convincingly argued that banning messages would drive demands to hidden, unpaid channels and create litigious gray areas around what constitutes "routine" contact, while making employers pay for after-hours time removes the coercive "free" availability that fuels the arms race. Side A's rhetoric was also sharper and more memorable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where it dismantled Side B's enforcement mechanism by showing that logging hidden texts for wage compensation is practically easier and safer for workers than screenshotting them to report a statutory violation, effectively neutralizing Side B's defense against the hidden channels argument.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides argued well, but B's central move—showing that PRO's own best defense (the "impossible morning deadline" scenario, raised via pressure questions) is solved not by regulating communication but by limiting required labor and hours—was never cleanly answered. PRO's closing conceded that the employer must "arrange coverage or let the deadline slip," which is precisely CON's proposed mechanism (workload/hours enforcement), not a unique benefit of a communications rule. Since PRO's strongest scenario (silent pressure with no explicit after-hours message) falls entirely outside the scope of a "right to disconnect," CON successfully showed the motion's core mechanism does less independent work than claimed, while its own costs (administrative rigidity, sector-exception mismatch) remained live. PRO had good openings and real-world grounding (Portugal/France), and its burden-shifting argument (employer must prove assessment isn't based on protected silence) had some force, but CON neutralized it by noting the same logic applies to workload enforcement. CON's progression across rounds was sharper and more decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 exchange ("Why? The employee can still work unpaid to deliver by morning" at B2.1, later reinforced in B4.7/B5.2) directly dismantled PRO's claim that suppressing after-hours messages makes extraction "no longer free," forcing PRO into a concession that the real protection comes from labor/hours limits rather than communication regulation.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won the decisive incremental clash: PRO’s strongest promised benefits (internalizing after-hours cost, ending free extraction, forcing realistic staffing/deadlines) are actually obligation/workload/rest rules, not a communications boundary. CON repeatedly showed the silent impossible morning deadline survives inert messages, that enforcement fear is shared, and that PRO’s own “core hours + rotas” compliance path risks the flexibility loss CON flagged. PRO framed power and recovery vividly and answered pressure cleanly, but never separated the unique value of the added rule from tools CON already endorsed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s sustained attack on B2.5/B4.3—“extraction no longer free”—showing an impossible daytime-set morning deadline is controlled by required-labor limits, not message rules (A2.1–A2.2, A4.7, A5.2).
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON successfully exposed the central logical flaw in PRO's case: PRO's solution to "silent" overwork (impossible morning deadlines without evening messages) relied entirely on enforcing limits on required labor and working hours, which was CON's own model. CON sharply demonstrated that a communications rule does nothing to stop a manager who simply assigns an impossible workload during the day, rendering PRO's claimed asymmetry and unique advantage void.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where it dismantled PRO's answer to the "impossible morning deadline" pressure question, showing that PRO's mechanism was just standard workload enforcement in disguise and failed to address silent coercion.
