### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON (A) built a disciplined distinction between "safety approval" and "market-entry terms," and used it consistently to justify proportionate, symmetric-in-principle conditions (change-triggered reassessment, standardized disclosure, plain labeling) rather than blanket exemption. PRO (B) leaned heavily on a "protectionism/double standard" frame but repeated it without deepening it, and crucially never resolved the tension A exposed in Rebuttal 2/Closing: B asserted cultivated meat's environmental superiority as a "guarantee" while simultaneously rejecting the disclosure that would verify that guarantee. That inconsistency went unanswered through B's final rebuttal and closing. B also effectively conceded A's labeling proposal ("cultivated chicken" as objective descriptor), undercutting its own "punitive labeling" attack. Both sides handled the Pressure Questions round with proper A1/A2 formatting, but A's answers more precisely clarified its framework (evidence-envelope trigger for reauthorization) while B's answers largely restated assertions rather than engaging the cell-line/tissue-production distinction A raised. A's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly ("safety opens the door; it does not settle every condition of entry") while B's closing mostly recapped the protectionism framing without landing a fresh blow. Overall, A progressed the debate more effectively and left the decisive inconsistency in B's case unresolved.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 point that "PRO has converted a plausible advantage into a 'guarantee,' then invoked that guarantee to reject verification" [A4.7] was the most damaging exchange — it exposed a live self-contradiction in B's environmental argument that B never squarely answered in its final rebuttal or closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON showed that safety approval answers edibility, not regulatory equivalence, and that distinct production systems justify proportionate distinct conditions—process-tied authorization and finite environmental disclosure—without constituting protectionism. PRO's case assumed cultivated meat's environmental superiority and treated any additional requirement as protectionism, but CON demonstrated these requirements serve legitimate inspectability purposes and that conventional meat's regulatory blind spots are flaws to correct, not standards to extend. PRO's stronger rhetoric could not overcome CON's sharper rebuttals and superior crystallization.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal exposing that PRO assumed cultivated meat's environmental conclusion ("guarantees a drastically smaller footprint") to argue against verification, directly undermining PRO's central causal story.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins by effectively weaponizing the proposition's "same basic terms" requirement against Side B's proposed asymmetrical burdens, specifically pre-market environmental audits and bespoke reauthorizations. Side A consistently and persuasively framed Side B's demands as a double standard that protects incumbent factory farms, a vulnerability sharply exposed by Side A's pressure questions. While Side B logically argued that conventional meat's regulatory blind spots shouldn't be replicated, this defense was comparatively weak against Side A's argument that holding a novel, potentially cleaner technology to a uniquely high standard stifles innovation and acts as textbook protectionism. Side A's rebuttals cleanly defended the adequacy of existing food safety triggers and maintained the offensive, whereas Side B remained trapped on the defensive regarding the asymmetrical nature of its proposed regulations.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's sustained attack on the environmental disclosure clash, where A highlighted the structural thermodynamic efficiency of cultivated meat and hammered Side B's admission that conventional meat enjoys a regulatory "blind spot," effectively framing Side B's bespoke audits as protectionist barriers to entry.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): B built a clean, consistently maintained distinction between technical stringency (which can legitimately vary by hazard) and legal/market status (which should track demonstrated risk, not production method). This framework let B absorb every safeguard CON proposed while still winning the motion's core claim. CON's strongest material—the tumor-cell analogy and monopoly-capture worry—was vivid but never converted into a rigorous causal link: B repeatedly asked what specific additional harm survives after safety and labeling requirements are met, and CON's answers (A4.1–A4.2) were largely assertive relabeling ("structural deception," "freeride on public trust") rather than a demonstrated mechanism, especially since patents and market concentration exist independent of food-category classification. B's pressure questions exposed this gap precisely, and CON's rebuttal in Turn 8 mostly reasserted the same claims rather than closing it. CON's rhetoric and framing were strong and creative, but the underlying logical structure had an unresolved leap from "different production process" to "permanently inferior legal status" that B named and pressed throughout without ever being neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Turn 7 move (B4.4): "does a distinct technical standard require a distinct legal handicap?"—crystallizing the debate's central fault line and forcing CON to identify a residual harm it never clearly supplied.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won by successfully narrowing the debate to a decisive question that CON failed to answer: what concrete harm remains after cultivated meat meets rigorous, process-specific safety standards and is honestly labeled? CON argued that biological novelty and patent monopolies require permanent legal separation, but PRO showed that different technical monitoring fits within "same basic terms" without justifying unequal market access, and that a separate food classification would not actually curb patents or market concentration. CON repeatedly invoked "structural deception" and "category error" but never established why equal legal status plus accurate labeling would mislead consumers or threaten public health once safety was assured. PRO's pressure questions exposed this evasion, and their closing crystallized that CON's permanent firewall ultimately protects incumbent suppliers from informed consumer choice rather than protecting the public.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 and Pressure Questions exchange, where PRO distinguished between different technical standards and permanently unequal legal handicaps, forcing CON to defend a "firewall" that blocked substitution by safe, labeled competitors without identifying any residual harm that justified unequal access.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A wins by establishing a much more reasonable and textually grounded interpretation of "same basic terms" as equal regulatory principles (the bargain of proving safety, accepting oversight, and truthful labeling) rather than identical technical tests or agricultural subsidies. Side A effectively dismantles Side B's argument that a separate legal classification is necessary to prevent monopolies, exposing it as protectionism for incumbent meat producers that fails to actually address IP or antitrust issues. Side B relies too heavily on repetitive semantic arguments about the physical differences between bioreactors and pastures, failing to overcome Side A's core point that different hazards simply require different specific controls under the same overarching regulatory framework.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they decisively answer Side B's pressure questions by clarifying that specialized inspection protocols do not justify permanently unequal market access, and that accurate labeling ("cultivated meat") prevents consumer deception without requiring a separate, protectionist legal handicap.
