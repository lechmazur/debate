### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON built a cleaner, more disciplined tradeoff frame (units built vs. units that never exist) and sustained it with a plausible causal mechanism (marginal projects die on small margin increases) supported by an intuitive analogy (contingency funds are earmarked, not slack). PRO's macro case—retrofit costs 10x more, universal design benefits everyone—was compelling but leaned on an unsupported and suspiciously precise "1–3% maximum impact" statistic in Rebuttal 2, which reads as invented precision rather than grounded reasoning, weakening its epistemic credibility exactly when it needed to rebut CON's central mechanism. CON's answers to pressure questions (earmarked buffers vs. individual retrofit feasibility) were more directly responsive than PRO's somewhat hedgy answer on cancellation thresholds. PRO's strongest unaddressed point—that landlords, not tenants, control retrofit decisions—was a good counter that CON didn't fully engage, keeping this closer than a blowout, but CON's tighter framing, more disciplined evidence use, and emotionally sharper closing edge out the win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 "you also cannot house a wheelchair user... but you cannot retrofit a building that was never constructed" exchange, sharpened by CON's contingency-fund analogy in Rebuttal 2, most directly tested whether "modest" costs are truly absorbable—PRO's reliance on an unsupported 1–3% statistic in response left this exchange favoring CON.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by consistently grounding the debate in the stark reality of the housing shortage. Side A's rebuttal explaining how construction contingencies actually work effectively neutralizes Side B's attempt to dismiss the financial tradeoff. Furthermore, Side A successfully leverages Side B's concession on the "9 vs 10 units" tradeoff, pointing out that an inaccessible unit still provides vital shelter for someone, while an unbuilt unit houses no one.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 explanation of contingency buffers cleanly dismantled Side B's claim that developers can simply absorb new mandates without killing marginal projects.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side B built a much stronger and more grounded case, effectively dismantling Side A's claim that developers could simply absorb modest cost increases through contingency buffers. Side B's framing of the core tradeoff as "built vs. unbuilt" rather than "accessible vs. inaccessible" was highly persuasive, and their closing rhetoric sharply highlighted the immediate human cost of reduced housing supply. Side A's concession in Rebuttal 2 that a 10% project cancellation rate was acceptable severely damaged their case, as losing a tenth of new housing supply in a crisis is a massive, not "small," loss. Side B won the weighing decisively by prioritizing immediate shelter over idealized, unbuilt stock.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's explanation of how construction contingency buffers actually work in Rebuttal 2 (A1), which completely neutralized Side A's primary defense against the claim that modest cost increases kill marginal projects.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO (B) built a tighter, more consistent case: the "you don't avoid the accessibility cost, you shift it onto disabled/injured tenants later at 3-4x price" argument was introduced early and never squarely answered by CON. CON's central weapon—supply collapse from marginal cost increases—remained largely hypothetical and unquantified, while PRO countered with a concrete (if unsourced) "<2% supply impact" figure that CON's closing failed to engage with directly. PRO's obsolescence rebuttal ("wide doors don't become obsolete") was sharp and CON's rejoinder (citing energy codes and smart-home tech) didn't actually rebut the specific claim about geometric features. CON's pressure question about the $15 "contradiction" was answered coherently by PRO (developer margin thresholds vs. tenant affordability are distinct), while CON's own answer to PRO's ACL tradeoff question ("unacceptable personal tragedy, but a statistical reality," bolstered by a weak ambulance analogy) effectively conceded the human cost without neutralizing it. Execution-wise, CON's Opening, Rebuttal 1, and Rebuttal 2 were all clipped for exceeding word limits, a repeated real cost that undercut its ability to fully develop and defend its supply-side case. PRO's closing crystallized the clash cleanly ("real harm now vs. imaginary unbuilt homes"), while CON's closing largely restated assertions without answering PRO's specific rebuttals.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 1 point that "you do not avoid the cost, you just shift it onto the disabled tenant... three times as much five years later" was the decisive exchange—CON never found an answer to this framing, and it remained the load-bearing comparison through closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B won decisively by effectively neutralizing Side A's primary arguments and maintaining strong, evocative framing throughout the debate. Side A struggled with time management, resulting in three clipped speeches, and failed to adequately address Side B's core point about temporary disabilities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 dismantling of the obsolescence argument by contrasting "basic geometry" with smart-home tech, and Side B's A1 comparing accessibility to fire codes.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Tencent Hy3 Preview (high) (CON)): Side A won by effectively contrasting the immediate, tangible harms of inaccessible housing with Side B's hypothetical supply reductions. Side A's rhetoric was sharper, framing developer profit thresholds as a policy choice akin to fire codes rather than an immutable law of nature. Side B suffered significant execution costs by being clipped in three separate speeches, and their analogies (like the ambulance comparison) failed to account for the universal, everyday utility of the design features Side A highlighted. Side A's closing crystallized the clash perfectly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the supply argument in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, reframing developer IRR thresholds as an arbitrary market rule and contrasting "imaginary unbuilt homes" with real, present human pain.
