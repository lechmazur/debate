### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by distinguishing community voice from an effective local veto and consistently weighing localized harms against national energy costs, grid congestion, and carbon obligations. Side A offered a compelling social-license case, but never established that every strongly opposed project could be replaced affordably or that consultation with mandated benefits becomes meaningless without veto power. Its reliance on generalized sabotage and backlash claims, plus nonexistent reference IDs, further weakened its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s conditional-versus-absolute opposition dilemma was decisive: conditional concerns can be addressed through consultation and mandated benefits, while absolute refusal cannot be negotiated away and therefore forces a choice between local veto and national objectives. Side A effectively conceded that its model lets a parish block a project and answered mainly by proposing costlier alternatives elsewhere.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Side B successfully framed the local veto as an expensive form of minority rule that stalls national carbon targets. Side B's pressure questions forced Side A to concede that alternatives like widespread undergrounding or shifting entirely to offshore/solar would cost consumers more. Side B capitalized on this by showing that maintaining the veto transfers massive costs to the general public. Side A's reliance on the threat of 'sabotage' and hallucinated reference tags weakened its final stance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which reframed Side A's defense of 'blocking the cheapest shortcut' by pointing out that this 'shortcut' is actually the cheapest electricity for 30 million households, heavily shifting the economic weighing to Side B.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Side A built a much stronger comparative case by grounding their arguments in the real-world consequences of local vetoes, such as the UK's onshore wind stagnation since 2015, reliance on imported gas, and grid constraint payments. Side A effectively framed the local veto as minority rule that imposes massive systemic costs on the national majority. Side B's arguments about sabotage and political backlash were more abstract and less persuasive against A's concrete economic and climate impacts. Side A also won the pressure question exchange by exposing the high costs of B's preferred alternatives (undergrounding, offshore) and forcing B to concede that absolute local opposition would permanently block the cheapest energy sources.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side B's "coercion" and "cost" pressure questions by pointing out that normal planning processes already handle compulsory purchase, and that the macroeconomic costs of the veto (gas imports, constraint payments) vastly outweigh local litigation costs.
