### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): B wins a clear but competitive debate. A’s best line was that screening may redirect capital and expertise through neutral investors or consulting channels, making the policy an imperfect and costly tool. But B more convincingly reframed investment as “smart money”: equity stakes, board roles, prestige, operational mentorship, and network access are not easily replaced by generic neutral capital. B also effectively argued that the standard is not perfect containment but meaningful friction in high-stakes dual-use sectors, and that refusing to screen because substitutes exist would make export-control logic incoherent. A’s regulatory-sprawl and boomerang concerns were plausible but less developed and somewhat outweighed by B’s security-focused comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to A’s fungibility point was the decisive exchange: B argued that capital may be substitutable, but Western venture-backed expertise and incentives are not, then tied that directly to why screening creates meaningful friction rather than needing a perfect seal.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A wins by exposing that screening fails to block fungible neutral capital or expertise channels while guaranteeing regulatory sprawl and boomerang effects; Side B never resolves the substitution problem despite identifying real threats.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal 2 (A4) directly answering pressure questions and dismantling the loophole claim by showing neutral-country substitution.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON)): CON wins by exposing a structural mismatch between PRO's identified threats and proposed remedy. PRO established that dual-use technology transfer is dangerous, but CON showed capital screening is leaky: capital is fungible across borders and expertise travels through non-investment channels. CON's two-condition frame—requiring Western capital to be both uniquely irreplaceable and immune to substitution—crystallized the decisive mechanism question PRO never squarely answered. PRO's powerful export-control loophole analogy collapsed against CON's distinction between physically interceptable goods and easily rerouted capital, leaving CON's cost-benefit framing as the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (B4), which deployed the two-condition test to show PRO's mechanism failed if substitute capital and expertise could simply reroute around screening, while dismantling PRO's export-control analogy.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully dismantling the causal mechanism of the PRO case. PRO built a strong initial frame around "smart money" transfers and a regulatory loophole compared to physical export controls. However, CON consistently demonstrated that global capital is inherently mobile and instantly substitutable: blocking Western funds simply routes investment through neutral-country vehicles, leaving the rival's indigenous development funded. CON further neutralized PRO's expertise-transfer claim by showing that knowledge flows through consulting, conferences, and advisory fees that capital screening does not intercept. CON's comparative weighing was decisive: the policy imposes certain costs (economic displacement, regulatory sprawl backed by the historical expansion of export controls, and accelerated adversarial self-sufficiency) while delivering only cosmetic, easily bypassed friction. PRO's retort that security is about "friction" and that neutral capital is "second-tier" failed to answer why functionally equivalent funding wouldn't still achieve the rival's strategic goals. Despite a clipped final sentence, CON's closing crystallized the mechanism gap cleanly and controlled the decisive clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's dismantling of the "loophole" analogy: sharply distinguishing physical export controls (which work because chips cross interceptable borders) from capital screening (where blocked money instantly reroutes through neutral jurisdictions), proving the policy merely changes the investor's name on the cap table without stopping the funding.
