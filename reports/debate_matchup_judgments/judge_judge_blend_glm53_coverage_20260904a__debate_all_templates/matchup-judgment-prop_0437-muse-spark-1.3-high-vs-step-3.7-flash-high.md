### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-matched cases, but B's framing fit the motion's actual ask more cleanly: the resolution already concedes some residents will prefer the old rules, so B's task was to show flexibility with regulation is the better tradeoff, which it did with a memorable reframe (a parking spot serves one owner at a time; a dining platform serves hundreds) and concrete regulatory fixes (fee-funded inspection, hours, revocation) that directly answered A's accessibility and enforcement worries. A landed real, undischarged points—especially the distinction between permanent, permitted obstruction and fleeting, unpermitted 2019-era clutter, and the equity harm of removing scarce parking for carless low-income households—but its ultimate ask (near-total rollback) never squarely answered B's sharpest pressure question (why rollback rather than funded enforcement) beyond a speculative claim about bureaucratic capture. A's clipped opening is also a real execution cost, cutting off its own closing argument in the first speech. B's closing crystallized the clash more cleanly and left fewer loose threads, giving it the more resolvable path to victory, though the margin is not large because A's core equity and permanence arguments were never fully neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's reframing in [B2.1]/[B4.1]-that a parking spot serving one car is less "shared" than a dining platform serving many-forced A onto the defensive about what counts as public use, and A's counter in [A2.2] (permanent permitted obstruction vs. fleeting unpermitted clutter) was the sharpest surviving point for CON, but B's Q1 pressure question exposing the rollback-vs-enforcement tension was never fully answered by A.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by defending regulated flexibility rather than unrestricted dining and by consistently comparing the intensive public benefits of curb dining with the highly exclusive baseline of storing individual cars. Side A identified legitimate access, noise, and enforcement concerns, but often treated remediable violations as unavoidable structural harms. Its claim that disabled residents would still face blockage under perfect enforcement contradicted the proposed requirement that all equipment and queues remain within the platform. Side A also relied on unsupported precise parking claims, overstated parking as universally free and accessible, and incurred a clipped-opening cost. Side B gave the cleaner motion-specific reason to preserve flexibility while penalizing harmful operators.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer to the accessibility challenge: wheelchair blockage was conceded as unacceptable, then separated from lawful outdoor dining through clear-path rules, platform-contained equipment, proactive inspection, and permit revocation. Side A never established why such enforced conditions necessarily leave sidewalks blocked, weakening its main route to rollback.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A successfully reframed the debate by contrasting the exclusivity of private car storage with the high-capacity, economically productive use of outdoor dining. Side A provided concrete, plausible policy mechanisms (fee-funded proactive inspection, parklets in the parking lane) to address Side B's accessibility and enforcement concerns. Side B's defense of on-street parking as "shared use" implicitly conceded Side A's point that it only serves car owners, and its reliance on cynical assumptions about city bureaucracies was less grounded than Side A's structural solutions. Side A's rhetoric and clash management were sharper throughout, making the comparative choice much clearer.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 1 and 2, where it flipped the "privatization" charge by exposing that free on-street parking is the most exclusive use of curb space, and dismantled the accessibility argument by pointing out that pre-pandemic sidewalks also had clutter and that parklets can be designed to keep the pedestrian path clear.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): A (CON) built a tighter, more textured case: the infrastructure/access framing let it turn B's own moves against it. When B analogized dining fences to bus stops and newsstands, A cleanly distinguished "open to all" from "paying customers only," neutralizing that analogy. When B touted wheelchair-height tables as an accessibility win, A exposed the sleight of hand—accessible seating inside a fence does nothing for the pedestrian blocked outside it. A's "timing gap" argument (harm to a blocked wheelchair user is immediate; complaint-based enforcement is retroactive, if it happens at all) was the debate's sharpest point and went essentially unanswered—B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing simply repeated "manageable, mitigable downsides" and "financial incentive to comply" without engaging the immediacy problem. A also directly answered B's pressure question about alternatives (rent relief, fee cuts, faster permits) in Rebuttal 2, but B's Closing ignored this and repeated the "no workable alternative" claim as if unaddressed—a real execution lapse that let a decisive rebuttal go unrecognized. B's opening had real substance (small-business survival, tax revenue, safety), but its later speeches mostly recycled the same three claims rather than adapting to the specific pressure A applied, leaving A's route to victory (basic access and livability rights outweigh optional commercial seating, given weak real-time enforcement) largely intact at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 1/2 exposure of the accessibility "sleight of hand" and the enforcement "timing gap"—wheelchair users and sleepless neighbors face immediate harm while B's complaint-based enforcement is necessarily retroactive—was never adequately answered by B, who kept repeating that clear zones are "strictly enforced" without addressing the night-time enforcement vacuum A specifically raised.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by making universal access and enforceability the decisive comparison. It directly answered Side B’s pressure, distinguished clear passage from car storage, and explained why delayed complaint enforcement cannot remedy immediate obstruction. Side B offered real economic and accessibility benefits, but repeatedly relied on unsupported claims about exact clearances, successful city programs, and thousands of closures, while calling reserved commercial seating fully public.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal was decisive: it answered both questions upfront, rejected the parking-versus-dining false choice, and turned Side B’s complaint-based enforcement model by contrasting immediate mobility harms with delayed inspections.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Step 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's core mechanisms, particularly on enforcement and accessibility. B's sharp distinction between the immediate physical harm of a blocked sidewalk and the delayed, reactive nature of complaint-based enforcement ("the harm is now; the enforcement is later") decisively neutralized A's mitigation strategy. B also effectively countered A's accessibility claims by highlighting the difference between accessible seating and an accessible path, and dismantled the "privatization" defense by contrasting public transit infrastructure with exclusive, pay-to-sit dining. Side A's case relied too heavily on the assumption of strict enforcement and conflated curb parking with sidewalk walking paths, leaving B with the cleaner, more compelling comparative case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's rebuttal to Side A's enforcement and accessibility claims in Rebuttal 2, specifically the point that a wheelchair user blocked at 10 p.m. cannot eat a future inspection, and that accessible tables inside a fence do not fix an inaccessible path around it.
