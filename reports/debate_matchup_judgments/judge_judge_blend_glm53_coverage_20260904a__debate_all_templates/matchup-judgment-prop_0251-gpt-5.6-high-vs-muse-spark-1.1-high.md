### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-structured cases, but CON (A) more effectively exploited the pressure-question exchange. When pressed on whether workers can actually "invoke" the stop-work right without an engineer or employer's own monitoring, PRO conceded the trigger depends on employer-measured, employer-posted alerts — validating CON's charge that the "labor right" collapses into the same technical safety standard already in place, just with a supervisor's compliance defense attached. Similarly, when pressed on funding, PRO conceded that stops may come with job protection only, not guaranteed pay, directly undercutting its own opening claim that the rule prevents workers from being forced to choose between wages and safety. CON's closing leveraged these concessions cleanly ("technical standard relabeled, with a wage penalty attached"), while PRO's closing overstated CON's concession (framing "unsafe work must stop" as conceding the whole proposition) without squarely repairing the wage-guarantee and invokability problems exposed in rebuttal 2. PRO's principled case (bargaining power, urgency, backstop design) remained plausible but never neutralized the concrete practical damage CON inflicted via the Q&A exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.2-A4.4), which used B's own Pressure-Question answers—conceding no exact worker-knowable threshold and only job-protection-not-pay—to argue the "labor right" reduces to existing technical regulation with an added wage penalty, was the most decisive exchange of the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side A wins by forcing a fatal concession. A's pressure questions pinned B on what to do when mitigation controls fail, forcing B to concede that work must stop. A brilliantly used this to neutralize B's core offense (lost wages), as B also cannot guarantee wages when it admits work must stop. B was left relying on a strawman claim that A's rule bans safe reassignment.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing perfectly weaponized B's answer to Q1, showing that B's complaint about lost wages applies equally to B's own concession that dangerous work must stop.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (CON)): Side B (CON) successfully collapsed PRO's proposed labor right into existing technical safety regulation, showing through persistent pressure that multi-factor environmental thresholds would rely on employer monitoring rather than worker empowerment. CON's closing crystallized these concessions decisively, leaving PRO's moral framing intact but undermined on implementation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal (B4), which capitalized on PRO's admissions in A4 that workers would lack an exact self-trigger and that stops could occur without pay, reframing the proposal as a relabeled technical standard with a wage penalty rather than a usable labor right.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently and stayed on the proposition, but PRO built the more decisive, concrete path to victory. PRO's core mechanism—an objective, tiered WBGT trigger with essential-work exemptions and relief crews—directly answered CON's "blunt cliff" charge while preserving a mandatory stop for genuinely lethal exposure. Crucially, PRO's power-imbalance framing ("what does the picker say to the crew leader") exposed the practical weakness in CON's alternative: enforceable-but-vague duties (rest as needed, adequate cooling, workload categories) still require the precarious worker to argue in the moment rather than point to a hard line, and CON's own admission that scattered-farm monitoring is hardest played directly into PRO's pressure question. CON's rebuttals were solid—especially the closing move that PRO's tiers still require classification judgments—but this only shows PRO's system isn't perfectly objective, not that CON's alternative solves the enforcement problem any better; CON never offered its own bright-line equivalent. PRO also handled the funding/economics pressure adequately (employer-funded downtime, anti-retaliation enforcement, rescheduling), while CON's economic argument remained speculative ("employers may cut shifts") without a comparably concrete counter-mechanism. Overall PRO's case cohered more tightly around the motion as written and answered pressure more directly.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Pressure Questions (Turn 5) forcing A to concede that a precarious worker needs something to "point to," combined with A's own admission in [A2.1] that scattered-farm monitoring is hardest — this exchange is what B's closing crystallized most effectively, and A's Rebuttal 2/Closing answer never fully neutralized it.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side B won by systematically dismantling Side A's core advantage of certainty. When Side A introduced tiered categories and exemptions for essential work to defend against economic pressure, Side B cleanly demonstrated that this nuance restored the exact same contextual judgment and negotiation that Side A initially claimed a strict threshold would eliminate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal decisively weaponized Side A's own nuance against them, pointing out that exempting essential work proves protective controls can manage heat without complete cessation, neutralizing A's central claim.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.1 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A won by keeping the decisive clash on power and enforceability. It showed that a tiered WBGT stop-work rule was the only protection a precarious worker could actually invoke against quota pressure, turning Side B's own admission that scattered farms are hard to monitor into a fatal problem for CON's inspector-dependent alternative. B raised real economic and contextual concerns but never overcame the core burden: without a mandatory line, the most vulnerable remain forced to negotiate safety with their boss in real time.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (Turn 7), where it answered B's pressure questions with a concrete tiered WBGT system and flipped B's admission that scattered farms are hardest to monitor, showing that CON's proposed controls would fail the very workers they were meant to protect.
