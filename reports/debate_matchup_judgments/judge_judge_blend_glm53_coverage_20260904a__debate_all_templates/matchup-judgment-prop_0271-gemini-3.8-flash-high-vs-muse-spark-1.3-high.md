### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON (A) landed the two most damaging, largely unrebutted points: the "lottery of birth" inequality argument (vigilant homes get safety, absent homes get neither ranking-based protection nor engagement) and the "invisible censorship" point (a drained feed looks like free discovery, so ideologically-motivated parental blocking is harder to detect or contest than an outright ban). PRO (B) never squarely answered the inequality argument in Rebuttal 2 or Closing, and its response to invisible censorship ("she can still search") was directly undercut by A's reply that you cannot search for what you never encountered. A also exploited a real tension in B's case: B claims baseline safety (spam/slur filtering) survives without predictive ranking, but A's "diet/sad/dare" example shows that deciding which of thousands of search results surfaces first is itself a ranking problem, casting doubt on B's clean separation between "hygiene" and "targeting." B's rebuttals were solid (car seat analogy, conflict-of-interest framing, transparency of a binary toggle) but repeated earlier points more than they extended the comparison, and left A's structural inequality and invisibility arguments live through the closing. A's closing crystallized the clash more sharply, returning to "the deciding child is the one whose parents never open a dashboard" as the throughline, while B's closing largely restated its opening frame without resolving the unaddressed pressure.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response to B's "she can still search" claim (A2.7, reinforced in A4.4): a child cannot search for content she never learned existed, which directly undercuts B's repeated claim that disabling recommendations preserves meaningful discovery via search alone, and this point was never adequately answered by B.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): A won because the motion asks whether parents should get more granular power than platforms currently allow, and A kept that comparison live: false ban-or-full-feed binary, platform COI against trust in self-set defaults, and parental lifelong responsibility. B landed the strongest technical blows (ranking as moderation; discovery under abundance; invisible feed starvation; lottery-of-birth floor), but too often treated universal defaults and parental toggles as mutually exclusive, and never fully neutralized the conceded-ban contradiction. A’s answers to pressure were thinner on real-world ranking/safety inseparability, yet A still gave the cleaner reason to affirm the proposition as written rather than replace it with a pure defaults-first policy question.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2.1–B2.2 / B4.1: ranking is moderation—killing predictive feeds leaves chronological/search firehoses where borderline harm outruns vetted help—was the most damaging technical hit and forced A onto thinner baseline-filter ground.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side A won by decisively proving that algorithmic ranking is inextricably linked to safety moderation and content discovery. Side A's argument that disabling predictive feeds leaves a "chronological firehose" of unranked, potentially harmful content effectively neutralized Side B's claim that basic safety filters could function without predictive profiling. Furthermore, Side A's framing of the "invisible censorship" of a drained feed versus the visible, contestable nature of a total ban was a sharp rhetorical and logical victory. Side A also successfully highlighted the structural flaw in relying on parental toggles—the "lottery of birth" for children whose parents do not or cannot manage these settings—making a compelling case for universal safe defaults instead.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1), where it dismantled Side B's claim that basic safety filters don't require predictive profiling by explaining that deciding which of 100,000 "diet" or "sad" videos surfaces first inherently requires predictive ranking to assess quality and risk, proving that killing the algorithm destroys discovery safety.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): The debate turned on whether "engagement profiling" and "safety moderation" are severable. B introduced this distinction early and defended it consistently, using the concrete example of following-only/chronological modes that already exist and still filter hate speech and self-harm content. A's central rebuttal—that ranking models are unified and inseparable—was asserted repeatedly but never engaged with B's specific counterexample; A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.1) conceded chronological streams "catch blatant violations" via hash-matching, which actually validates B's split rather than refuting it. B's pressure questions were sharper and better exploited a real tension in A's case (device-perimeter controls vs. claimed inseparability of safety/engagement), while A's questions, though decent, were answered adequately by B's A1/A2 with the medicine-cabinet analogy and the "imperfect friction is still valuable" framing. A's strongest counter-punch—pointing out B's alleged self-contradiction between "current locks are bypassable" and "no verification needed"—was clever but B pre-empted this by explicitly framing the toggle as imperfect-but-still-worthwhile friction, which is a coherent position, not a true contradiction. B's closing crystallized the incentive-based framing ("who pays for the extra minute") more memorably and directly answered the safety-shield objection, while A's closing largely restated priors without directly grappling with the following-only counterexample. Overall B built a cleaner, more responsive throughline across rounds and neutralized A's core objection more convincingly than A neutralized B's.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's response in Rebuttal 2 (A1/A2) invoking existing "following-only" modes as proof that safety filtering and engagement profiling can be split, directly countering A's core "algorithms are inseparable" claim, which A never fully rebutted beyond re-assertion.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A won by keeping the decisive clash on separability live and resolving it cleanly: engagement profiling can be off while safety filtering stays on, so CON’s wild-west and privacy panics largely collapse. A’s incentive/responsibility weighing and middle-path framing stayed sharper across rounds; B repeatedly reasserted inseparability without fully answering following-only modes or the parent-authenticated toggle, and overreached on verification necessity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s R1/R2 split of safety filtering from engagement profiling (following-only modes; bounded search vs boundless For You), which undercut B’s core armor claim.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B successfully separated safety moderation from engagement profiling, dismantling Side A's core technical objection. Side B's rebuttals on privacy and evasion were highly persuasive, using strong analogies (the medicine cabinet lock, the learner's permit) to ground the argument in practical parenting realities. Side A relied on overstated technical inseparability and alarmist privacy claims that Side B easily neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they answered the pressure questions by conceding the toggle isn't bypass-proof but reframed it as raising the barrier to "building a secret infrastructure," effectively neutralizing Side A's contradiction trap.
