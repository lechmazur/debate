### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON (A) built a more coherent and layered case: it distinguished the danger of a single, instantly-trusted national credential from today's diffuse, hard-to-query analog leaks (the "proves little vs proves everything" point), and it turned PRO's central selling point—revocability—into a liability by noting that a credential that can be killed everywhere also strands the victim everywhere. A's answer to the pressure question about optionality-creep (incentive-driven rot, not statutory repeal) and its "finite theft vs generative master-key theft" distinction were sharp, directly responsive, and never squarely neutralized. PRO (B) argued competently and consistently invoked "the surveillance/fraud problem already exists," but this framing under-addressed the qualitative distinction A kept pressing (centralized queryability and instant trust vs scattered, low-trust paper), and B's closing mostly restated earlier points rather than resolving that specific clash. Both sides followed the Q&A format correctly, but A's answers did more analytical work.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.2), directly answering B's pressure question by distinguishing "finite" stolen money from a "generative" stolen master credential, combined with A's later move of framing fast revocation itself as a harm (freezing the victim everywhere), was the most decisive exchange—B never fully neutralized either point.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully differentiating the centralized, systemic risks of a digital ID from the diffuse, messy risks of the current analog system. B's use of institutional incentives to explain "paper rot" effectively dismantled A's reliance on statutory safeguards for optionality.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's first rebuttal cleanly dismantled the SSN analogy by pointing out that an SSN requires cobbled forgeries, whereas a digital ID provides an instantly trusted master key, shifting the magnitude of the fraud risk.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON won by establishing that a national digital ID creates a qualitatively new and more dangerous concentration of trust than the status quo. CON successfully distinguished the current diffuse, weak SSN system from a digitally signed master key; showed through the SSN precedent that optionality erodes into de facto mandate through economic incentives; and argued that building a queryable verification index creates permanent surveillance infrastructure. PRO's framing that the status quo already concentrates harm and surveillance was initially powerful, but CON's rebuttals and pressure answers demonstrated that scattered analog messiness actually limits systemic exploitation, whereas a unified digital credential expands both attacker payoff and state surveillance capacity. PRO's defenses—statutory paper guarantees and revocability—were blunted by CON's historical example of functional creep and the argument that revocation freezes victims everywhere while paper rots. The final clash on whether the "index already exists" broke in CON's favor because CON's closing clearly distinguished between unqueryable photocopies and a live digital proof system, leaving PRO's comparative weighing unpersuasive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's distinction between the current scattered analog identity mess and a unified digital master key—arguing that photocopies and SSNs cannot be queried live and prove little without forgeries, while a national digital signature proves everything instantly and creates a searchable trail. This rebuttal neutralized PRO's core claim that concentration and surveillance already exist, and it set up CON's closing that "convenience can be repealed, an indexed society cannot."

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but CON built the more resilient throughline. CON's core move—"optional in law becomes mandatory in market," anchored by the SSN history—was raised early and never fully neutralized; PRO's answer (legal bans, required alternatives, auditors) was asserted rather than shown to overcome the very market dynamics CON cited as having already captured the SSN. CON's second load-bearing point, that correlation across "yes/no" checks at a bank, landlord, and clinic still builds a linkable dossier even without a central database, directly undercut PRO's credit-card analogy; CON's distinction ("a card authorizes money from one account, this credential authorizes who you are, everywhere") was a sharp, undamaged rebuttal. PRO's strongest material—current system already broken, revocation beats permanent SSN bleed, access gains for rural/disabled users—remained compelling but didn't resolve CON's central claim that the fraud/access gains and the surveillance/breach harms are the same event (near-universal adoption), which CON crystallized cleanly in closing. PRO's pressure questions were good but CON's answers, while conceding ground on the SSN, pivoted effectively to "this credential follows the same incentive path," which PRO's rebuttal 2 answered only abstractly. Overall CON's rebuttals did more damage to PRO's core defenses than vice versa.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 response to the credit-card analogy ("A card authorizes money from one account. This credential authorizes who you are, everywhere") combined with the "three yeses" correlation argument was the most decisive exchange, since it directly defeated PRO's central design-based reassurance that ubiquity does not imply centralization.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by strictly maintaining the comparative against the status quo, effectively proving that a "temporary freeze" of a digital token is vastly preferable to the "permanent bleed" of a stolen SSN. Side B struggled to explain how a cryptographic "yes/no" ping is a worse surveillance harm than the current system's proliferation of physical documents and SSNs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's distinction in Rebuttal 2 that a stolen phone opens one window before cancellation, whereas a stolen SSN opens doors forever (temporary freeze vs. permanent bleed), neutralizing Side B's attack on revocation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON successfully argued that the fraud reductions and access gains PRO needs require the very universal adoption that itself creates the surveillance spine and concentrated breach target, undermining PRO's attempt to treat harms as merely contingent. CON's rebuttal that cryptographic identity proofs are linkable across contexts—unlike credit cards—collapsed PRO's decentralization analogy, while CON's historical precedent of SSN function creep weakened PRO's assurances that "optional" would remain so in practice.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's distinction between credit-card ubiquity and identity-credential ubiquity in Rebuttal 2, showing that a reusable national proof creates a clean, linkable signal across banks, landlords, and clinics, thereby collapsing PRO's claim that scale of use does not mean scale of surveillance.
