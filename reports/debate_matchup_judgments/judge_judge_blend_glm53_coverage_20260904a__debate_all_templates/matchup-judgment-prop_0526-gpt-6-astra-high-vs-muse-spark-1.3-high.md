### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Both sides built sophisticated, well-structured cases and answered pressure questions directly, making this a genuinely close debate. B's opening move — "comparable integration does not mean comparable contribution" — was the sharpest structural point of the debate, and its fairness/birth-lottery and chosen-vs-imposed framings were original and rhetorically crisp. But A's tu quoque on aggregation (thousands of fiscal gains vs. thousands of disrupted childhoods) blunted B's strongest quantitative claim, and A's dependency-based definition of family priority gave a workable, largely unrebutted answer to B's chain-migration worry. Decisively, under pressure-question A1, B conceded that a "merely modest fiscal edge" does not justify years of parent-child separation — a concession that validates A's core principle (relational harm can outweigh ordinary economic advantage) in the paradigm case, even though B's closing tried to cabin it as a narrow humanitarian floor rather than a general rule. That concession, combined with A's cleaner answers on scope and its effective symmetry rebuttal on aggregation, gave A the more resilient path through the final exchanges, even though B's framing remained lively and never fully collapsed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's aggregation rebuttal in Rebuttal 2 ("thousands of disrupted childhoods... selective accounting, not stewardship") directly neutralized B's strongest structural claim that modest fiscal edges become decisive in aggregate, forcing B into the more strained chosen/imposed distinction to recover.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO won a competitive debate by tying the motion’s actual wording—rebuttable priority, not unlimited entitlement—to living residents’ relational stakes, irreparable separation harms, and incomplete economic scores. CON’s scarce-goods and chosen-vs-imposed framing was sharp and often memorable, but the late attempt to shrink the live dispute to only young-child cruelty, plus the claim that any public-need exception collapses priority, never fully neutralized PRO’s cleaner decision rule: ordinary fiscal edge does not automatically outrank reunification when integration is comparable. PRO’s pressure forced CON’s costly parent-child concession and answered chain-migration limits more cleanly than CON resolved the line between shortage and “ordinary revenue.”
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A2.2’s claim that exceptions surrender family priority vs B4.5/B5.3’s severity-and-likelihood rebuttable-priority reply, which preserved the motion without treating every tax gain as decisive.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully arguing that the resident family member's relational interests are a legitimate public interest, dismantling Side A's attempt to silo the resident's identity into "taxpayer" (public) and "family member" (private). Side B effectively exploited Side A's concession of a "humanitarian floor" to show that economic return is not an absolute trump card, and defended a principled framework for weighing severe public harms against ordinary fiscal advantages. Side A's "chosen vs. imposed" argument was strong, but their dismissal of spousal separation as mere "queuing" weakened their case against Side B's emphasis on irreplaceable time and the state's duty to its existing residents.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 and Closing, where they dismantled Side A's "humanitarian floor" concession, showing that if a modest fiscal edge cannot justify separating a parent and child, then economic optimization is not the sole metric of public interest, and that separating spouses is an irreplaceable loss rather than mere "queuing."

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B built the more analytically disciplined case. Its opening reframe—distinguishing "comparable integration" from "equal claim on scarce admissions"—set up a clash A never fully escaped. Crucially, B caught A in two unresolved tensions: (1) A counted family's ancillary benefits (unpaid childcare enabling parental work, household crisis-absorption) as "extra, non-fungible legitimacy" while dismissing the nurse's healthcare contribution as mere fungible "staffing," an asymmetry A never justified; and (2) A's own guestworker critique of temporary, insecure status was undercut when A later offered temporary visas/training as the fix for economic shortages, which B explicitly flagged. B's dependency-based principled line (protect spouses, dependents, and genuine caregivers without a blanket family-first rule) gave a cleaner, more limiting answer to the pressure question about where the line falls than A's vaguer "family goes first because denial destroys a shared life." A's rhetoric ("rent workers vs. keep members") was vivid and its mechanisms (rootedness, retention, democratic access via sponsorship) were plausible, but its closing reasserted values without repairing the double-counting and consistency problems B had already exposed. B's closing dilemma—if alternatives to economic admission work, little is lost by not prioritizing family instead; if they don't, calling the need "fungible" is empty—crystallized the clash more decisively than A's closing recap.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (B4.1, B4.4) catching A's asymmetric treatment of childcare vs. healthcare benefits and A's contradiction between criticizing guestworker insecurity yet relying on temporary visas as the fix—left unresolved through A's closing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): CON kept the decisive burden clear: comparable integration removes one objection to family admission but does not establish general priority over scarce places that serve other residents' needs. PRO showed real family goods and belonging stakes, yet never proved why core protections must expand into a ranking that displaces economic selection when stakes are contested. CON's opportunity-cost frame and refusal of fungibility-as-proof decided the clash.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2/closing pressure that alternatives (pay/training/temp visas) must actually meet the need, and that resident care claims are not mere roster-filling versus an irreplaceable bond, neutralized PRO's fungibility move.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A wins by sharply distinguishing between the integration of a migrant and the public utility they provide. While Side B argues that equal integration makes the economic case a tie, Side A successfully demonstrates that a skills-selected nurse fulfills an urgent public need that a backlogged sibling does not. Side A also effectively dismantles Side B's claim that economic needs are fungible through temporary visas or higher pay, pointing out that these alternatives are not guaranteed to work and that patients bear the cost of shortages. Side A's framework—protecting serious dependency while allocating remaining spots based on actual public stakes—provides a much cleaner and more rigorous decision rule than Side B's categorical preference for kinship.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal to the fungibility argument in Rebuttal 2 and the Closing, pointing out that merely naming alternatives like temporary visas or higher pay does not guarantee the public need is met, and that patients are also members whose welfare must be weighed.
