### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B ran a tighter, more logically disciplined case throughout: the "whack-a-mole" mechanism (removing the jersey logo doesn't reduce total gambling exposure, which flows through ads, odds graphics, and affiliate content the league doesn't control) was never fully neutralized by A. B also turned A's own alcohol analogy against them (the real-world remedy is statutory broadcast codes, not unilateral refusal) and exposed a genuine unresolved threshold problem. The decisive moment came in A's closing, which conceded that broadcast licensing and sponsorship control are "identical where it matters" — intended to collapse B's leverage argument, but B's closing correctly flipped this into "identical protection with less money," which is a clean, devastating summary of the whole exchange. A's case relied heavily on the normalization/endorsement mechanism, which is plausible but never demonstrated to change behavior beyond assertion, and A's own concession in the final speech undercut its central pillar. Compounding this, A's Opening, Rebuttal 1, Rebuttal 2, and Closing were all flagged as clipped for exceeding word limits, meaning the judge never saw A's full argument four separate times — a real execution cost that repeatedly cut off A's momentum right where clarification was needed.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's closing move turning A's own admission ("identical where it matters" between broadcast licensing and sponsorship) into "identical protection with less money" — this crystallized the entire debate's arithmetic in B's favor and left A's normalization harm claim unsupported by any distinct policy benefit.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): B won a close, high-quality policy clash. A landed real damage on endorsement potency and the “only leash” claim via broadcast rights, but never closed the loop that jersey refusal measurably improves youth outcomes once ecosystem ads remain. B kept the decisive comparison clear: same limited control at best, certain revenue loss, undefined threshold, and the alcohol model pointing to uniform rules rather than unilateral league refusal. A’s repeated clipping and weak threshold answer raised execution cost; B adapted more cleanly through the leash exchange and closing flip.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s pressure that broadcast licensing already supplies the “leash” without endorsement, and B’s Reb2/closing reply that sponsorship uniquely extracts money from bookmakers while “identical control, less money” fails as child protection.

Side A’s cost-benefit ledger—unproven behavioral benefit against certain lost revenue for youth programs—remained live because Side B never established that jersey endorsement is the decisive causal driver of youth gambling nor adequately answered why the remedy should be a unilateral league refusal rather than uniform broadcast regulation (the alcohol model). Side B’s execution was further hampered by word-limit violations that clipped every major speech. Side B landed the valid point that endorsement carries distinct weight, but without proving that removing it changes outcomes while the rest of the ecosystem floods children with gambling content, Side A’s comparative case that the policy is a “press release” that “sends them the bill” proved more durable.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s saturation argument in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, which showed that even if broadcast licensing offers comparable in-broadcast control (Side B’s challenge), neither sponsorship nor broadcast restrictions reach the broader digital ecosystem, so refusing sponsorship merely forfeits bookmaker revenue and youth-program funding without reducing the actual volume of gambling impressions children receive.
