### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): The decisive clash centered on the word "achievable." B built a strong comparative case (two-state beats one-state and status quo) and landed a sharp early rebuttal exposing tension in CON's confederation alternative (if trust is too low to draw a border, it's too low to integrate). But under pressure, B conceded "no current coalition is ready" and could only offer "compounding costs" as a vague forcing mechanism toward moderation. A seized this concession directly, noting that pressure has no built-in direction (it can produce annexation or radicalization as easily as compromise), and correctly reframed the burden: PRO, not CON, must show its endpoint is achievable, not merely less bad in the abstract. A also blunted B's trust-asymmetry attack by arguing partition itself requires extensive simultaneous concessions (Jerusalem, refugees, settlements, borders) plus ongoing cooperation, and that confederation/rights-based steps can be modular rather than requiring instant deep integration. B's closing reasserted the alternatives-are-worse framing but never answered the burden-shift point or repaired the "no coalition, only hope" critique. Both closings were clipped, an execution cost shared by both sides, but A's rebuttal 2 did the more decisive work in the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 (A4.3-A4.4), which reframed the burden of proof onto PRO and directly exploited PRO's pressure-round concession that "no current coalition is ready," arguing that unbearable pressure does not reliably produce moderation rather than radicalization.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won by making the decisive comparison clearer and more consistent: under CON’s own low-trust premise, one-time territorial separation plus international buffers is more viable than modular confederation or rights hybrids that still require deeper ongoing coordination, shared institutions, and politically harder demographic accommodations for Israelis. B kept the motion’s “least-bad achievable” framing alive by treating alternatives as worse (civil war or permanent second-class status) and by using structural pressures rather than immediate coalitions as the path. Side A landed real damage on the absence of present coalitions and the security-sovereignty contradiction, and its modular alternative was original, but it never fully escaped the contradiction B repeatedly pressed, and clipping on opening/closing hurt execution. The full exchange left B’s separation-vs-integration route easier to vote on.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s repeated trust contradiction (if border partition is too hard, confederation/shared arrangements are harder; settles land-swaps as less impossible than dissolving Jewish-majority status) across Rebuttal 1, Pressure Qs, and Rebuttal 2.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the two-state solution as the only structurally viable separation mechanism that preserves mutual self-determination, while dismantling Side B's confederation alternative through the trust paradox: if populations are too polarized to agree on borders, they cannot sustain the deeper daily cooperation confederation demands. Side B landed strong blows on the security-sovereignty contradiction and political achievability, but failed to establish that its modular alternative was more realistic under pressure, and its clipped opening and closing carried execution costs. Side A's ownership of the "least-bad" framing absorbed imperfection critiques and proved more resilient across the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's trust paradox rebuttal in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2—arguing that separation minimizes daily friction while confederation requires boundless perpetual trust—proved the most damaging exchange, as Side B never fully resolved why polarized populations could sustain shared institutions if they could not sustain a border.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued the resolution as written and executed the pressure-question format correctly, but B built and sustained the more decisive comparative frame. B's four-constraint test and its "asymmetry" move (if enough leverage exists to force Israel into binational equality, that same leverage more plausibly forces border/settlement compromise while preserving Israeli statehood) directly answered A's core "physical impossibility" claim and was never fully neutralized—A's Rebuttal 2 reasserted a legal-vs-physical distinction rather than explaining why one-state institutional mechanics (military, immigration, refugee policy, constitutional identity) are more tractable than negotiated swaps. Meanwhile A's strongest rhetorical point—that the two-state framework is a "shield" enabling annexation—was squarely rebutted by B (B2.6: abandoning the goal is "at least as likely" to normalize annexation without equality), and A's later speeches largely repeated the shield claim without engaging that counter. A's pressure answers (federalized power-sharing, minority vetoes) were vaguer than B's phased-sovereignty and boundary-treaty answers, leaving A's alternative under-specified on its own achievability. B's closing crystallized the clash cleanly; A's closing mostly restated prior claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's asymmetry argument in Rebuttal 2 (B4.4-B4.5)—turning A's "immense leverage" claim for one-state transformation into a reason partition is more achievable—was the pivotal exchange, and A's response (reasserting a legal/physical dichotomy) did not fully dislodge it.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO)): PRO built the cleaner comparative case that two states remain least-bad and more achievable because they divide authority and preserve each side's core national project while requiring bounded compromise; CON's physical/security objections were sharp but never neutralized PRO's decisive asymmetry point that converting unequal control into consented binational institutions asks more (military control, immigration, identity) of both distrustful publics than negotiated borders and phased security. PRO adapted better across rounds, answered pressure honestly on statehood and settlers, and crystallized the clash without late widening.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 asymmetry (if pressure can force full Israeli renunciation of exclusive sovereignty for one state, it more plausibly forces withdrawal/swaps that preserve Israel itself) squarely damaged CON's achievability route and was never fully overcome.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.6 Sol (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON)): Side A won by exposing a structural asymmetry in achievability that Side B never resolved. While Side B mounted a vivid case that geographic entanglement makes partition physically daunting and that the two-state framework serves as a diplomatic smokescreen for ongoing occupation, Side A showed that one state requires overcoming the exact same Israeli ideological resistance plus a far deeper political revolution: dissolving exclusive Jewish sovereignty, merging security forces, and settling refugees, immigration, and constitutional identity inside a single polity. Side A’s pressure questions revealed that Side B had no credible institutional answer for who controls the military or borders in a shared state, offering only abstract labels like “federalism” and “minority vetoes.” Side A crystallized the decisive comparative test in the closing: if sufficient political pressure exists to force Israelis into a binational state they reject, the same pressure can more plausibly produce negotiated borders and withdrawal while preserving Israel’s national existence. Side B’s smokescreen argument and geographic impossibility case remained live but could not overcome the burden of showing that one state is actually more achievable rather than merely more morally urgent. The debate was competitive and well-argued on both sides, but Side A built the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s asymmetry argument in Rebuttal 2 (Turn 7): if sufficient international pressure and political change can compel Israel to accept equal binational citizenship, then that same pressure can more plausibly compel evacuation, land swaps, and withdrawal while preserving Israel’s national state—a bounded transition rather than a wholesale dissolution of sovereignty.
