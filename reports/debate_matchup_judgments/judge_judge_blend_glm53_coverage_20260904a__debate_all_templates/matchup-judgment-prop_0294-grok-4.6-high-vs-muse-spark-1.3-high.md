### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-organized cases and engaged directly with pressure questions in proper A1/A2 format. B's opening case (rotating myths, plural/revisable review) was strong, and its pressure question forced A to concede that a board ordering denial of a documented massacre is an "abuse" not an "exercise" of citizen authority. But A recovered that concession cleanly by drawing a durable line between fabrication (a floor already policed by courts, press, competing publishers) and framing/emphasis (the "ceiling," which is the real site of most textbook fights). A's repeated point that "reporting the split" still requires editorial choices about which split leads, which verb opens, whose grief is photographed first, was never squarely defused by B — B's charter/staggered-terms answer to "who appoints reviewers" actually reinforced A's "gavel moved one floor up, but harder to fire" critique, since panel errors run for more cohorts with recourse only in journals rather than at the ballot box. Because A closed last, A got the final, unanswered word restating this floor/ceiling distinction and the "power with better camouflage" line directly against B's closing "child can argue with a text that reports the split" move. That sequencing plus the unresolved "selection is still a choice" critique gives CON the cleaner, more resilient path through the debate, though the margin is not large given how sharp and well-matched both sides were throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's rebuttal 2 line that B's charter-based reviewer appointment "just moves the gavel one floor up, where voters can change the building but never the chapter" was the most decisive single exchange: it exposed that B's "independence" still rests on political appointment while sacrificing the fast electoral correction A had already conceded was necessary, and B never fully neutralized this before A restated it uncontested in the final closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side A strategically traps Side B in the pressure questions and leverages the concessions perfectly in the final two speeches. By forcing Side B to admit that outright fabrication is an "abuse" of citizen authority corrected by courts and the press, Side A proves that evidence fundamentally overrules political control. Side A then convincingly argues that it is better to proactively apply this evidentiary constraint via scholarly review than to wait for post-hoc checks after the children have already been taught a politically distorted narrative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal and closing masterfuly capitalize on Side B's admission that boards cannot order outright lies. Side A uses this to establish that evidence already overrides the vote, making proactive review superior to reactive outrage.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): PRO (Side B) won by demonstrating that democratic board control allows partisan capture through omission and framing, not merely outright fabrication. CON limited legitimate checks to elections and courts after the fact, which provided no safeguard for students taught misleading emphasis in the interim. PRO's framework—plural, revisable scholarly review that reports competing interpretations rather than crowning a single moral winner—held up better under pressure. While CON landed strong blows on the impossibility of fully neutral expertise, PRO successfully argued that evidence-based plurality is preferable to campaign-based capture, and crystallized the distinction by showing CON's own concession that evidence can overrule a board-ordered massacre denial undermined the claim that only citizens may authorize historical narratives.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over CON's "fabrication only" standard (A4.2, A5.1) versus PRO's argument that systematic omission and framing constitute the real harm of political capture (B4.4-B5.4), where PRO showed that CON's narrow definition of falsity would leave most partisan textbook distortion untouched.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued with real sophistication and honored the pressure-question format cleanly. A's sharpest weapon was collapsing B's fact/meaning distinction — showing that page-count, sequencing, and "first sentence" choices are themselves narrative selection, not neutral fact-checking — and this landed as a genuine dent in B's case. But B's incentive-and-correction mechanism proved more resilient under pressure: B offered a concrete institutional answer to "who appoints/removes reviewers" (multiple nominating bodies, staggered terms, removal only for fraud), used the civilizing-mission example effectively to show scholarly revisionism, not electoral turnover, actually broke a long-entrenched false narrative, and forced A into a costly concession — that "no system unteaches a lie already given" — which undercuts A's own central remedy (removability) just as much as it undercuts B's stability claim. A never fully explained why scholarly capture is as bad as electoral capture, only asserted it, while B's case for why scholarly checking reduces the rate of error in the first place (rather than merely allowing later correction) went comparatively unanswered. That asymmetry in how well each side's core mechanism survived scrutiny tips a close, high-quality debate to B.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's [A4.4] move ("the panel decides what evidence students must see... that is exactly independent review selects the record") most sharply exposed the fragility of B's fact/meaning split, but B's [B4.1]-[B4.4] answer to the appointment/removal pressure question, paired with the civilizing-mission correction example, did more lasting damage by forcing A into the "no system unteaches a lie already given" concession that weakens A's own removability-as-remedy argument.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully collapsing Side A's distinction between "facts" and "meaning." Side B effectively argued that the selection of facts, page counts, and headings inherently creates the narrative, making it a subjective civic act rather than an objective record. Side B also leveraged Side A's own mechanism (protecting reviewers from being fired over historical interpretations) to prove that such a panel would lack democratic accountability when academic orthodoxies fail.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4's takedown of A4's panel mechanism, pointing out that insulating reviewers from being fired over a "verdict on empire" explicitly creates the unaccountable capture B warned about, and that deciding which evidence students see is exactly how narratives are chosen.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): PRO exposed a structural asymmetry that CON never solved: children cannot vote, so electoral accountability arrives too late to prevent miseducation. While CON landed sharp blows on the inseparability of fact and narrative, PRO defended a concrete, transparent mechanism—mixed nomination, staggered terms, published footnotes and dissents—that limits capture better than a 5-4 school board can. The decisive clash resolved in PRO’s favor: a reversible political lie still miseducates the child who learns it, whereas a footnoted scholarly process, however imperfect, points toward checkable truth rather than applause.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B2’s argument that elections are terrible fact-checkers and that scholarly evidence, not political generosity, overturned entrenched orthodoxies like the civilizing-mission narrative.
