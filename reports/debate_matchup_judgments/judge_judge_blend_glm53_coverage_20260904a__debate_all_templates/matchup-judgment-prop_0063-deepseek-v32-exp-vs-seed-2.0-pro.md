### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate for three reasons that survive the full exchange. First, PRO's epistemic discipline is consistently better: its key supporting claims—Miami's porous limestone geology, non-linear tipping points, permafrost collapse, the impossibility of restoring vanished coastlines—are grounded in widely known, plausible facts and mechanisms. CON (Side A) repeatedly leaned on suspiciously precise numbers ("cut flood risk by nearly 40% in just 12 years," "30-50 years of lead time from the most conservative projections," "a mandatory relocation plan published just three months after the storm") that read as brittle fabricated precision in a closed-book debate. The rules require discounting that kind of specificity, and it materially weakens CON's two most-used arguments. Second, PRO's Rebuttal 2 answer to the binding/flexible contradiction—that the mandate binds the process and milestone accountability, not a pre-ordained outcome—is the single most decisive move in the debate. It separates "mandatory planning" from "mandatory retreat," directly neutralizing CON's core logical attack, and CON's closing never recovers a clean answer to it. Third, PRO identifies and holds a genuine gap in CON's alternative: insurance mandates and building codes may slow incremental risk but do not create the inland infrastructure, equitable land swap frameworks, or phased community transition capacity that long-term planned retreat requires. CON never answers this gap. CON's justice argument and resource-diversion point have real force, and the Lower 9th Ward example is rhetorically effective. But both depend on the contested and suspiciously specific claims about what actually happened in New Orleans, and PRO's rebuttal—that chaotic post-disaster processes, not proactive inclusive planning, generate that kind of injustice—is at least equally plausible. The closing stalemate on justice means PRO retains the stronger live route: even CON's best example does not defeat the motion as written.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2, answering CON's Q1 by distinguishing that mandatory planning binds the process and creates enforceable milestones, not a pre-ordained retreat outcome. This directly neutralized CON's central contradiction charge (binding vs. adaptive) and reframed mandatory planning as a continuous risk-assessment framework that preserves both the option to stay and the capacity to leave—the argument CON never squarely defeated in the closing.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): Side A won by establishing a cleaner comparative case: mandatory planning is either redundant (if flexible) or locks in avoidable displacement and diverts mitigation funds (if binding). The Lower 9th Ward example and Miami data were used effectively to ground this, and A answered the pressure questions directly while exposing B's internal contradiction. B repeated the "chaos vs. planning" binary without neutralizing the flexibility problem or offering a mechanism that avoids the harms shown.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2: directly answered both pressure questions, crystallized the redundancy/harm tradeoff, and turned B's dual-track claim against itself using the existing New Orleans evidence.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = DeepSeek V3.2 (PRO)): CON (Side A) won by exposing a persistent, unresolved tension in PRO's case: mandatory long-term relocation planning must either be binding enough to force action—which diverts critical funds from mitigation and locks in avoidable displacement—or flexible enough to adapt to successful mitigation, which makes it redundant beside existing risk assessments. CON effectively turned PRO's justice argument with the Lower 9th Ward example, showing that formal mandatory plans can codify unjust displacement rather than prevent it, and answered PRO's voluntarism concern with concrete alternative mechanisms (risk-based insurance, building codes, and mitigation funding). PRO's closing never escaped the redundancy-or-coercion trap, leaving CON's path to victory live after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 treatment of the Lower 9th Ward and the flexibility dilemma. CON rebutted PRO's claim that post-Katrina injustice stemmed only from chaotic disaster response by demonstrating that a formal mandatory relocation plan explicitly rejected the community's cheaper mitigation proposal, directly proving that mandatory planning can legally enshrine unjust displacement. CON then sharpened this into the "redundant or coercive" trap that PRO never resolved.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO (Side B) held the stronger position across the full exchange for three main reasons. First, PRO anchored the debate to the right comparison: not whether relocation is pleasant, but whether planned relocation beats unavoidable chaotic displacement. CON never fully escaped this framing. CON's best answer was "fix voluntary programs and adapt in place," but PRO's recurring point—that two-thirds of the most vulnerable households are already locked out of both tracks—was never adequately answered. CON said "reform those programs" without explaining how that actually reaches trapped low-income households before the next disaster hits. Second, PRO's neutralization of Rotterdam and Tokyo was effective and durable. CON introduced these as proof adaptation works but never recovered from the rebuttal that these are among the wealthiest cities on earth and inapplicable to cash-strapped coastal communities. Third, PRO answered the funding question more concretely by redirecting disaster recovery funds currently spent on repeat rebuilds—a plausible mechanism CON called a "fantasy" without real engagement. CON's strongest live argument—that merely announcing a relocation plan triggers an economic death spiral—was partially answered by PRO's distinction between unphased Alaska-style plans and their tied-announcement-to-support-package model. PRO's answer was somewhat abstract, but CON's rebuttal that "hope keeps investment alive" was thinner still. Both sides used moderately brittle precision (the "8x" and "two-thirds" figures), so no decisive penalty either way. CON's rhetorical work on agency and dignity was genuine but never translated into a practical path for the most vulnerable households PRO kept foregrounding. PRO made the debate easier to decide by consistently returning to the core inevitability argument and the equity gap, which remained the cleaner route to the motion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's response in Rebuttal 1 to the Rotterdam/Tokyo example was the most decisive exchange. CON introduced it as strong evidence that adaptation works, but PRO's sharp counter—that those cities have access to tens of billions in infrastructure funding unavailable to low-income flood-prone communities—was direct, plausible, and effectively ended that line of argument. CON never returned to it with a meaningful response, leaving a gap at the center of their adaptation case.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side B wins because it built the clearer comparative case: voluntary programs leave the vulnerable trapped (supported by repeated examples), adaptation is infeasible for most at-risk places, and phased mandatory planning avoids exactly the chaos already occurring post-disaster. Side A never neutralized the two-thirds statistic or the cost comparison and retreated into abstract agency claims.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4 directly answering both pressure questions with A1:/A2: labels and tying answers back to live examples of post-disaster scattering and funding priorities.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = DeepSeek V3.2 (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): PRO wins by establishing that for poor, climate-vulnerable communities, the real alternative to orderly mandatory planning is not successful adaptation but chaotic, unsupported displacement. CON's reliance on Rotterdam and Tokyo collapsed under PRO's wealth-based distinction, and CON never provided credible examples of affordable adaptation for low-income areas. CON's counterproposal to fix voluntary programs remained vague, while PRO supported their case with plausible mechanisms, cost comparisons, and the equity argument that the most vulnerable are already trapped. PRO directly answered CON's pressure questions about Alaska blight and underfunding by distinguishing their phased, compensated model and proposing reallocated federal disaster funds, whereas CON's Rebuttal 2 resorted to abstract appeals to hope and agency that did not overcome PRO's concrete framing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's limitation of CON's adaptation examples to wealthy cities and direct neutralization of CON's Alaska planned-retreat comparison in Rebuttal 2, which left CON without feasible alternatives for poor communities.
