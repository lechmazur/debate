This framing, introduced in Rebuttal 1 and sustained into the closing, gave CON a live route to victory that PRO never squarely neutralized. CON's pressure question Q1—demanding concrete triggers, beneficiary classes, and spending limits—directly exposed PRO's definitional gap. PRO's answers in Rebuttal 2 were partially credible (time-limited payouts, employer-matched vouchers), but CON immediately turned these answers against PRO: the trigger language cannot reliably distinguish AI-caused layoffs from recession, outsourcing, or ordinary churn; and if the vouchers are employer-matched and demand-guided, that is ordinary workforce policy, not an AI displacement package, so it does not prove the motion. This dilemma—either PRO's plan is general resilience (conceding CON's model) or it is AI-specific (and faces false targeting)—was never resolved. PRO's strongest arguments—the lag-time problem and the cost-asymmetry framing—are genuinely compelling and kept the debate competitive, but CON's rebuttal that "conditional readiness" (pre-drafting legislation, piloting, strengthening existing systems) already addresses the lag without precommitting to a national AI-specific benefit structure was a credible answer that PRO failed to undercut. Minor execution costs for PRO include referencing nonexistent reference IDs [C1.6, C2.3] in A4.4; CON's clipped Rebuttal 1 is noted but the core arguments survived in later speeches. CON's closing cleanly crystallized the live clash rather than introducing new material, while PRO's closing restated the speed argument without answering the false-targeting problem or the conditional-readiness alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Pressure Question Q1 (demanding concrete triggers, beneficiary class, and spending cap) followed by CON's Rebuttal 2 response [B4.3]: PRO's own answer—payouts triggered by "involuntary job loss in exposed cognitive sectors"—either sweeps in ordinary recession and outsourcing layoffs or requires an impossible causal inquiry into whether AI specifically caused each dismissal. ### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON)): Side A won on the two most decisive clashes. First, when CON finally defined "definitive proof" in Rebuttal 2 as "sustained, statistically significant structural unemployment," Side A's closing correctly crystallized why that definition is self-defeating: by that threshold, the damage is already done and workers have already absorbed the shock. CON never answered this timing problem. Second, Side A's pressure question Q2 directly exposed CON's persistent misdescription of wage insurance. Wage insurance pays displaced workers only after they accept a lower-paid new job—it is mechanically an incentive to move, not a subsidy to stay. CON's Rebuttal 2 answer (it pads compensation in low-value roles and keeps workers in dead-end jobs) simply restated the misdescription without engaging the mechanism, leaving the rebuttal live. Side A's "lagging indicator" framing held throughout, and its asymmetric risk argument—modest shock still benefits from readiness, severe shock punishes delay irreversibly—was never squarely neutralized. Side B's strongest moments were the "funded or unfunded / press release" paradox and the retraining uncertainty point; both landed real pressure. Side A answered the funding question adequately (build administrative rails and eligibility systems now; scale payouts with actual claims) and the retraining uncertainty with modular, employer-linked, repeatedly updated design. Neither answer was devastating, but both were sufficient to prevent CON from converting those pressures into wins. CON's closing was largely repetitive and added little synthesis, while A's closing sharpened the key comparison cleanly. Side A's two clipped rebuttals impose a real execution cost and prevent a wider margin, but the shown substance was strong enough, and CON did not capitalize on the missing conclusions effectively.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Pressure Question Q2 and the follow-through in Rebuttal 2 constitute the most decisive exchange. By asking why wage insurance—paid only when a worker leaves and accepts a lower-paid new job—counts as a subsidy for declining sectors, Side A forced CON to defend a mechanical misdescription. CON's answer restated the claim (it pads low-value roles) without engaging the mechanism (you must have already left the old role to collect). This left CON's core wage-insurance objection permanently weakened for the rest of the debate.
- **Grok 4.3** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B wins because it more convincingly framed preparation as scalable readiness that lowers transition costs and directly answered the timing pressure on definitive proof, while Side A repeatedly overstated fiscal distortion without neutralizing the lagging-indicator problem or the concrete human costs of delay.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 directly answered both pressure questions with the readiness distinction and market-movement clarification, exposing the all-or-nothing flaw in CON's case.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = Gemini 3.5 Flash (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) secured the win by consistently framing unemployment data as a lagging indicator, which made CON's wait-for-proof standard inherently reactive and costly. PRO's asymmetric risk weighing (preparation yields useful transition tools even if the shock is modest; waiting risks irreversible damage if the shock is real) was decisive and never fully answered. CON raised coherent concerns about fiscal drag and training uncertainty, but PRO effectively neutralized them by clarifying that preparation means building scalable administrative rails and short-cycle, employer-linked training rather than blank-check entitlements. CON repeatedly mischaracterized wage insurance as a subsidy that traps workers in declining sectors despite PRO's clear, repeated explanations of its function as a mobility bridge. CON's late pivot to relying on existing safety nets was underdeveloped against PRO's point that current systems are not designed for rapid mid-career white-collar displacement. PRO's comparative weighing, policy mechanics, and risk framing were sharper and more responsive throughout the exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Rebuttal 2 decisively dismantled CON's false dichotomy between massive permanent subsidies and doing nothing by introducing the readiness frame (comparing policy rails to fire departments). It directly answered CON's pressure on funding and training feasibility while exposing the core timing vulnerability in CON's case: waiting for definitive proof means improvising bureaucracy after damage has already spread.
