### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON (Side A) built a tighter, more consistent throughline: peak demand is pinned by school calendars, weather, and festivals, so "flexible" travelers redirected to off-peak or alternative districts are additional trips, not substitutions, while iconic hubs stay just as full. CON also exploited a real structural weakness in PRO's case—the "willing neighborhoods"/safeguards-first promise runs into a sequencing problem, since promotion and short-let conversion typically outrun protective rules. PRO's opening was solid and pre-empted the "just clustering more" critique with a managed-redistribution framework, but under pressure PRO progressively narrowed its claim to a "flexible portion" and, when pressed for a concrete share of demand actually being redirected, explicitly admitted "there is no defensible universal percentage" and that Saturdays "may remain fully booked." CON seized on this in Rebuttal 2 and Closing, arguing PRO had effectively conceded that most tourism keeps funneling into the same districts, leaving PRO defending peak-rationing plus marginal off-season attendance rather than the resolution's actual ask. PRO's closing tried to redefine the bar ("does not require most visitors to abandon landmarks"), but this reads as a retreat rather than a rebuttal, and CON's closing crisply capitalized on that gap. Both sides handled the Q&A format correctly, but CON's questions landed harder and PRO's answers, while direct, confirmed the weakness rather than resolving it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (A4.5): after PRO's A1 answer conceded no defensible percentage of demand actually shifts and that peak Saturdays may stay fully booked, CON directly framed this as PRO "conceding the proposition," turning PRO's own hedge into the decisive weighing point for the rest of the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON won by holding PRO to the motion’s text: the proposition demands spreading demand rather than funneling most tourism into the same districts, yet PRO conceded that iconic hubs could remain fully booked and never showed what share of demand would actually shift. CON’s “wrong remainder” frame—showing that the flexible travelers PRO targets are not the ones driving peak overload—proved decisive in demonstrating that PRO’s plan was hub management with spillover, not genuine redistribution. PRO’s inability to quantify the moved share or explain how most tourism would leave the magnets left their case as a narrower tweak than the motion required.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s Rebuttal 2 distinction between flexible off-peak travelers and inflexible peak-season families, demonstrating that shifting the former does not convert the latter and therefore leaves the core funnel intact.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by successfully challenging the core causal mechanism of Side A's case: that spreading demand actually relieves peak overload. B convincingly argues that peak tourism is structurally inflexible due to weather and school calendars, meaning off-peak promotions mostly induce new demand rather than substituting for peak crowds. B also sharply attacks A's "willing neighborhood" safeguards as unrealistic, noting that tourism flows and short-lets outpace local bylaws once a district is promoted ("a willing shop is not a willing street"). While A makes a reasonable semantic defense of the motion and correctly identifies that marginal relief is valuable, B's grounded critique of tourist behavior and policy execution provides a cleaner, more decisive path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's dismantling of A's "flexible portion" claim, arguing that the flexible travelers targeted by off-peak campaigns are not the same demographics causing the inflexible school-holiday peaks, thus exposing spreading as induced demand rather than true substitution.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): The debate turned on whether, once genuinely protective caps exist, dispersal adds anything beyond what the caps already achieve. A pressed this distinction sharply and consistently ("A high share of a smaller, controlled total is not a crush" [A4.1]), and exposed a real asymmetry in B's case: B treated regulation as sufficient protection when applied to new "secondary" neighborhoods but dismissed the same regulation as inadequate ("nicer paperwork") when applied to existing districts [A4.4]. B never resolved this tension—its closing reasserted "a high share of a smaller total is not relief" [B5.2] without new argument, essentially repeating the claim A had already targeted. B's seasonal argument (that promotion "substitutes" rather than "adds" trips) was also flagged by A as an unproven assumption [A2.4, A4.7], and B's rebuttal simply asserted substitution again without defending it. B scored real points on intensity-as-harm and boom-bust economics, and its questions in the Pressure round were sharp, but A's answers (A1/A2, properly formatted) directly and effectively neutralized them by reframing the comparison as burden-per-cap-unit rather than headcount-of-districts. Both sides met formatting requirements cleanly. A's tighter logical consistency and successful exposure of B's regulatory double-standard gives it a clear, if not overwhelming, edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's turn 4 response ("That confuses where visitors go with how many arrive at once... A square hosting most city sightseeing can still operate within a genuinely protective limit" [A2.1]) combined with the asymmetry charge in A4.4 was the most damaging exchange, and B never fully recovered from it, largely reasserting the same intensity claim in the closing without new support.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): CON successfully framed the debate around the unresolved uncertainty in PRO's core mechanism: dispersal policy assumes substitution, but PRO never established that redirecting visitors would not simply expand total tourism and create new host burdens. By showing that existing protective tools could be applied symmetrically to already-concentrated districts, CON undermined PRO's claim that spreading is necessary rather than merely different. CON's closing crystallization—that reducing tourism to tolerable levels where it is concentrated is preferable to redistributing an unproven net benefit—provided the cleaner comparative rationale.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's double-standard rebuttal in A2.3 and A4.4, pressing PRO on why housing and licensing rules would suffice in new neighborhoods but be dismissed as inadequate in existing ones, most effectively damaged PRO's comparative framing and forced PRO to rely on an asserted but unproven causal substitution.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A wins a clear and highly engaging debate by maintaining a more persuasive and memorable frame throughout the exchange. Side A successfully characterized Side B's "containment" strategy as merely policing a manufactured crush and designating original residents as permanent "burden-bearers." While Side B mounted a very strong logical challenge regarding a regulatory double standard—asking why housing and cap rules would protect dispersed neighborhoods but fail in concentrated ones—Side A effectively answered this by arguing that structural conversion (souvenir monocultures, short-term rentals) persists as long as a district remains the primary funnel, regardless of the cap. Side A's rhetoric ("containment is not kindness," "shuttered kitchens," "holding pen") made the stakes of urban tourism policy much more vivid, and its weighing of intensity versus geographic spread ultimately provided the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to Side B's pressure on the regulatory double standard, arguing that even with strict caps, funneling the majority of visitors into one district structurally converts it into a visitor economy, whereas dispersal creates a lighter, manageable presence that does not recreate a sacrifice zone.
