### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-supported cases, but A's rebuttals did more damage to the live clash. A's central mechanism—that camera-induced harms (witness silence, juror self-censorship, lawyer theatrics for a mass audience) are structurally invisible to acquittal-rate statistics—survived B's repeated demand for empirical proof, because B never explained how such harms would even register in the metrics B cited (Florida's stability, federal pilots). More decisively, A turned B's "full archive corrects distorted clips" argument by pointing out that virality rewards outbursts, not context, and forced B to retreat in Rebuttal 2 from "the public gets informed" to "journalists, defense counsel, and appellate courts get informed"—a narrower claim that undercuts B's own opening thesis about democratic transparency for ordinary citizens. A also exposed a real gap in B's witness-protection answer: ex parte sealing motions only work for witnesses savvy enough to request them, not the unlawyered tenant or coworker A repeatedly invoked. B's burden-shifting framing ("secrecy is the harder position in a democracy") and closing rhetorical turn on "surveillance of justice" were sharp, but they never squarely answered why an unfalsifiable-but-plausible chilling effect should be discounted just because it resists an acquittal-rate test. B's Rebuttal 1 being clipped before the judge saw its conclusion is a minor additional execution cost. The debate was close and both sides argued the actual motion well, but A's tactical rebuttals landed more cleanly on the decisive points.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's turn on the "archive as corrective" argument (A4.1, building on A2.5) was the most decisive exchange: it forced B to concede in Rebuttal 2 that the corrective value of full recording lies with institutional actors rather than the mass public B's opening had promised would be better informed, weakening B's core transparency rationale.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by prioritizing the integrity of verdicts and explaining concrete mechanisms through which cameras pressure ordinary witnesses, jurors, and lawyers. Side A offered safeguards and broad appeals to camera-jurisdiction experience, but repeatedly treated the full archive as an answer to viral clips without confronting that cameras create the uniquely vivid footage in the first place. Its characterization of camera restrictions as courtroom secrecy was also too sweeping. B’s harms were difficult to measure and sometimes framed too unfalsifiably, but its comparative weighing was stronger: transparency has limited value if producing it changes testimony or verdicts.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s response that a full archive feeds rather than neutralizes selective clipping was decisive. It directly answered A’s main transparency mechanism: casual audiences do not grant contextual footage equal attention, while the recorded trial supplies permanent material for viral excerpts. B also effectively showed why discretionary protection may miss unrepresented ordinary witnesses who do not know to request it.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (CON)): CON won a competitive debate by keeping the trial’s core purpose—unpressured fact-finding—live and by repeatedly showing why PRO’s remedies fail on ordinary participants: judicial shielding still forces a frightened tenant/coworker into a public ask, an unwatched archive does not equalize viral clips, and flat acquittal rates miss pre-verdict silence and audience fear. PRO’s democratic default and “no measured collapse” burden-shift were real and recurring, but A’s first rebuttal was clipped and later replies often restated the openness principle without fully neutralizing the mechanism of invisible chill. CON made the decisive tradeoff clearer: when publicity corrupts the verdict it is supposed to illuminate, fairness outranks the appearance of openness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1–B4.2: attention/virality, not scarcity, powers misleading clips, and acquittal rates miss pre-verdict chilling—directly cracking PRO’s archive-and-metrics path.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Both sides built coherent, well-organized cases, but CON (A) landed the more decisive and least-answered comparative point: the asymmetry between reversible legal harm (a bad verdict can be appealed) and irreversible broadcast harm (a face, once aired, cannot be un-broadcast, and re-identification technology already exists). PRO (B) responded skillfully to the vulnerable-witness objection by retreating from "blur/shield" language to a cleaner "cameras off entirely" carve-out, which neutralized part of A's reidentification attack, but this retreat exposed a new gap: the carve-outs only cover named high-risk categories, leaving ordinary defendants and witnesses broadcast without consent and without a comparably strong remedy. A exploited this gap directly in both pressure answers and the closing, and B never squarely answered the reversible/irreversible harm framing, instead reasserting the "checkable record" theory. A's rebuttal that "someone can check" is not the same as "someone will check" also went largely unanswered in substance. B's opening and framework (public trial as a hollow promise without cameras, sunlight disciplining bias) remained strong and consistently defended, keeping this close, and A's Rebuttal 2 was clipped for length, costing it a clean finish. But on the decisive comparative weighing that emerged from the clash, A's case held up better by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's repeated asymmetry argument (reversible conviction vs. irreversible broadcast exposure, sharpened in A2.6/A4.6/A5.4) was the most damaging unanswered thread; B's shift from "shield faces" to "cameras off entirely" in A1 of Rebuttal 2 was the most important concession, since it implicitly admitted the original carve-out design was insufficient.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B won a clear but competitive debate by showing that permanent, searchable footage creates irreversible privacy and reputational harms, while Side A's central benefit depended on someone actually reviewing the full record. Side A answered the high-risk witness objection well with camera-off carve-outs, but did not adequately resolve harms to ordinary defendants and witnesses or B's distinction between footage being checkable and being checked. B's clipped rebuttal and occasional overstatement about jurors and re-identification narrowed the margin.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's strongest exchange was the response to gavel-to-gavel context: full footage does not itself correct misleading clips when almost nobody watches it, while the broadcast remains permanent and searchable. That directly weakened Side A's main cure for decontextualization and supported B's asymmetry weighing.
- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): B won a competitive debate by converting the clash into a cleaner comparative: irreversible, non-consensual reputational and identification harm versus a mostly theoretical checking benefit. A built a coherent case for usable public access, regulated carve-outs, and falsifiability, and answered pressure cleanly by clarifying cameras-off rather than blur-and-hope. B still edged the decisive live routes: a pool camera is a permanent machine-readable record, not just more gallery scrutiny; “most” still exposes defendants, jurors, and ordinary witnesses; snippets multiply faster than patient checkers appear; and an acquittal or reversed conviction cannot un-broadcast a face. B’s Rebuttal 2 clip was a real execution cost, but the asymmetry weighing survived and was crystallized again in closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's irreversible-harm asymmetry: legal error can be remedied; broadcast identity/context stripping cannot, so the injury runs one way to non-consenting participants.
