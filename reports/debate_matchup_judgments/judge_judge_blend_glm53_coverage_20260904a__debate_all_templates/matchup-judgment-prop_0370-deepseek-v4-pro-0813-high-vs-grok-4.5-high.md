Overall B built a clearer, more consistently defended path tied to the motion's wording.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently and stayed on the motion, but PRO (Side B) built a tighter case that tracked the motion's exact language ("routine condition," "personal phones") and sharpened across rounds. PRO's central move—distinguishing the personal phone as the one device that cannot be "left behind" without losing access to banking, health, and family life, versus a company phone that can be powered off without collateral cost—directly answered CON's recurring "it's policy, not hardware" refrain and gave PRO a structural reason (not mere preference) for why device separation matters. PRO also pre-empted the "proves too much" charge by tying coercion specifically to the unique privacy stakes of the phone rather than generic job conditions like uniforms or licensure, a distinction CON never fully dismantled. CON's containerization/quiet-hours solution was reasonable but repeatedly asserted rather than proven equivalent to hardware separation, and CON's own alternative (stipended personal install) was shown by PRO to still function as the default, career-consequential path under unequal bargaining power. Both sides answered pressure questions with proper A1/A2 format, but PRO's answers did more actual repair work on its case, while CON's answers mostly restated its policy-centric framing without closing the "why does leaving the device behind differ" gap. This makes PRO's path to victory the one still standing at the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's A1 answer in Rebuttal 2 (B4.1)—arguing a company phone can be powered off or left behind without muting the rest of one's life, while a personal phone cannot—directly neutralized CON's recurring claim that device type is irrelevant and only policy matters, and CON never fully rebutted this physical-separation logic in the closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO)): Side A won a very tight debate by successfully shifting the core clash from hardware to policy. Side A effectively demonstrated that Side B's proposed alternative (a company phone) would still be subject to the same employer power dynamics and after-hours reach, thereby proving that the issue lies with employer policy rather than the physical device.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing rebuttal pointing out that leaving a company phone behind is not a real off-switch if shifts or pay depend on answering it, which neutralized Side B's strongest argument for hardware separation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Grok 4.5 (high) (PRO); B = DeepSeek V4 Pro 0813 (high) (CON)): Side A won by establishing that a personal phone is structurally different from other job requirements because of its intimate density and unavoidable always-on role in private life. Side B’s "regulate the reach" framework was persuasive but never fully dissolved A’s core claim that hardware separation creates a boundary workers actually control, whereas policy settings on a personal handset can be breached or pressured. A’s answers to B’s pressure questions—especially that leaving a company phone behind does not mute the rest of a worker’s life—sharpened the decisive clash and gave the cleaner reason to affirm the categorical "should not."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s second rebuttal (A4.1–A4.2), which answered Side B’s pressure questions by showing that physical separation creates a genuine off-switch workers control, while quiet-hours policies and containerized apps on a personal phone do not.
