  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 effectively neutralized B's core dilemma by distinguishing between seller-side gambling enforcement (licensing) and buyer-side consumer protection (dashboards), while pointing out that B's solution was just dismantling a slot machine piece by piece without the proper legal label.

PRO's crystallization that the child inside the loop does not care about the label, but the regulator with licensing power does, was decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's second rebuttal (A4) demonstrating that CON's piecemeal bans on near-misses, escalation, and dark patterns constitute "gambling regulation with worse drafting and no licensing teeth," making the dispute purely nominal while the real protective machinery disappears.

### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): Side A won a clear but competitive debate by showing that mandatory consumer protections can address odds, spending, age access, and manipulative design without gambling classification. Side B persuasively established heightened risks to minors, but never adequately proved that only gambling law could make protections mandatory or that prohibition was preferable to calibrated controls. A’s arcade and trading-card comparisons also kept substantial pressure on B’s proposed definitional boundary, though A overstated B’s limited concession about caps.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s answer that consumer-law mandates can be just as compulsory and enforceable as gambling rules decisively undercut Side B’s repeated claim that the gambling label uniquely creates mandatory protection.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): Side A won a fiercely contested debate by successfully embracing the "prohibition" aspect of gambling regulation rather than running from it. Side A's argument that "we do not cap a child into safe casino play" decisively beat Side B's alternative of consumer spending caps. Side B argued brilliantly on definitional grounds using claw machines and trading cards, but Side B's closing relied heavily on mischaracterizing Side A as having "conceded the cap stops the harm," which Side A explicitly denied.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing sequence embracing prohibition ("for minors, prohibition is the protection") effectively neutralized Side B's repeated attacks that gambling laws act as a "blunt instrument" or a total ban.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GLM-5.3 (high) (CON)): CON wins by defending a stable definitional line and exposing the cost of PRO's redefinition. PRO built a strong harm story around variable-ratio reinforcement and child vulnerability, but CON neutralized much of it by showing that degree-of-harm distinctions call for consumer regulation, not category redefinition. The arcade and trading-card parallels put PRO's limiting principle under sustained pressure, and CON masterfully extracted a damaging concession: PRO admitted a mandatory hard cap "would itself be a gambling-style control" that stops the financial harm. That concession collapsed PRO's claim that the gambling label is necessary for protection, leaving it as mere prohibition without a principled warrant. PRO's closing tried to frame prohibition as the point, but CON had already shown that prohibition inside gambling law requires an actual wager, which loot boxes lack. Across the exchange, CON sharpened the clash while PRO's route to victory remained dependent on a definitional stretch that never overcame CON's counter-examples or the cap concession.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's pressure question and subsequent use of PRO's concession that a mandatory age-gated spending cap is a "gambling-style control" that stops the harm, which proved the gambling label's only unique addition to consumer law is total prohibition and made PRO's alternative framework look unnecessary.
