Overall CON produced the more consistent, harder-to-dislodge throughline and benefited from the last word crystallizing an unanswered burden.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): B's case rested on one clean, technology-independent asymmetry — biometric identifiers cannot be reissued the way cards or passwords can — and B maintained this thread from opening to closing, successfully pivoting away from the weaker surveillance argument (partially conceded to A) toward this irreversibility point once A pressed on closed-loop hardware. A's strongest material was practical and economic (cashierless retail viability, transit throughput, the "fast lane equals coercion" contradiction), and A did force B to sharpen "convenient" into a natural-speed-vs-engineered-disadvantage distinction, which B answered coherently with an accessibility-law analogy. But A's entire rebuttal to the core irreversibility claim was that hashing/tokenization prevents raw storage — which answers a *breach* concern, not B's actual claim that the underlying bodily identifier itself cannot be replaced. A only asserted that hashes "can be revoked and reissued" in the closing speech, a new technical claim introduced too late (violating the closing's no-new-evidence norm) and never substantiated against B's point that the physical identifier itself remains exposed. B's closing correctly flagged that this central asymmetry was never engaged. A's cashierless-retail argument was also weakened when B reframed it as "a privacy tax on the poor," a reframe A never adequately rebutted, mostly repeating "no service at all" rather than engaging the equity tradeoff. Overall B controlled the framing (floor not ban) more successfully and left the decisive clash — irreversibility — unrebutted, giving B a clear, if not overwhelming, win.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 (A2) pivoting the closed-loop-hardware pressure question away from surveillance and grounding the case in technology-independent irreversibility, later crystallized in B's closing note that A never actually answered why an irreplaceable identifier should be a precondition for buying groceries.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): A won by defending the motion as a choice-preserving floor rather than a requirement of identical payment speeds. B raised real concerns about ambiguity, throughput, and closed-loop hardware, but its largest harms depended on repeatedly equating “convenient” with equally fast and non-biometric payment with staffed legacy checkout. Its claims that cashierless retail requires biometric exclusivity and that cryptographic architecture resolves immutable-identifier risk were also overstated. A preserved the stronger comparison: reduced surveillance and irreversible-data exposure at the modest cost of retaining a functional alternative.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s answer to the throughput attack was decisive: a card tap can remain convenient without matching biometric speed exactly, so natural performance differences do not require deliberately removing or degrading alternatives. This substantially weakened B’s transit-paralysis framing.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = GLM-5.2 (max) (PRO)): Side A (CON) wins by successfully dismantling Side B's (PRO) core impacts and exposing the severe practical costs of the proposition. PRO's case relied heavily on the "immutability" of biometrics—the claim that a breached face or palm cannot be reissued. Side A delivered a sharp, technically grounded rebuttal by pointing out that modern systems store salted cryptographic hashes, not raw biometrics, and that these hashes can indeed be revoked and reissued. PRO failed to engage with this technical parry in later speeches, simply repeating the irreversibility talking point in the closing. Furthermore, Side A dominated the clash over autonomous retail. When pressed on the economic cost of keeping legacy alternatives, Side A effectively explained that mandating a convenient non-biometric option in cashierless stores requires building physical checkout counters and hiring staff, entirely destroying the autonomous business model. Side B's response—that the business model simply "needs adjusting"—was a weak concession that the mandate kills the innovation. Side A's pressure questions were also slightly sharper, trapping Side B's assumptions about the low cost of legacy card readers. Overall, Side A built a more rigorous, practical, and technically responsive case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's rebuttal to the "irreversibility" argument. By clarifying that biometric systems process revocable, salted cryptographic hashes rather than storing raw physical identifiers, Side A neutralized PRO's primary emotional and security impact, a technical distinction PRO never adequately answered.
