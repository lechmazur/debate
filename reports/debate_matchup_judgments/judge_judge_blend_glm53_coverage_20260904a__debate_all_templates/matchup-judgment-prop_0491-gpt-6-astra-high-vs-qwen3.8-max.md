### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Both sides argued at a high level with genuine engagement, but CON (B) better matched the demanding burden set by the motion's word "strictly." PRO's case ultimately rested on a speculative "displacement" harm — that genetic-informed allocation could quietly redirect resources without any observable cost increase — but PRO never showed this harm was distinctively tied to genetics rather than any budget tradeoff (age, claims, hazards all involve the same allocation choices). CON exploited this directly, culminating in the closing reduction that "if that uncertainty justified banning an input, it would ban budgeting itself," which went unanswered since it was the final speech. CON's proposed alternative (ban surcharges/penalties/cost increases, require audits, needs assessments, and voluntary/clinically-valid design) was a concrete, calibrated response to the stated harms, whereas PRO's insistence that outcome regulation "can't reconstruct the counterfactual" was asserted rather than demonstrated, especially once CON offered pre-change needs assessments as a comparator. PRO's role-based distinction (employer-created hazards vs. inherited traits, documented demand vs. predictive inference) was a genuine and clever point, but CON's rebuttal that hazards too involve anticipatory, distributional choices blunted it without full resolution. On balance, CON's framing made the debate easier to decide: it isolated the actual harm (cost-shifting, penalties, access reduction) and showed a workable non-prohibitive fix, while PRO's bright-line rule remained justified mainly by enforcement convenience and abstract risk rather than a demonstrated worker injury unreachable by regulation.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 point that any budget prioritization creates winners/losers, crystallized in the closing line that treating this as ban-worthy "would ban budgeting itself," was the most damaging blow to PRO's central displacement-harm argument, and it went without a further live response since it landed in the final speech.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A wins by cleanly proving that Side B's outcomes-based regulatory alternative is unenforceable because it relies on untestable counterfactuals about what benefits would have otherwise been funded. Side B makes a significant execution error in the closing by accidentally attributing Side A's critique of the regulatory framework to Side A's own model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal and closing dismantling Side B's 'access-expanding' audit, showing that inspecting a chosen package cannot reconstruct the alternative package or prove that inherited group risk was a fair basis for allocation.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): Side A wins by proving that outcome-based regulation cannot reliably detect the core harm: employers reallocating limited benefit dollars toward genetically favored programs while other pressing needs go unmet, all without cutting any formal benefit or identifying individuals. B never solved this counterfactual enforcement gap—workers would have to prove a fairer package they never received. A also held a defensible principled line distinguishing inherited population risk from accepted inputs like claims or age, showing that clinical recommendations and hazard mitigation already suffice. The input ban provides an enforceable boundary that B’s harm-based standard cannot match.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The exchange over A’s pressure question on hidden budget displacement (A3.1, B4.1, A5.2), where A demonstrated that B’s “access-expanding” audit only measures against the status quo and cannot reconstruct the alternative, fairer package an employer forwent. This exposed a structural weakness in CON’s regulatory alternative that persisted through the closing.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Both sides argued cleanly and stayed on the motion, but CON built and sustained a sharper wedge: the universal-coverage-expansion example (using population genetic aggregates to add benefits for everyone with no profiling, no cost increase, no coercive target) that a "strict prohibition" would still forbid. CON pressed this from the opening through the closing (B1.8, B2.8, B4.5, B5.4), and PRO's main answer—"if the only reason is expanded care, genetics is unnecessary"—never actually rebutted CON's distinct point that population genetics can reveal undiagnosed/unmet need that clinical and claims data miss, so the input is not merely superfluous. PRO's closing pivoted almost entirely to the enforcement/bright-line argument and never returned to close this gap, which CON explicitly flagged as unanswered in its final speech. CON also offered a concrete regulatory alternative (review the "whole package," bar cost increases/coercive targets/access limits) that directly answered PRO's Pressure Question about detecting hidden motive, whereas PRO's rebuttal to that answer (A4.1–4.2) reasserted the bright-line's simplicity without showing why CON's package review would fail to catch the hidden shifts it worried about. PRO's structural "financial logic" argument was strong but not decisive once CON showed the same logic already operates through claims data, making a genetics-specific ban under-inclusive against the harm and over-inclusive against benefit. The debate is close and both sides argued in good faith, but CON left fewer live, unanswered threads by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's recurring pressure on the "universal coverage expansion without profiling" example (B1.2, B2.8, B4.5, B5.4) versus PRO's unresolved "genetics is unnecessary" reply (A2.1, A4.3–4.4), which never engaged CON's distinct claim that genetic aggregates can reveal unmet need clinical/claims data miss.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully co-opting Side A's enforcement mechanism. Side A argued that a strict ban is uniquely enforceable because regulators can check vendor contracts to see if genetic data was used, whereas Side B's model requires guessing motives. Side B countered effectively by pointing out that regulators could use those exact same contracts to ensure genetic data wasn't used to raise costs, eliminating Side A's uniqueness on enforcement while preserving beneficial uses.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 (A1) decisively neutralized Side A's enforcement advantage by pointing out that the exact same documents (vendor contracts, models) Side A relies on to enforce a ban are available to Side B to enforce conditional permission.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): Side A won because it neutralized CON&#39;s central cholesterol example by showing the same expansion was achievable via ordinary clinical and claims data, leaving genetic data&#39;s distinctive contribution as the actuarialization of inherited risk. A then established a decisive enforcement asymmetry: a strict ban turns vendor contracts and model documentation into dispositive evidence of violation, whereas CON&#39;s conditional permission forces regulators to reconstruct hidden motives from complex plan outputs they cannot reliably audit. B never showed why permitting the input is necessary when its benign uses are replaceable and its structural temptation to economize around genetic disadvantage is exactly what the safeguards claim to bar.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The Rebuttal 2 exchange over enforcement bright lines, where PRO showed that documentary evidence of data use is conclusive under a ban but merely permissible under conditional permission, while output review cannot reliably separate benevolent coverage additions from concealed cost-shifting or biometric targeting.
