### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): PRO built a more durable case: the analogy between mandating achievements and mandating abuses, plus concrete historical examples (Lost Cause erasure, Indigenous boarding schools, Germany's compulsory Holocaust education) gave real-world weight to the claim that discretionary/local curricula predictably erase uncomfortable history. CON's core distinction—that a mandate to "study" atrocities inevitably becomes state-scripted "moral choreography" or "compulsory guilt," as opposed to merely requiring facts—was clever but increasingly strained, especially once CON's own answer to Q1 conceded that the public may legitimately require atrocities be taught when local boards erase them. That concession functionally granted much of what the motion demands, and PRO's closing exploited it effectively, even if "conceded the debate" overstated the point slightly. CON's slippery-slope warnings about politicized pendulum curricula remained a live and reasonable concern, but never squarely outweighed PRO's asymmetry argument that inaction guarantees erasure while mandate only risks future dilution. Both sides argued with discipline and avoided fabricated sourcing, but PRO's tighter fit between argument and the actual motion text, plus sharper use of the pressure-question exchange, gives it the edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question and CON's A1 answer ("Yes—but only to protect evidence, not to script civic guilt") was decisive: it forced CON to concede the substantive mechanism the motion requires, and PRO's closing leveraged that concession to collapse CON's central distinction between "protecting evidence" and "mandating study."
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): A wins by showing that B's alternative of local professional discretion is historically disproven and that B applies an inconsistent double standard. A demonstrated that without an enforceable statutory floor, local majorities routinely erase atrocities from classrooms, and that mandating the factual record of state violence is symmetrical to existing mandates for national achievements. B's mechanism critique—that mandates invite political capture and moral choreography—was philosophically sharp, but B never overcame A's core historical evidence or explained how "evidence-based standards" would actually be enforced against local erasure without collapsing back into a mandate. A's pressure questions successfully exposed this tension, and A's closing crystallized the decisive point: democratic vigilance requires citizens to know how state power fails, and the status quo of sanitized mythmaking manufactures civic blindness.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's double-standard rebuttal in Rebuttal 2 (A4.3-A4.4), which exposed that B accepts state-mandated national achievements but treats documented state abuses as uniquely dangerous to require, undermining B's "state indoctrination" framework.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (CON)): PRO retained the core democratic-accountability and anti-erasure routes while CON conceded the key mechanism: B4.1 admits public can require facts when boards erase. A exposed the double standard that achievements are already state-mandated, so requiring documented abuses is a factual floor, not state-scripted guilt. B's capture/pendulum worry never overcame the historical evidence that local discretion enables Lost Cause and boarding-school erasure. The pressure questions from A made that concession decisive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A Rebuttal 2 (A4.3-A4.4) turning B's capture objection: showing CON accepts state-mandated glory but calls mandated factual inclusion of abuses indoctrination, and that evidence-based inquiry is impossible if evidence is allowed to be concealed.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO)): PRO built a coherent, evolving case around a "minimum floor, not a monopoly" framing that directly answered CON's central objection: that governments cannot be trusted with curricular power. PRO's pressure question exposed a real tension in CON's position—CON distrusts the state but implicitly trusts local boards, which are themselves political actors capable of quiet, unchecked sanitization. CON's answers to this pressure (localized flaws are "contestable," professional historiography without statutory mandate) largely restated the original position rather than resolving why invisible, diffuse omission is preferable to a visible, contestable national requirement. PRO's "visible vs. invisible" framing and its rebuttal to the collective-guilt charge (institutional responsibility, not personal guilt) were not squarely neutralized. CON's arguments about centralization risk and partisan purges were plausible and repeated with some rhetorical force, but they never fully answered PRO's core comparative claim about correctability. PRO's closing crystallized this asymmetry cleanly, while CON's closing largely repeated earlier framing without new engagement. This makes PRO's path to victory clearer and less contested by the end.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question ("If local boards can already soften slavery... why is their discretion less political than a transparent floor?") and its follow-up in Rebuttal 2 reframing the mandate as a floor rather than a monopoly was the most decisive exchange; CON's answer (local discretion is more "contestable") did not fully rebut why diffuse, invisible omission is safer than a visible, scrutinizable national standard.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A won by sustaining a clear comparative: a visible, contestable national floor prevents the silent erasure of atrocities that already occurs under decentralized local discretion. Side B never overcame the concrete problem of local boards sanitizing history, instead relying on abstract fears of partisan weaponization that Side A showed were more accountable than invisible omission. A’s pressure questions also exposed that B could offer no alternative mandatory safeguard, leaving CON without a mechanism to ensure the teaching ever happens.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 (A4), where it answered B’s pressure questions by showing a transparent floor makes distortion visible and contestable, whereas CON’s decentralized model leaves the least accountable actors free to sanitize history quietly, crystallizing the decisive comparative.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Qwen 3.8 Max (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won the centralization clash. CON's core that mandates hand the untrustworthy state a weapon was turned by PRO into visibility vs invisibility: local discretion already politicizes history through silent omission (slavery, internment, repression) while a transparent national minimum floor is contestable, grounded in scholarship, and does not impose collective guilt but democratic responsibility. CON conceded abuses must be studied but offered no enforceable alternative, and its Germany story was matched by PRO's more accurate account of mandates cementing consensus.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Rebuttal 2 A1/A2 where PRO answers B's pressure about who defines the floor: conceding the state selects but distinguishing a contestable minimum from a monopoly, and showing omission is harder to correct than a visible mandate.
