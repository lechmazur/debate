### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (CON); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO)): PRO wins a close but real margin. The decisive clash is on enforcement. PRO's pressure question (B3.1) exposed the structural hole in CON's position: if you endorse internationally negotiated labor and climate floors, what enforceable consequence do you permit other than market exclusion? CON's A1 answer named WTO disputes, ILO mechanisms, and aid conditionality, but PRO had already anticipated and answered this in B4.4: "a floor without consequences is a slogan." CON's multilateral alternatives are real but practically weak, and PRO correctly noted that endorsing the norms without accepting market access as the consequence is effectively defending voluntary diplomacy. The motion is about enforceability, and PRO had the cleaner argument on why the EU's market access is the right enforcement instrument. CON made strong subsidiary arguments — the toy analogy distinction (product-level vs. process-level regulation) is analytically sound, the China-imposing-its-standards parallel is genuinely sharp, and the small-producer fixed-cost argument was consistent throughout. CON also effectively used PRO's own concession (harm can relocate) to attack the causal link between the mechanism and the stated beneficiaries. PRO's response to the relocation point ("CON's world keeps Europe financing the abuse") is adequate but doesn't fully close the gap. However, CON's closing crystallized the wrong beneficiary argument clearly but never fully answered the enforcement trap: if ILO and WTO floors matter enough to invoke, losing EU market access is precisely the consequence that makes them real. PRO's logic is internally consistent and returns cleanly to the motion's core question. Both sides were disciplined on grounding with no fake citations; CON's China parallel and "design cannot invert structure" gave CON originality credit, while PRO's rhetorical framing of the EU market as a tool that can either reward or penalize abuse was consistently effective.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Q1 pressure question (B3.1) — asking what enforceable consequence CON would allow if a trading partner violates the very floors CON endorses — and the follow-through in B4.4 ("a floor without consequences is a slogan") was the debate's most decisive exchange. CON's answer named multilateral mechanisms but never demonstrated they provide timely, effective consequences against a specific violating exporter, which allowed PRO to close the debate on its strongest ground.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side A won by effectively proving that without market consequences, labor and climate standards remain optional. A also successfully neutralized B's 'extraterritoriality' arguments by demonstrating that trade already relies on process-based proofs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1 distinction between product and process standards was excellent, but A's R2 counter citing existing process-based trade rules (like rules of origin and natural resource legality) decisively neutralized it.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (PRO); B = MiniMax-M3 (CON)): Side B wins because it sustained a sharper mechanism-level critique: mirror clauses are process-based extraterritorial rules that impose regressive fixed costs on small producers regardless of design, while the conceded risk of leakage means the stated climate and labor beneficiaries may see no improvement. Side A repeatedly appealed to "better design" without showing how fixed costs stop favoring multinationals, and three clipped speeches left key rebuttals incomplete. Side B also crystallized cleanly in the closing that the tool mismatches the goal, whereas Side A's enforcement logic never resolved the structural harms it would inflict on the least powerful producers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's opening of Rebuttal 1 (B2.1-B2.4), distinguishing product-level border tests from process-based extraterritorial regulation, paired with the sustained architectural critique that compliance fixed costs inherently concentrate supply chains into large multinationals and that "the pen stays in Brussels."

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON); B = MiniMax-M3 (PRO)): PRO (Side B) wins a clear but competitive debate. Three clipped speeches for CON are a real execution cost, leaving arguments unresolved at critical moments. On substance, PRO's core framing — that the EU's internal standards already produce external leakage, making mirror clauses a matter of consistency rather than coercion — was established early and never fully neutralized. GSP+ as documented evidence of preference-based conditionality working with developing economies anchored PRO's case in observable precedent. PRO's pressure questions were sharper: Q2 forced CON to articulate a principled line between legitimate (GSP+) and illegitimate (mirror clauses) conditionality. CON's A2 did offer a real distinction (preference vs. access), but PRO's Rebuttal 2 A1 then accepted that distinction and defended the access-restricting nature of mirror clauses on the same logic as food safety — a confident rather than evasive answer. CON's strongest live argument — the two-choice enforcement dilemma (paperwork compliance vs. contested blocking) — was genuinely damaging and PRO's CBAM/ILO benchmarks only partially answered it, since national treaty ratification is not supply-chain-level verification. However, CON's "targeted tools match the harm" alternative was repeatedly underspecified, and PRO's "tools without a destination" crystallization effectively framed CON's alternative as a description of the status quo rather than a policy. PRO's consistent structure, clean A1/A2 labeling, and the memorable framing of the binary gave it an overall edge in guiding a third-party reader to its conclusion.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's Pressure Question Q2 ("where exactly is your principled line between leverage that works and leverage that works too well?") and the subsequent exchange. CON articulated a real distinction (preference vs. access conditionality), but PRO's Rebuttal 2 A1 then openly accepted that mirror clauses do restrict access and defended this as precisely the point — the same logic as product safety. This exchange clarified the actual stakes rather than blurring them, and PRO came out ahead because accepting the exclusionary framing while grounding it in familiar EU trade logic was more persuasive than CON's attempt to hold the distinction as a decisive objection.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by successfully dismantling Side A's core analogies and precedents, culminating in a sharp catch in Rebuttal 2 where B exposed A for watering down the motion's "market access conditionality" into a mere tariff preference.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 2 was decisive, perfectly catching A's attempt to frame the punishment as simply paying the "standard external tariff," which B correctly identified as sliding from the proposition's access conditionality to a mere preference scheme.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = GPT-5.5 (high) (CON)): Side A won by a razor-thin margin by establishing that the status quo structurally rewards offshoring and showing that EU conditionality already works (GSP+). While Side B landed sharp challenges on enforcement dilemmas and the distinction between preferences and market access, A successfully absorbed B’s alternative as “tools without a destination” and maintained cleaner execution by staying within word limits while B was clipped in three separate speeches. The decisive clash came down to whether broad mirror clauses provide a necessary framework or an unenforceable veto; A’s framing of reciprocity as correcting existing leakage and as consistent with EU border practice proved marginally more resilient after the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s Rebuttal 2 reframing of mirror clauses as the “destination” that gives targeted tools direction, directly answering Side B’s pressure questions on coercion and comparability while blunting the “regulatory veto” charge by anchoring standards in existing ILO and Paris Agreement frameworks.
