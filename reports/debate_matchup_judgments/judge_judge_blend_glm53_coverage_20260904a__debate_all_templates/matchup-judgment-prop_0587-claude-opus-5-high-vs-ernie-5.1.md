  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's combination of A4.4 (cementing the marginal framing) and A4.6 (exploiting Side B's admission that domestic policy cannot touch foreign blast furnaces) structurally won the debate.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by establishing that the motion demands a marginal comparison between domestic policy alone and domestic policy plus a border adjustment. Side A’s honest concession that domestic tools have zero reach into foreign furnaces ceded the decisive structural ceiling: American policy alone is capped at roughly one-eighth of global emissions and leaks carbon offshore, whereas even a partially operational border mechanism reaches the other seven-eighths through announcement-driven operational changes. Side A’s timing objection was competitive but never fully neutralized Side B’s “cheap seat” response or explained how a domestic-only regime avoids leakage. Over five years, a partial reach beyond the border beats a perfect reach that stops at it.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s Pressure Question 1 forcing Side A to admit that no domestic subsidy or regulation can touch a Chinese blast furnace, which Side B then weaponized across the remaining speeches to lock in the irreversible ceiling argument.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON)): Side A won by successfully framing the debate as a marginal comparison and exposing the hard geographic ceiling of Side B's domestic-only approach. Side A's distinction between capital stock timelines (subsidies) and operational shifts (border adjustment) provided a highly plausible mechanism for impact within the five-year window. Side B's concession that domestic policy cannot touch foreign emissions, combined with a failure to effectively counter the leakage argument, left Side A with a decisive comparative advantage.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Turn 7 response to the pressure questions, particularly dismantling Side B's "tonnage" test by pointing out that operational shifts happen on quarterly clocks without needing final legal implementation, and forcing Side B into a fatal concession about domestic policy's geographic limits.

### Judge Notes

- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B won by decisively leveraging the 5-year timeframe, arguing that without a domestic carbon price, a U.S. CBAM would spend the window mired in administrative setup and trade disputes. Side B also effectively demonstrated how subsidies act globally through cost-curve reductions, while neutralizing Side A's leakage arguments by exposing the reality of resource shuffling.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2 dismantled the idea of using a domestic methane leak fee as an anchor for a border adjustment, highlighting the impracticality of applying it to imports like Turkish rebar.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON won by enforcing the five-year timeline. PRO's border-adjustment case relied on a speculative causal chain—foreign governments enacting carbon pricing to retain revenue—but could not name a single major exporter that would do so within the window, nor show where abatement would occur in years one through five. CON demonstrated that subsidized deployment has already demonstrably bent global cost curves (e.g., solar adoption in Vietnam), giving it a concrete, locatable emissions record. PRO's "signal" and leverage arguments remained diffuse and institutionally immature for the short horizon, and their anchoring to domestic regulatory costs did not overcome CON's objection that the U.S. lacks a customs-ready carbon price, making early implementation a dispute-heavy paperwork exercise rather than a ton-cutting mechanism.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's second rebuttal response to PRO's pressure questions, turning the demand for specificity into a referendum on the five-year window: "PRO has asked you to compare abatement they cannot locate in time or place against abatement that is shipping in containers right now," capped by the line "An imperfect border charge doesn't produce imperfect abatement. It produces paperwork."
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B decisively won by exposing the fatal implementation and timeline flaws in Side A's case. Side A's attempt to anchor a border adjustment to unrelated domestic regulations (like a methane fee) was dismantled by Side B as a guaranteed WTO dispute rather than a workable carbon price. Furthermore, Side B's pressure questions forced Side A to admit its foreign leverage mechanism was speculative and lacked concrete tonnage within the five-year window, while Side B's cost-curve argument for subsidies was historically grounded and immediate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where they dismantled Side A's answers to the pressure questions, specifically pointing out that a methane penalty cannot be levied on imported steel and that Side A's examples of foreign carbon pricing were either pre-existing or merely in the drafting phase.
