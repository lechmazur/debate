### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): This was a competitive debate with genuine live clashes surviving into the closing. Side B (PRO) held a narrow but real advantage on two counts. First, the first-timer/accomplice coverage gap was the strongest individual argument in the debate: incapacitation only activates after arrest, conviction, and repeat-offender labeling, leaving first-time shooters, accomplices, and substitutes untouched. A's "network anchor" response in A4.1 was clever—removing the crew's primary shooter degrades network capacity—but it conceded the basic structural limitation and doesn't hold for every dispute, retaliatory spiral, or opportunistic assault. B pressed this gap every round and A never fully closed it. Second, B's severity-vs.-certainty point about impulsive, retaliatory, and intoxicated violence held throughout; A's deterrence argument was partially clipped in the opening and never developed fully. A's strongest live routes—durability (permanent removal vs. episodic suppression) and accountability self-policing vs. external court orders—were real and partially sustained. The streetlight counter-analogy in A4.5 was the sharpest individual rebuttal move in the debate. But B's answer that violence concentrates in predictable windows (so targeted presence doesn't require round-the-clock everywhere coverage) was adequate, and the "concrete geographic and body-camera controls over a narrow tactic" response to the self-policing objection held plausibly. B also had better execution: A suffered two significant clips (opening and closing), which cost crystallization at crucial moments, while B's single clip in Rebuttal 2 came after the required A1/A2 answers were complete. Overall B maintained more unanswered pressure and cleaner format execution through the full exchange.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 in the pressure round ("how does longer sentences reduce the next retaliatory shooting by a first-time or not-yet-convicted participant more than officers at the exact hot spot tonight?") was the sharpest moment in the debate. A's A1 response in Rebuttal 2—that removing the repeat-offender network anchor permanently degrades the entire network's capacity—was a genuine and clever reframe, but it conceded that incapacitation is structurally narrow (it only works when the repeat offender really is the catalyst) and did not account for the full range of retaliatory, spontaneous, or substitute-driven violence B had flagged throughout. That exchange left B's coverage argument substantially live through the closing.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO)): Side B won by successfully pressing the "bottleneck" argument—that sentencing only applies to a narrow subset of offenders after a long chain of systemic successes, missing first-timers and accomplices. Side A attempted to reframe these systemic hurdles as "external checks" for accountability but never resolved the resulting loss of scale. Side B also effectively mitigated the displacement critique by demonstrating why micro-locations generate unique risks.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 1 and subsequent pressure on the justice system pipeline, pointing out that Side A's policy only activates after arrest, clearance, and conviction, making it too late and narrow to match immediate prevention.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (PRO); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON)): Side A won by establishing a cleaner comparative frame that endured through the full exchange: upstream prevention at the trigger point versus back-end punishment for a narrow, already-convicted subset. A successfully showed that increasing sentence lengths is delayed and misses first-time shooters, accomplices, and imminent retaliation, while accountable hot spot policing reaches the actual places and hours where violence clusters. A also blunted B's main offensive argument by explaining that persistent clustering implies places are not interchangeable, undermining the claim that suppression merely displaces harm. B landed real pressure on durability and resource intensity, but never fully solved the scope problem—many violent acts are not committed by people already convicted of qualifying repeat offenses. Execution costs also hurt B more: B's opening and closing were clipped, leaving arguments incomplete, whereas A maintained cohesive framing throughout.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's answer to displacement in Rebuttal 1 (A2.2-A2.3) and Pressure Question 2, which exposed that if offenders could shift costlessly, violence would not persistently cluster on the same micro-locations. This neutralized B's primary attack on hot spot policing and preserved A's live route to victory.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Both sides ran disciplined cases, but CON (Side A) holds the narrower edge across the full exchange. CON's incapacitation argument—that longer sentences prevent crimes with certainty, citywide, 24/7, for the highest-risk offenders—was coherent from start to finish and was never cleanly neutralized. PRO's clearance-rate/pipeline reframe ("you're optimizing the tail end of a broken pipeline") was the most original and damaging move of the debate, and CON's counter—that "feeding the pipeline is not beating the policy at the end of it"—was adequate but left PRO's asymmetry argument partially live. The exchange that ultimately tips the balance is CON's exploitation of PRO's own Rebuttal 2 concession: PRO acknowledged in [B4.1] that domestic violence "follows different dynamics and responds to different interventions entirely," and CON immediately pressed in [A4.6] that the motion covers violent crime overall, not just street-corner gun crime. This gave CON a genuine comparative advantage in scope that PRO never recovered from. Additionally, PRO suffered real execution costs from multiple clipped speeches (Opening, Rebuttal 2, Closing), leaving their pipeline and baseline-probability arguments incomplete at critical moments, while CON's closing crystallized the comparison cleanly and completely. PRO's theoretical grounding on certainty-vs.-severity and geographic concentration was solid, and the "my strategy feeds theirs but not vice versa" asymmetry was conceptually strong, keeping this competitive. But CON's broader footprint across crime types and the completeness of its speeches across the round gave it the cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The most decisive rebuttal exchange was PRO's "broken pipeline" argument in B4.5–B4.6—contending that longer sentences optimize the tail end of a system where most violent crimes never result in arrest—versus CON's response in A4.3–A4.6 that (a) incapacitation targets the highest-risk identified offenders even if few, and (b) PRO's own concession that domestic violence and similar offenses follow different dynamics exposed the place-based strategy's limited reach across all violent crime types. CON's exploitation of that concession was the sharpest, most damaging single move in the debate.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side A wins by effectively neutralizing Side B's "broken pipeline" argument and decisively capitalizing on the scope of the motion. When B argued that hot-spot policing "feeds" the prosecution pipeline, A successfully reframed this as an admission that policing is merely an input, while incapacitation provides the durable reduction. Furthermore, A seized on B's concession that domestic and mobile violence fall outside hot-spot dynamics, correctly pointing out that the motion concerns "violent crime overall."
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal (A4) was decisive, simultaneously flipping B's "pipeline" argument by labeling hot-spot policing as just an input, and capitalizing on B's domestic violence concession to win the scope of the motion.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO); B = GPT-5.4 (high) (CON)): B won by sustaining a cleaner comparative frame: incapacitation removes repeat offenders everywhere, all day, for years, while hot spot effects are temporary and geographically bounded. B's second rebuttal recast A's pipeline argument as making hot spots merely an input into incapacitation rather than a superior substitute, and B's closing exploited A's narrowing to street-corner gun violence to show a place-based strategy cannot clearly outperform a person-based one for violent crime overall. A raised a strong clearance-rate challenge but never fully answered the durability and scope problem, and suffered repeated word-limit clips that left key speeches unfinished.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's second rebuttal ([B4.1]-[B4.7]), where B reframed A's "pipeline" argument as evidence that hot spot policing is at best an input into incapacitation, not a superior substitute, and showed that A's narrowing to street-corner violence conceded the broader "violent crime overall" frame.
