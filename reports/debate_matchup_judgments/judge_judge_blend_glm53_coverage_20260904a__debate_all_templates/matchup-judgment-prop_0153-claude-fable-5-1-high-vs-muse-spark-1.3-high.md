### Judge Notes

- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side B won a competitive debate by distinguishing financial incentive from coercion and showing how regulation can separate enforceable payment and parental responsibility from unenforceable medical control. Side A exposed a serious autonomy and commodification tension, but relied too heavily on the unsupported premise that needing or valuing payment makes consent coercive. It also never fully resolved B's comparison that altruistic scarcity drives demand underground or abroad. A's clipped rebuttal and questionable claim that all cited jurisdictions retreated to bans further weakened its case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's response to the coercion and kidney analogies was decisive: it identified stable participants, distinguished acute desperation from ordinary financial motivation, and contrasted compensated ongoing labor with permanent organ removal. Side A's reply that meaningful pay is itself pressure never established why an attractive offer necessarily negates consent.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON); B = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO)): Side A won by systematically dismantling the distinction Side B tried to draw between bodily autonomy and enforceable parentage contracts. Side A's principled defense of using adoption law over pre-birth orders neutralized Side B's primary offensive push regarding custody disputes.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the kidney analogy in Rebuttal 2 was exceptional, noting that under a pre-birth order, what permanently leaves the body is a child, and the surrogate's 'ongoing consent' functionally ends at conception.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Muse Spark 1.3 (high) (PRO); B = Claude Fable 5.1 (high) (CON)): CON demonstrated that compensation and strong protections are structurally in tension: payment recruits economically pressured women, and enforceable contracts require pre-birth parentage orders that override the surrogate’s ongoing autonomy. PRO’s comparisons to ordinary wage labor and organ-sale distinctions were blunted by CON’s observation that under a pre-birth order the surrogate permanently loses the child by court order because money changed hands, and by the historical failure of “regulated” markets abroad. While PRO effectively showed bans drive demand underground, CON successfully framed the motion as self-defeating: real protections either remove the price or the enforceability, leaving compensated surrogacy as coercion and exploitation with a licence. Side B’s edge is narrow but real.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON’s second rebuttal and closing turned PRO’s “ongoing consent” and kidney analogies by showing that a pre-birth parentage order means the surrogate’s consent ends at conception and what leaves her body permanently is a child, not tissue—making the transaction a state-enforced surrender of a person rather than compensated labor.
