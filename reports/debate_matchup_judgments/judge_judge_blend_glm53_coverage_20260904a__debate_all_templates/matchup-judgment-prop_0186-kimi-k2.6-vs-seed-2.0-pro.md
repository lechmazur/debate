### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): Side A built a cleaner, more durable framework: recognition is a bundle of legal privileges (sovereign immunity, UN votes, lending access) distinct from the practical engagement (envoys, UN agencies, humanitarian corridors) that keeps aid and negotiation running. This distinction let A absorb B's most powerful weapon—the 2023 Afghanistan earthquake example—by separating counter-terrorism/banking sanctions from recognition status itself. B never fully closed that causal gap; it reasserted that banking/insurance blocks were "the direct, predictable outcome of withholding recognition" without showing why sanctions-driven de-risking requires formal non-recognition specifically, leaving that claim asserted rather than demonstrated, and arguably crossing into unverifiable precision (exact timelines, causal claims about insurers and banks) that the rules flag as brittle. B's hypocrisy-codification argument and "no regime has ever democratized from non-recognition" point were live and interesting, but A's answers (written standards as focal points for challenge; recognition as insulation rather than isolation) adequately blunted them without being fully conceded. Both sides answered pressure questions in format, but A's Rebuttal 1 being clipped for exceeding the word limit is a real, if modest, execution cost. On balance, A's argument architecture was tighter and better defended across rounds, giving it a clear but not overwhelming edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's move distinguishing recognition-as-legal-status from practical humanitarian/diplomatic engagement, first laid out in Rebuttal 1 and sharpened in the pressure-question exchange over the 2023 Afghanistan earthquake, was the most decisive clash: B never adequately showed why banking/insurance/customs blockages required non-recognition specifically rather than separate sanctions regimes, and A's reframing (recognition insulates rather than isolates) survived the exchange as the more coherent causal story.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Kimi K2.6 (PRO); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON)): A won by consistently separating practical engagement from the legal and material privileges of recognition. B raised serious concerns about humanitarian coordination and politicized standards, but never firmly established that recognition itself was indispensable rather than sanctions relief, waivers, or humanitarian channels. B's unusually precise Afghanistan anecdote and sweeping historical claim were insufficiently grounded, while A's benchmark answer, though incomplete, preserved a credible accountability benefit. A's clipped rebuttal and thin proof that conditionality causes reform kept the debate competitive.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's response that non-recognition does not prohibit negotiations, UN relief operations, or special humanitarian arrangements most directly weakened B's central engagement-versus-abandonment framing. B largely asserted that formal recognition caused banking and logistics access without adequately separating recognition from sanctions and regulatory restrictions.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (CON); B = Kimi K2.6 (PRO)): Side A won by successfully proving that withholding formal recognition inherently paralyzes the mundane legal and financial infrastructure required to sustain human life, defeating Side B's attempt to separate diplomatic endorsement from practical engagement.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): Both sides built coherent cases, but CON's mechanism was more consistently defended across rounds. CON's most damaging move was turning PRO's own Taliban example inside out: PRO cited 2021 recognition as proof recognition enables abuse, but CON pointed out that the years of non-recognition before 2021 produced no reform at all, only famine and stranded civil society — a point PRO never directly answered in Rebuttal 2 or Closing. CON also exposed a real concession: when pressed on the great-power-veto objection, PRO's answer ("it will be applied inconsistently... still better") effectively conceded the core critique rather than refuting it, leaving CON's "veto with better branding" framing largely intact. PRO's rhetoric was vivid and its "leverage" framing was compelling, but some supporting claims ($7 billion figure, "banned within three months," "systematically diverting aid") read as unverifiable brittle precision, while CON's comparatively grounded NK/protecting-power point was more measured. CON's answers to pressure questions, while partly reframing the burden rather than directly naming a case, were argued honestly as contesting the premise, whereas PRO's answers conceded more ground than they recovered. Overall CON made the clash easier to resolve and left fewer live threads for PRO.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's reuse of the Taliban case in Rebuttal 1 and Rebuttal 2 — showing that pre-2021 non-recognition produced years of stagnation and famine rather than reform — decisively undercut PRO's central example and was never squarely answered by PRO afterward.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO); B = Kimi K2.6 (CON)): B won by exposing PRO’s missing causal bridge: regimes valuing recognition does not establish that withholding it produces reform, especially when standards are selectively enforced. A usefully noted that negotiations can occur without recognition, but relied heavily on inaccurate Taliban claims, overstated recognition’s effects, and never demonstrated that conditionality improves rights enough to outweigh politicization and civilian costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B4.1 most decisively answered A’s pressure: craving recognition proves that sovereign privileges are valuable, not that dictators will trade repression for them. That distinction directly weakened A’s central leverage claim.
- **Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview** (A = Kimi K2.6 (CON); B = ByteDance Seed2.0 Pro (PRO)): Side A won by cleanly severing Side B's core mechanism: the idea that withholding recognition creates actionable leverage. Side A successfully argued that while dictators crave recognition for its sovereign perks, withholding it does not actually force them to liberalize (using the 90s Taliban as proof). Side A also effectively countered Side B's analogies, pointing out that inconsistent application of recognition doesn't just let some bad actors off the hook, but atomizes entire societies and leaves them without formal diplomatic interfaces.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4.1 brilliantly dismantled Side B's main pressure point by distinguishing between a regime's desire for sovereign privileges and the false assumption that withholding those privileges actually forces liberalization.
