### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Both sides built plausible, non-fabricated cost/mechanism arguments and executed the Q&A format properly. CON (A) opened strong on scale-cost problems with tutoring labor and on the "30 hours vs 90 minutes" dosage framing, and pressed real concerns about turnover and administrative bloat. PRO (B) built an equally strong counter-frame around construction/pension costs as the "true" denominator problem, and consistently reframed tutoring as the direct "active ingredient." The decisive moment came in the endgame: CON's Rebuttal 2 conceded that smaller classes work by enabling "small-group rotations," and PRO's closing sharply exploited that concession, arguing this proves targeted small-group instruction (i.e., tutoring) is the actual engine of gains, making the extra infrastructure spending unnecessary. CON's closing never directly answered this specific turn, instead recapping earlier points about treadmill costs and 30-hour coverage. This left PRO's most damaging rebuttal unresolved at the end of the debate, giving PRO the cleaner final position on the core "per dollar" comparison.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's closing turn on CON's own "small-group rotations" concession (A4.2), arguing that if targeted small-group instruction is what actually drives gains in smaller classes, that undercuts CON's case for funding costly infrastructure instead of buying that instruction directly via tutoring — a point CON's final closing did not rebut.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): Side B won the decisive per-dollar comparison. It plausibly showed that reducing every class requires costly additional teachers, benefits, and often space, while tutoring concentrates cheaper labor on students with the largest remediable gaps. Side A compellingly identified turnover, administration, and broader classroom benefits, but never substantiated its crucial claim that tutoring overhead could match or exceed the structural cost of major class-size reductions. Its comparison of ninety tutoring minutes with thirty classroom hours also treated all improved classroom time as equally productive without resolving B’s point that tutoring purchases much more concentrated attention.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange was its answer to the thirty-hours argument: smaller classes spread resources across all students for incremental benefits, whereas tutoring targets intensive instruction at students with the highest potential gains. Side A’s small-group-rotation answer showed a real class-size mechanism, but B’s closing effectively noted that tutoring purchases that active ingredient directly without first funding an entire additional classroom structure.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (PRO)): CON won by proving that smaller classes deliver broader, more durable gains across all thirty weekly hours in both subjects while tutoring remains narrow, reactive triage with severe hidden operational costs. CON's small-group rotation answer to PRO's pressure question proved decisive, showing that intensive individualized attention is achievable within a smaller classroom without abandoning other students, thereby absorbing PRO's core comparative advantage into CON's model.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's answer to PRO's Q2 about intensive remediation in a 15:1 classroom using small-group rotations (A4.2), which neutralized PRO's claim that undivided attention requires a parallel tutoring system.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently with plausible mechanisms and no fabricated evidence, but B built a cleaner, more consistent frame (numerator/denominator, targeting vs. dilution) that it sustained across every round. B's pressure question exposed the deepest structural weakness in CON's case—that an 18-student class remains whole-group instruction, so the causal mechanism for gains is unclear—and A's answer (invoking "dynamic small-group rotations organically") was an unsupported, late-introduced assertion that partially conceded the point rather than resolving it. B also successfully turned A's own labor-shortage argument back on class-size reduction (55% payroll expansion vs. targeted paraprofessional staffing), a genuinely damaging rebuttal that A never fully neutralized. A landed some good points of its own (hidden logistics/chaos costs of running tutoring in "classroom corners," the "total gains" reframing, teacher burnout as a root-cause fix), and its closing chaos critique on tutoring logistics went unanswered by B's closing, but this was a less structurally decisive point than the whole-group mechanism problem B exposed. On balance B provided the clearer, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's pressure question (Q2) asking how whole-group instruction in an 18-student class outperforms 1:2 diagnostic tutoring, and A's weak, newly-asserted "dynamic small-group rotations" answer, was the most decisive exchange—it exposed an unresolved gap in CON's core mechanism claim that persisted through closing.
- **GPT-5.6 Sol (high)** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B won the per-dollar comparison more clearly. It paired tutoring’s intensive diagnostic mechanism with a persuasive denominator argument: targeted use of a broader labor pool is substantially cheaper than universally expanding certified staffing and facilities. Side A identified real scaling frictions, but never showed that recruitment, supervision, and scheduling costs would approach the permanent expense of its own proposed reduction from 28 to 18 students. Its claims that smaller classes would cure shortages and create superior aggregate gains were plausible but insufficiently established.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B’s strongest exchange turned Side A’s scaling objection back on class-size reduction: tutoring needs fewer workers drawn from a broader pool because it is targeted, whereas Side A’s model requires a 55 percent staffing expansion plus additional rooms. Side A’s response emphasized burnout and whole-week benefits but did not resolve that cost asymmetry.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Gemini 3.1 Pro Preview (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): Side B (PRO) won because it established a clearer comparative framework—numerator (instructional impact) versus denominator (cost)—and defended it through rebuttals by showing that class-size reduction’s universal staffing demands are at least as scaling-challenged as tutoring, while tutoring’s targeted design and use of existing infrastructure keep costs lower. CON’s aggregate-gains and teacher-burnout arguments were plausible but never fully offset the staggering fiscal and logistical burden of shrinking every K–8 classroom, leaving PRO’s per-dollar math more intact.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: The scaling-turn exchange where PRO rebutted CON’s staffing critique by demonstrating that class-size reduction requires an even larger, harder-to-fill expansion of fully certified teachers and facilities, thereby neutralizing CON’s central scaling objection and reinforcing tutoring’s relative feasibility.
