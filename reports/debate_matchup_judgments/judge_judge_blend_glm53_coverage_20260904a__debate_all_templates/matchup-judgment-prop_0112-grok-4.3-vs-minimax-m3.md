### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A dominated on the motion's actual question: whether tying basic medical access to work status is preferable. From the first rebuttal onward, A correctly identified that B was defending employer insurance as a funding mechanism rather than defending the access tie itself. A's sharp contrast—deductibles are legal gates that exclude; queues are triage that still treat—materially damaged B's rationing argument and was never answered. A's pressure question about illness-forced job loss exposing the "reciprocity" frame as inverting the purpose of healthcare was devastating, and B's answer (continuation options, job mobility as remedy) was visibly circular: someone who cannot work due to illness cannot change employers to access different coverage. B's later speeches shrank in word count and retreated to the same "levers" argument without closing the gap on the core burden. A's closing crystallized the decisive clash cleanly: "getting sicker makes you less covered" is a feature of the employer model, not a flaw to be worked around. B never found a route to defend the motion's actual premise rather than the funding architecture behind employer insurance.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's second rebuttal distinguishing deductibles from queues: a deductible is a legal gate the system will not open without payment, while a queue is a clinical triage sequence that still treats the patient. This reframe made the rationing-equivalence claim B had relied on since the opening untenable, and B offered no direct response to it in the closing.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by maintaining relentless focus on the motion: whether tying basic medical access to work status is preferable. A showed that employer coverage inherently withdraws at the moment of peak need—when illness prevents work—while single-payer decouples coverage from employment. B never squarely answered this structural cruelty, offering weak remedies like job mobility and continuation coverage that ignored A's job-lock and gap arguments. B repeatedly drifted to funding, innovation, and political allocation without demonstrating why those concerns require conditioning access on work status. A delivered sharper rebuttals, won the pressure-question exchange decisively, and crystallized the clash memorably in the closing.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's second rebuttal distinguishing deductibles as exclusionary legal gates from queues as clinical triage, demonstrating that a laid-off worker with appendicitis is shut out entirely under employer coverage rather than merely queued—directly answering B's pressure questions while exposing the fatal asymmetry in access denial.
- **Qwen 3.6 Max Preview** (A = MiniMax-M3 (PRO); B = Grok 4.3 (CON)): Side A won by relentlessly anchoring the debate to the motion's exact condition: tying basic medical access to work status. A demonstrated that employer-based coverage structurally fails precisely when medical need peaks, turning B's "reciprocity" framing into a liability. A's rebuttals sharply contrasted financial exclusion (deductibles, network drops, job loss) with clinical triage (queues), directly answering B's pressure while exposing the inadequacy of B's proposed remedies (job mobility, continuation coverage) for vulnerable patients. Side B relied on idealized market mechanisms and abstract funding arguments that never squarely answered why work-tied access is preferable for basic care. A's closing crystallized this decisive clash cleanly and memorably.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2 directly answered B's pressure questions by contrasting the deductible (a financial gate that excludes the unemployed) with the queue (a clinical triage system that still treats), then pivoted to show how B's defenses of reciprocity and market levers actually highlight the employer model's fragility during peak medical need.
