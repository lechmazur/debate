### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): PRO wins a clear but genuinely competitive debate on the strength of execution consistency and better adaptive framing across rounds. CON had the sharpest individual argument in the debate—the Title IX trigger distinction between third-party NIL payments (not institutional payroll) and CBA-mandated institutional compensation, producing a genuine catch-22 (equalize pay = gut revenue split; don't equalize = void)—but PRO never fully resolved it. However, three of CON's five speeches were clipped for exceeding word limits, a real and repeated execution failure that left multiple arguments truncated mid-thought. PRO's consistent full-length speeches allowed it to maintain a coherent through-line: NIL already produces massive gender gaps with no correction mechanism, Title IX is a structural advantage for bargaining rather than a threat, and CON's objections ultimately amount to defending the absence of athlete voice. PRO's recharacterization of Title IX as a legal floor that voids discriminatory clauses was incomplete (it doesn't explain how the gap gets filled with compliant terms), but CON's closing rebuttal attacking this was itself clipped before completing the argument. PRO's pressure questions were slightly weaker than CON's; CON's Q1/Q2 on the bargaining unit composition were more structurally incisive. But PRO answered both with the school-wide unit argument and the Title IX void mechanism more coherently than CON managed in its partial responses. Overall, CON had the better isolated arguments; PRO ran the better debate.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A1 in Rebuttal 2 [A4.1–A4.2]: the distinction between third-party NIL deals (not institutional payroll, not a Title IX trigger) and CBA-mandated university payments to employees (direct institutional compensation that triggers Title IX in a categorically different way). This was the sharpest, most grounded specific argument in the debate. PRO's response—Title IX voids discriminatory clauses, providing a structural floor—was not wrong as far as it went, but it never identified the mechanism by which a voided clause becomes a compliant replacement, leaving CON's catch-22 alive.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): Side A won by making the decisive clash about mechanism rather than aspiration. Side B established the intuitive case that collective bargaining creates enforceable athlete voice and floors, but too often answered structural objections by saying a CBA “could” include equity and non-revenue protections. Side A’s Title IX distinction between third-party NIL and institutional payroll, plus the school-wide/sport-specific bargaining dilemma, directly attacked the feasibility of Side B’s core model. Side B’s legal-floor answer helped but did not fully solve A’s point that voiding discriminatory terms does not itself produce a workable revenue-sharing contract. A’s clipping costs keep the margin moderate, but its rebuttals were more damaging.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A’s response to the Title IX question was the most decisive exchange: NIL was framed as third-party market activity, while CBA revenue sharing was framed as institutional employee compensation, forcing PRO to explain a compliance mechanism it never clearly supplied.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (CON); B = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (PRO)): CON wins by exposing that PRO's collective bargaining alternative rests on idealized assumptions that collapse under legal and incentive scrutiny. CON distinguishes third-party NIL money from institutional employee compensation, demonstrating that only the latter triggers Title IX in the way PRO's revenue-sharing model would. CON further shows that voiding discriminatory clauses under Title IX creates legal gaps, not compliant replacements, and that a bargaining unit dominated by revenue-sport athletes lacks the incentive to ratify the gender-equitable cross-subsidies PRO promises. PRO never provides a concrete mechanism to thread this needle, repeatedly falling back on "could be structured to comply." Meanwhile, CON defends the current system as legally distinct, institutionally accountable, and adaptively evolving—an imperfect but coherent alternative to a mechanism that requires bargaining units to act against their own financial interests.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's Rebuttal 2 (Turn 8), which directly answered PRO's pressure questions by distinguishing institutional payroll from third-party NIL under Title IX, reframed athletic-director accountability against bargaining-unit self-interest, and crystallized the decisive flaw: PRO's CBA depends on terms that no rational, revenue-sport-dominated bargaining unit would actually ratify.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 4.6 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side B wins by a clear but competitive margin. The decisive clash was the "floor" argument: B established that NIL provides no protection for athletes who attract zero sponsors, and A never cleanly answered it. A's response — that NIL already delivers five-figure deals to swimmers — only works for athletes who can attract sponsors, which B correctly identified as a minority. B's double-standard framing in B4.5–B4.6 (A measures collective bargaining against perfection while measuring NIL against feasibility) landed well and went essentially unanswered. B also neutralized A's strongest weapon — "certain money today" — by pointing out that Alston and pending antitrust suits mean the status quo is already collapsing, making the choice not between stable NIL and collective bargaining but between organized labor and institutional chaos. A ran a competent, multi-pronged case with genuine force on the 20% domination concern and structural tax/Title IX barriers, and the "sledgehammer vs. scalpel" framing was memorable. But the 20% domination argument was asserted more than proven — B's response (all large unions manage divergent interests) was not neutralized, only repeated past. The tax/Title IX objections are real but were weakened by B's observation that the current framework is already legally untenable. Two clipped speeches were an execution cost for B, but the core arguments were delivered before the clip points. The residual live argument — NIL leaves most athletes unprotected while the system crumbles — was B's cleaner path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's B4.5–B4.6 double-standard charge was the most decisive exchange: B caught A applying different evidentiary standards to each system — demanding a guaranteed union floor while offering athletes no floor at all under NIL — and A's rebuttal in A4.5 simply restated the "immediate money" line without engaging the asymmetry. This framing crystallized the core imbalance in A's case and was never genuinely answered.
- **GPT-5.5 (high)** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): B won by making the central comparison clearer: NIL is not a floor, it is a fragmented market where many athletes can earn nothing, while collective bargaining can create enforceable minimums, health protections, and a formal voice. A’s best lines on tax, Title IX, union capture, and immediate NIL money were relevant, but they were overclaimed and weakly grounded, especially the claim that occasional five-figure NIL deals amount to a floor for most athletes. B also effectively reframed A’s case as demanding perfection from collective bargaining while accepting chaos from the status quo.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s answer to the pressure questions was decisive: a union floor need not erase inequality to be valuable, because even imperfect minimum compensation and protections are categorically different from NIL’s absence of any guaranteed protection.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Baidu Ernie 5.1 (CON); B = Xiaomi MiMo V2.5 Pro (PRO)): Side B won by showing that the core issue is the lack of guaranteed protections for athlete-workers, while exposing Side A's conflation of volatile NIL market opportunity with a genuine compensation floor. Side A's warnings about tax and Title IX were plausible, but Side B effectively framed them as manageable transition costs rather than permanent barriers, and demonstrated that defending an ad hoc system with no enforceable minimums offers no affirmative vision for the majority of athletes who lack individual market leverage. Side B's sharper pressure question answers and clearer weighing of guaranteed protections against speculative harms provided the decisive edge.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's second rebuttal distinguishing an enforceable union floor from volatile individual NIL earnings, and turning the burden of legislative uncertainty back onto Side A's defense of a legally unsustainable status quo.
