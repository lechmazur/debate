### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON built the more disciplined case by distinguishing "ending exclusionary zoning" (which it conceded) from the motion's stronger demand for active government funding and a deliberate revival campaign. Its "occupancy, not inventory" reframe was the debate's most decisive move: existing parks, libraries, and sidewalks already exist and often sit underused, so PRO needed to explain why subsidized new footage would generate use where old footage does not, and PRO's answer (rooms fail only because they are "marooned in sprawl") was asserted rather than demonstrated and never squarely closed the gap CON kept re-opening. PRO's rhetorically strong "paywalling the public sphere" and "government created the isolation, government must fix it" framing was compelling but rested on a contradiction charge (CON citing public parks somehow concedes the whole funding mandate) that CON cleanly defused by separating ordinary upkeep from an active build-out campaign. CON's pressure questions exposed this gap most sharply, and PRO's answers, while formatted correctly, did not resolve it, leaving CON's narrower, better-defended claim as the stronger path to a verdict.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's A4.5/A4.6 response to PRO's occupancy defense ("if public rooms already sit unused, extra subsidized footage... does not purchase the practice of showing up") directly neutralized PRO's proximity/vitality mechanism and reasserted the unanswered inventory-vs-occupancy distinction that PRO never fully closed.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): CON maintained a live route that PRO never neutralized: existing parks, libraries, sidewalks and cheap commercial seats already provide free contact, so gathering is not paywalled and inventory is not the bottleneck. PRO's paywall and asphalt-void story was answered by B's occupancy-scarce, phones-emptied-rooms argument and by the distinction that defending a modest existing commons does not concede a funded revival campaign. PRO's threshold for "declined enough" stayed conceptual and its answer that empty rooms are badly placed did not prove extra funded rooms will create belonging.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B R1/R2 occupancy vs inventory – phones emptied rooms that still exist and parks sit unused – which directly undercut PRO's claim that more funded square footage will cure loneliness.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (CON)): Side A won by successfully trapping Side B on the issue of public parks and libraries. Side B relied heavily on these existing government-funded spaces to prove that community gathering wasn't entirely "paywalled" by commercial venues. Side A brilliantly pointed out that this concession proved the necessity of government-funded social infrastructure, exposing a core contradiction in Side B's opposition to active public investment. Side A also provided a stronger causal story linking car-centric zoning and single-use mandates to the decline of incidental community contact, effectively neutralizing Side B's "occupancy, not inventory" argument by explaining that current inventory is marooned in inaccessible sprawl.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's Rebuttal 2, where they directly answered the pressure questions and weaponized Side B's reliance on public parks to prove that non-commercial civic life requires deliberate government carving of a commons.

### Judge Notes

- **Claude Sonnet 5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON); B = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO)): Both sides argued competently, but B built the more durable structural point: current zoning (parking minimums, single-use codes) is already an active government choice, so "staying out" is not neutrality, and reversing it is exactly the "zoning for revival" the motion contemplates. This undercut A's central rebuttal move, which relied on a sharp deregulation/active-zoning distinction that the motion's text doesn't clearly demand. A's strongest weapon, ghost storefronts, was answered directly (vacancy stems from mandated retail without walkable density, not from the mixed-use/parking reforms B actually proposes), and A never fully rebutted that counter beyond repeating "circular." A also spent significant rebuttal energy accusing B of "retreating" to subsidizing only libraries/parks, but B's opening already scoped funding to public leftover spaces and zoning (not subsidy) as the tool for private venues—so much of A's "gotcha" framing was closer to a strawman than a genuine trap, and B called this out effectively (B4.2). B's closing crystallized the clash sharply: CON's position ultimately amounts to accepting the decline as an acceptable cultural shrug, a framing A never squarely disowned. A's case remained forceful on the dangers of picking winners and cultural causation, but did less comparative work to neutralize B's core "the state already shaped the market" argument, leaving B with the cleaner, more resolved path to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Rebuttal 1 move (B2.3, extended in B4.5) reframing existing zoning as an already-active government product—so removing it counts as the motion's "zoning for revival," not mere neutrality—which A never fully dislodged despite repeated assertions that this was "just deregulation."
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO maintained the more coherent live route: single-use zoning and parking minimums are already active state choices, so reversing them is the zoning revival the motion names, and funding public rooms like libraries/parks solves the lingering-is-unprofitable market failure without picking private cafes. CON's core distinction between deregulation and active revival collapsed and its ghost-storefront point was turned into evidence of low-density mandates, not absent demand. CON never answered where else cheap lingering could return if not in public rooms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: PRO's R1/R2 answer on ghost storefronts — empty mandated retail diagnoses shops without walkable neighbors/parking deserts, not futility of revival, and public-room funding avoids winner-picking.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Grok 4.6 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.8 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A built a much stronger, more cohesive case by grounding their arguments in plausible urban planning mechanisms like parking minimums, single-use zoning, and high-turnover retail requirements. Side A effectively neutralized Side B's "ghost storefront" and "picking winners" attacks by clarifying that funding targets public spaces (libraries, parks) while zoning targets enabling mixed-use environments, rather than mandating ground-floor retail in parking deserts. Side B relied too heavily on a weak semantic distinction between "deregulation" and "active zoning" and repetitive slogans about manufacturing friendship, failing to adequately address Side A's core point that the status quo is itself a product of active government zoning.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's response to the "ghost storefront" argument, explaining that empty ground-floor retail is the result of mandating shops without walkable density or housing, effectively dismantled Side B's central empirical attack.
