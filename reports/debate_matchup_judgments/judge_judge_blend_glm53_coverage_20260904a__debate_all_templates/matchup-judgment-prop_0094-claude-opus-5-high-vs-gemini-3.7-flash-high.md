### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO)): B won a clear but competitive debate by owning the motion as sequencing and leverage rather than prohibition. The decisive path was enforcement asymmetry: once fleets are politically indispensable, permit revocation and throttling become hard, so conditions must bind while fleets are still small. B turned A's own permit/geo-compliance language into support, named concrete "clearer rules" thresholds under pressure, and kept labor as an unmet burden. A had a real safety/baseline and digital-leverage countercase, but never fully neutralized the wasting-asset and network-blockage comparison, and early silence plus weaker labor answers cost route clarity.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's leverage/concession turn: A's conditional permits with geo-compliance are the instrument, and that leverage only works pre-saturation (B2.4–B2.5, B4.5–B4.6, B5.2–B5.3).
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): PRO won the sequencing clash. A showed a capped, geofenced fleet still observes the city and that controlled density yields usable data, defusing the Catch-22. The decisive move was turning CON's own "conditional permit with geo-compliance" into the motion's instrument and adding concrete, checkable thresholds plus a ratchet, answering the indefinite-veto charge. CON never answered the wasting-asset enforcement asymmetry — that a city can throttle 300 but politically cannot switch off 3,000 — and relied on a per-mile safety metric that A showed misses network blockage risk.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's R2 A1/A2 — reframing capped vs uncapped mileage, naming three rule artifacts as a ratchet schedule, and arguing leverage decays with fleet size so CON's permit is actually a geofence+cap whose bite must come early.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (PRO); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (CON)): Side A wins by building a much stronger and more realistic case around political leverage and systemic risk. Side A's core argument—that regulatory leverage is a "wasting asset" that decays as a fleet becomes essential to voters—brilliantly neutralized Side B's claim that digital enforcement tools (like software throttling) work just as well at scale. Side A also provided a concrete, three-part threshold for "clearer rules," effectively dismantling Side B's "indefinite paralysis" offense. Furthermore, Side A's distinction between per-mile crash rates and systemic network blockage (e.g., stalled AVs blocking emergency vehicles in zero-slack systems) was a sharp, original rebuttal to Side B's human-error safety argument. Side B argued well but relied too heavily on theoretical programmatic enforcement and struggled to overcome Side A's grounded political realities.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side A's closing crystallization of the leverage asymmetry: pointing out that a city can easily throttle a 300-vehicle fleet administratively, but cannot politically switch off a 3,000-vehicle fleet that 100,000 residents rely on, proving that leverage decays with scale.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON); B = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO)): CON won by making the decisive clash decideable: the motion’s sunset is unworkable, and PRO’s own named lift condition (curb pricing, data sharing, override protocols) is conduct regulation that can bind the first permitted vehicle. That made numeric caps look redundant plus harmful via incumbency and lost substitutability. PRO kept a live systemic-risk and leverage story, but never explained what the rationing layer still adds once those rules exist, and never recovered from answering the trigger question in CON’s preferred terms.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A4 turning B4.1’s named sunset ordinance into proof both sides want the same rulebook and the cap is an inferior add-on.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): CON won the sunset and instruments clash. PRO eventually defined the lift condition as data-sharing, curb pricing, and override standards - CON's own conduct regulation - and never showed what a numeric cap adds once those bind vehicle one. CON's incumbency/substitutability turn with the Cruise 2023 permit pull answered leverage and exposed that caps entrench the first entrant, while PRO's volume-is-behavior and blast-radius claims were met by correlated-fault and pricing-enforcement responses that were never neutralized.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's R1+R2 sequence pressing the no-sunset trigger and then turning PRO's own ordinance answer into a concession that the cap is redundant once conduct rules exist, combined with the Cruise revocation example to flip leverage.
- **Qwen 3.7 Max** (A = Gemini 3.7 Flash (high) (PRO); B = Claude Opus 5 (high) (CON)): Side B wins by decisively dismantling the mechanism of Side A's proposed cap. B effectively trapped A on the "sunset condition" of the policy, forcing A to admit that the cap would be lifted by specific conduct regulations (data sharing, curb pricing, override protocols). B then brilliantly pointed out that these conduct regulations can and should be applied to the very first vehicle via an operating permit, rendering the numeric cap a redundant rationing scheme that merely entrenches incumbents and destroys the market substitutability cities need to enforce permits (as seen in the Cruise example). While A made strong points about physical geometry and proactive containment, B's reframing of the clash from "chaos vs. caps" to "condition the license vs. count the cars" was rhetorically sharp and argumentatively fatal to A's case.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: Side B's Rebuttal 2, where B uses A's own pressure question answer to prove that A's sunset condition is just B's conduct policy, and uses the California/Cruise example to show how cities maintain leverage through substitutability rather than caps.
