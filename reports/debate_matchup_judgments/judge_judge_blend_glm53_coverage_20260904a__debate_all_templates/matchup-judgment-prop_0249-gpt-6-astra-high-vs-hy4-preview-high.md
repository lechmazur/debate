### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON)): A won the decisive live clash: even if devices are patchable at sale, someone must still be obliged to write and ship fixes after a late vulnerability. B’s answer (owner or third-party volunteers) was permission, not provision, and left ordinary households stranded. A’s pressure Q1 forced that concession and closing crystallized capability ≠ accountability. B landed real costs on thin-margin devices, offshore enforcement limits, and stronger ex-ante design leverage, keeping the debate competitive, but never neutralized A’s core externalities-plus-abandonment route or showed that design alone substitutes for continuing responsibility on reachable makers.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A’s abandoned-camera pressure (Q1) and later “permission not provision / capability is not accountability” weighing against B’s owner-or-third-party reply.
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): CON wins by demonstrating that the mandate's fines are uncollectable against the worst offenders (offshore and bankrupt manufacturers) while burdening reachable incumbents with costs that shrink the low-end market. PRO concedes key enforcement limitations and that updates cannot stop default-credential botnets. CON's alternative—ex ante mandatory patchability enforceable at sale and the border—binds all sellers equally without the market distortions, making it the cleaner path to actual security.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's exploitation of PRO's concession that fines are largely uncollectable against bankrupt or foreign sellers, paired with the argument that this taxes reachable incumbents while letting the least-secure offshore devices flood in, decisively reframed the enforcement comparison.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (CON); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (PRO)): PRO maintained that abandonment creates information-asymmetry and externality costs that patchability alone cannot fix; CON's alternative relies on owners or third parties to supply fixes after support ends, which is permission not provision, while conceding fines are still effective for reachable firms. Price and category-setting uncertainty was acknowledged but weighed against hidden compromise costs.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B's Q1 about a post-support vulnerability and A's answer that owners/third parties can fix it, crystallized in B's closing as permission vs provision, exposed that CON leaves security work unassigned.

### Judge Notes

- **Grok 4.5 (high)** (A = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO); B = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON)): B won by keeping the decisive clash on proportionality and the word “all.” A’s market-failure/externality case and family-camera opening were strong, and tiering plus escrow/bonds meaningfully answered abandonment and cost concerns. But B consistently showed that if regulators can risk-tier support periods, they can also decide whether a continuing support duty is warranted at all; targeted secure-design, disclosure, and remediation duties need not wait for injury and need not impose a standing maintenance obligation on every low-exposure device. A’s refusal to allow exemption even when costs exceed total expected security benefit (including third-party harm), plus the residual fixed-cost problem of very short windows, left the universal floor harder to justify than B’s alternative. The exchange narrowed cleanly in B’s favor.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: B’s Rebuttal 2 / Closing on A’s no-exemption answer: costs can still exceed total expected benefit (including hospital/botnet harms), and tiering cannot dodge that without either undercutting protection or quietly abandoning “all.”
- **Kimi K2.6** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): CON won by showing that PRO could not justify applying the mandate to every device regardless of cost-benefit, even when third-party harms were counted, and that PRO's own risk-tiering logic actually supported targeted rather than universal coverage. PRO's market-failure narrative remained live, but CON's proportionality critique proved the cleaner route to victory.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: CON's rebuttal that if regulators are competent enough to calibrate risk-tiered support periods, they are competent enough to determine whether a category needs mandatory lifecycle support at all—an asymmetry PRO never resolved.
- **Muse Spark 1.1 (high)** (A = GPT-6 Astra (high) (CON); B = Tencent Hy4 Preview (high) (PRO)): Side A (CON) won the proportionality clash. PRO established externalities and timing, but conceded under pressure there is no exemption even when [A3.1] support costs exceed total expected benefit including third-party harm, and answered with "very short window" that leaves fixed costs intact. CON showed if regulators can risk-tier duration they can also decide whether a lifecycle duty is warranted at all, and that targeted duties (secure defaults, patchability where justified, recall/disable) address the foothold risk without funding ghost firms via extra-regime tools like bonds and importer liability beyond the motion's fines.
  Most decisive rebuttal noted: A's Rebuttal 2 and Closing on Q1 – showing B's [B4.1] refusal to exempt even after including hospital/botnet externalities, combined with fixed-cost-of-patch-capability, turned PRO's universal floor into an unjustified mandate.
